# The Warring States Period

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Sun Tzu, Shang Yang, Mencius, Zhuangzi, Han Feizi, Su Qin, Zhang Yi, Bai Qi, Qin Shi Huang (as unifier)

## Summary

The Warring States Period (475–221 BCE) was an era of intense interstate conflict in pre-imperial China when the nominal authority of the Zhou Dynasty fragmented into competing kingdoms that waged relentless warfare over territorial control and supremacy. Emerging from the Spring and Autumn Period (722–475 BCE), the era saw seven major states—Qin, Chu, Zhao, Wei, Han, Yan, and Qi—and numerous smaller polities vie for dominance through military innovation, diplomatic alliances, and economic development. This period witnessed revolutionary advances in military technology and organization (mass infantry armies replacing aristocratic chariots, the crossbow, iron weapons, siege engineering), the composition of foundational military texts including *The Art of War* attributed to Sun Tzu, the flourishing of the "Hundred Schools of Thought" (including Confucianism, Daoism, Legalism, and Mohism), the expansion of agricultural productivity through iron tools and irrigation, and the gradual emergence of Qin as the ascendant power through superior administrative reform under the Legalist minister Shang Yang. The period culminated in Qin Shi Huang's conquest of all rival states between 230 and 221 BCE and the establishment of China's first centralized empire, ending more than two and a half centuries of fragmentation.

## Political Structure and the Seven States

### The Fragmentation of Zhou

The Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BCE) had ruled through a feudal network of enfeoffed lords bound by ritual kinship and obligation to the Son of Heaven. By the Spring and Autumn Period, these lords had become effectively autonomous rulers whose military and economic power dwarfed that of the Zhou court. The Warring States era began when the three successor states of Jin—Han, Wei, and Zhao—were formally recognized by the Zhou king in 403 BCE, legitimizing the complete collapse of the old feudal order into a system of sovereign competing states.

### The Seven Powers and Their Characteristics

- **Qin** (capital: Yueyang, later Xianyang): Western state occupying the Wei River valley of modern Shaanxi; transformed by Shang Yang's reforms into a militarized agrarian state; emerged as the dominant power by the 3rd century BCE.
- **Chu** (capital: Ying): Largest state by territory, occupying the Yangtze River valley and beyond; culturally distinct from northern states; produced the *Chu Ci* (Songs of Chu) poetic tradition and the poet-minister Qu Yuan.
- **Qi** (capital: Linzi): Prosperous eastern state on the Shandong Peninsula; home to the Jixia Academy, the first state-sponsored center of learning, which hosted scholars from across China; major commercial and intellectual center.
- **Zhao** (capital: Handan): Northern state descended from Jin; fielded powerful cavalry forces after King Wuling's reforms; Qin's primary military rival in the 3rd century BCE.
- **Wei** (capital: Daliang): Central state, early military and intellectual leader; home of the Mohist school and early Legalist thinkers; declined after the Battle of Maling (341 BCE).
- **Han** (capital: Xinzheng): Smallest of the seven powers; positioned in the central plains; first to be conquered by Qin (230 BCE).
- **Yan** (capital: Ji, modern Beijing): Northeastern state; site of famous assassination attempts including Jing Ke's near-successful attempt on the Qin king's life in 227 BCE.

## Military Innovation

### The Transformation of Warfare

The Spring and Autumn Period had featured primarily aristocratic warfare: small armies of charioteers commanded by noble officers observing ritual courtesies of war. The Warring States era saw a fundamental transformation into mass infantry warfare reflecting the mobilization of entire state populations. Armies of tens of thousands, eventually hundreds of thousands, replaced the thousands of the earlier era.

Key military innovations included:
- **Crossbow**: The mechanical crossbow, deployed in mass formations, democratized ranged warfare and gave infantry forces the ability to defeat cavalry and chariot charges.
- **Iron weapons and armor**: Iron, which was more widely available than bronze, enabled mass arming of peasant infantry.
- **Siege engineering**: The demands of capturing walled cities drove development of siege machinery including scaling ladders, battering rams, earth ramps, and early trebuchets.
- **Cavalry**: King Wuling of Zhao (r. 325–299 BCE) controversially adopted the fighting style of northern steppe peoples—including trousers, which allowed horseback riding—creating effective cavalry units that other states soon imitated.

### The Battle of Changping (260 BCE)

The Battle of Changping stands as one of the bloodiest engagements in ancient history. Qin forces under the general Bai Qi encircled a large Zhao army near the town of Changping (modern Gaoping, Shanxi) after Zhao replaced its experienced commander Lian Po with the untested Zhao Kuo, who fell into Qin's tactical trap. Chinese historical records, primarily Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian*, state that approximately 400,000 Zhao soldiers surrendered and were subsequently buried alive—a figure many historians consider exaggerated but reflective of the enormous scale of the massacre. Whether precisely accurate or not, Changping effectively crippled Zhao as a military power, clearing the way for Qin's final campaigns.

### *The Art of War* (*Sunzi Bingfa*)

Attributed to Sun Tzu (Sun Wu), a military theorist of the late Spring and Autumn / early Warring States transition, *The Art of War* is the most influential military text in Chinese history. Its thirteen chapters articulate principles of strategy: knowing oneself and the enemy, the use of deception, adapting to circumstances, the priority of winning without fighting. Whether Sun Tzu was a single historical figure or a composite of later editors remains debated by scholars. The text was definitively compiled and annotated during the Warring States period and has since been applied to military strategy, business, and diplomacy globally.

## Intellectual Flourishing: The Hundred Schools of Thought

The competitive state system created an unprecedented intellectual marketplace. Rulers competed to attract talented advisors, and scholars traveled between states selling their philosophical and administrative expertise. This produced the "Hundred Schools of Thought" (*Zhuzi Baijia*)—a flowering of philosophical traditions unmatched in Chinese history.

### Major Schools

**Confucianism** (*Rujia*): Derived from Confucius (551–479 BCE), elaborated by Mencius (372–289 BCE) and Xunzi (310–235 BCE). Confucius emphasized moral self-cultivation, ritual propriety, and the rectification of social relationships; Mencius argued for the innate goodness of human nature and a ruler's obligation to the people's welfare; Xunzi countered that human nature required moral education and social ritual to correct its natural tendencies. Though largely ignored by rulers seeking immediate power, Confucianism became the dominant official ideology of the Han Dynasty and all subsequent empires.

**Daoism** (*Daojia*): Associated with the legendary Laozi (traditional dates 6th century BCE) and the historical Zhuangzi (c. 369–286 BCE). Emphasized living in harmony with the natural order (*dao*), skepticism of social convention, and the limits of human knowledge and control. Zhuangzi's writings—elaborate allegories and paradoxes—represent one of the most sophisticated literary and philosophical achievements of the period.

**Legalism** (*Fajia*): The philosophy that proved most immediately successful for state-building. Legalists including Shang Yang (c. 390–338 BCE), Shen Buhai, and Han Feizi (c. 280–233 BCE) argued that states should be governed through universal law (*fa*), bureaucratic position (*shi*), and administrative technique (*shu*), rather than aristocratic virtue or kinship. Rewards and punishments, systematically applied, would align individual interests with state power. Shang Yang's reforms in Qin embodied these principles and transformed the state's military-agricultural capacity.

**Mohism** (*Mojia*): Founded by Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE), Mohism promoted "universal love" (*jian'ai*)—concern for all people equally rather than for family or state—and opposed aggressive warfare. Mohists were also notable engineers and developed sophisticated theories of logic, optics, and mechanics. The school was influential during the Warring States but declined under the Han.

### The Jixia Academy

The state of Qi established at its capital Linzi the Jixia Academy (稷下學宮, c. 318–279 BCE)—the world's first state-sponsored institution of higher learning. The Qi court funded scholars of all philosophical persuasions to reside, teach, and debate. At its height, Jixia housed hundreds of scholars, including Mencius, Xunzi, and figures from Daoist and Legalist traditions. It embodied the cosmopolitan intellectual culture of the Warring States period and prefigured later Chinese institutions of imperial scholarship.

## Shang Yang's Reforms in Qin

The transformation of Qin from a western frontier state into the era's dominant power is inseparable from the administrative reforms of Shang Yang, who served as chief minister to Duke Xiao of Qin from 359 BCE until his death in 338 BCE.

Shang Yang's reforms included:
- **Abolition of hereditary aristocracy**: Government positions were made meritocratic—advancement through military achievement rather than birth.
- **Universal law**: A written legal code applied equally to nobles and commoners, reducing the arbitrary power of the aristocracy.
- **Household registration and mutual surveillance**: Population registration organized into five-family units (*wu*) and ten-family units (*shi*), each collectively responsible for reporting crimes; a form of social control that maximized state knowledge of the population.
- **Standardized weights and measures**: Creating a unified economic standard across Qin.
- **Agricultural colonization**: Incentivizing migration to open frontier lands with promises of land grants, expanding Qin's agricultural base.
- **Military merit system**: Soldiers were rewarded with noble ranks, land, and servants based on the number of enemy heads taken; this created powerful material incentives for military aggression.

These reforms were deeply resented by the traditional aristocracy but proved extraordinarily effective. Shang Yang was executed after Duke Xiao's death—torn apart by chariots by his enemies—but his institutional reforms endured and powered Qin's subsequent conquests.

## Diplomacy and Strategic Theory

### Vertical and Horizontal Alliances

The multistate system generated sophisticated diplomatic theory. The dominant strategic debate concerned whether weaker states should ally against Qin (*Hezhong*, "Vertical Alliance") or make separate accommodations with Qin (*Lianheng*, "Horizontal Alliance"). The diplomat Su Qin (c. 333–284 BCE) famously persuaded six states to form a defensive coalition against Qin, reportedly serving as chancellor of six states simultaneously. His rival Zhang Yi served Qin and systematically dismantled coalitions through separate negotiations and bribes. These strategies anticipated later theories of alliance management and balance-of-power politics.

## Agricultural and Economic Development

Iron agricultural tools, including plows, spread through Chinese agriculture during the Warring States period, enabling cultivation of harder soils and supporting population growth. Major irrigation projects—including the Dujiangyan system built by the Qin engineer Li Bing around 256 BCE in Sichuan—expanded arable land and demonstrated states' willingness to invest in long-term agricultural infrastructure. Population estimates for the period are uncertain, but China's aggregate population may have grown from roughly 20 million at the start of the period to 40–50 million by the time of unification.

## The Qin Conquests (230–221 BCE)

Qin Shi Huang (born Ying Zheng, r. 247–221 BCE as king of Qin; 221–210 BCE as first emperor) directed six campaigns over nine years that systematically eliminated the remaining six states:

1. **Han (230 BCE)**: Smallest state; rapidly conquered.
2. **Zhao (228 BCE)**: Weakened by Changping; its capital Handan fell after siege.
3. **Wei (225 BCE)**: Qin forces diverted the Yellow River to flood the capital Daliang.
4. **Chu (223 BCE)**: Largest state required a 600,000-man army under general Wang Jian.
5. **Yan (222 BCE)**: Its famous assassin Jing Ke had nearly killed Qin Shi Huang in 227 BCE; Yan fell and its crown prince was captured.
6. **Qi (221 BCE)**: Surrendered without significant battle.

The king of Qin proclaimed himself *Qin Shi Huang* ("First August Emperor of Qin") in 221 BCE, creating a new title combining *Huang* (august/emperor, associated with mythical divine rulers) and *Di* (emperor, previously reserved for legendary sage-kings).

## Significance

The Warring States Period established the intellectual and practical foundations of imperial China. The military texts, philosophical systems, and administrative innovations developed during this era became canonical for all subsequent Chinese dynasties. The period demonstrates how competition—far from stifling cultural achievement—generated remarkable philosophical diversity and technological advancement. The eventual unification by Qin established the model of centralized bureaucratic rule, standardized governance, and imperial ideology that persisted for over two millennia.

The Warring States period is thus the crucible in which Chinese civilization forged its defining characteristics: competitive federalism gave way to centralized empire, and competing philosophies yielded to an official synthesis dominated by Confucianism and Legalism. The era remains central to understanding Chinese political theory, military strategy, and the historical narrative of unification that Chinese statecraft has repeatedly invoked.
