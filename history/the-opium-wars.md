## Summary

The Opium Wars (1840–1842 and 1856–1860) were two armed conflicts between the British Empire and Qing-dynasty China, fought over Britain's right to trade opium in Chinese markets. The wars resulted in decisive British military victories and forced China to open its ports to Western trade, accept foreign diplomatic representation, and cede Hong Kong to British control. These conflicts marked the beginning of China's "Century of Humiliation" and fundamentally reshaped Chinese society, economy, and politics under escalating Western imperialism.

---

## Prelude: Britain's Failed Diplomacy (1793–1830s)

Britain's frustrations with Chinese trade restrictions long predated the Opium Wars. In 1793, King George III dispatched Lord George Macartney as his first ambassador to the Qing court, carrying elaborate gifts of British manufacture and proposals to open new ports, establish embassies, and negotiate commercial treaties. Emperor Qianlong famously dismissed every request. His imperial edict to George III declared: "We possess all things. I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures." The Qing court received Macartney as a vassal's tribute mission, not as equal diplomatic partners—a reflection of the "celestial empire" (tianxia) worldview, in which China was civilization's center and all foreign rulers were theoretically subordinate to the Son of Heaven.

A follow-up mission under Lord Amherst in 1816 failed even to gain an audience with the Jiaqing Emperor. These diplomatic rebuffs hardened British merchants' conviction that only commercial pressure—and eventually force—could open China's markets. They turned increasingly to opium, grown in British India, as the instrument to balance the trade deficit.

## The First Opium War (1840–1842)

### Background: The Trade Imbalance

By the late 18th century, British merchants had become frustrated with China's restrictive Canton System, which confined all foreign trade to the southern port of Guangzhou (Canton) and limited commerce to a handful of licensed merchant guilds known as the Cohong. British demand for Chinese silk, porcelain, and tea far exceeded Chinese demand for British manufactured goods, creating a significant trade deficit. To balance this imbalance, British merchants (particularly those of the British East India Company) began smuggling Indian opium into China, where addiction had become widespread among the population.

The Qing government, alarmed by the social and economic consequences of opium addiction and the outflow of silver bullion used to pay for opium, initially attempted to ban the drug entirely. However, the profitable opium trade proved irresistible to foreign merchants, and official corruption allowed smuggling to continue unabated.

### The Crisis and Conflict

In 1839, the Qing court appointed Lin Zexu, a respected scholar-official, as imperial commissioner to suppress the opium trade in Guangzhou. Before acting, Lin composed a remarkable open letter addressed to Queen Victoria, appealing to her moral authority: "We note that you England is a foreign country... your subjects do not dare to smoke opium... yet you lead people to import this poison into China." The letter was never formally presented, but it stands as a landmark document in Chinese diplomatic history—an appeal to moral universalism that anticipated later Chinese arguments about sovereignty and the hypocrisy of Western imperialism.

Lin Zexu then moved against the opium stocks. Between June 3 and June 25, 1839, his teams publicly destroyed 20,283 chests of opium—approximately 1,188 tons—at Humen (Bocca Tigris) near the mouth of the Pearl River. Workers mixed the drug with salt and lime in specially dug trenches, then flushed the mixture into the sea over 23 days. Lin reportedly apologized to the sea deity for the pollution. This dramatic destruction earned him an enduring place in Chinese historical memory as a defender of national sovereignty. However, the action provoked Britain's response. Citing the seizure of British property and the unfair restrictions on trade, Britain dispatched a naval expeditionary force to China.

The First Opium War (1840–1842) saw the British Royal Navy easily overwhelm Qing naval forces. Chinese junks and coastal fortifications proved no match for British steam-powered gunboats and superior artillery. The British captured key ports along the Chinese coast, including Shanghai, Ningbo, and Xiamen, and threatened to attack Nanjing, the grand old capital. The Qing court, unable to defend itself militarily, sued for peace.

### The Treaty of Nanking (1842)

The Treaty of Nanking (1842), China's first unequal treaty with a Western power, imposed humiliating terms on the Qing government:
- **Hong Kong ceded**: Britain gained permanent control of Hong Kong Island.
- **Five treaty ports opened**: Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai were opened to British trade, ending the Canton System's monopoly.
- **Most Favored Nation status**: Britain secured the right to trade on the most favorable terms granted to any other nation.
- **Indemnity imposed**: China was forced to pay 21 million silver dollars in compensation for the cost of the war and the destroyed opium.
- **Extraterritoriality established**: British nationals in China could not be tried in Chinese courts—a privilege that stripped the Qing of judicial sovereignty.

The treaty marked the beginning of the "unequal treaty system" that would define China's relationship with the Western powers for the next century.

### Supplementary Treaties with Other Powers (1844)

Britain's military success immediately attracted other Western powers seeking identical advantages without fighting their own wars. On July 3, 1844, the United States concluded the Treaty of Wangxia, negotiated by American envoy Caleb Cushing and Qing official Qi Ying. Though the United States had not participated in the war, it secured Most Favored Nation status, extraterritoriality, and access to the five treaty ports on equal terms with Britain. France signed the Treaty of Whampoa on October 24, 1844, adding a provision specifically permitting Christian missionary activity throughout China.

These treaties activated a structural "ratchet mechanism": the Most Favored Nation clauses meant that any concession granted to one power automatically extended to all treaty powers. China could not contain the spread of privileges—every new treaty tightened its constraints and drew additional states into the system.

## The Second Opium War (Arrow War, 1856–1860)

### Renewed Tensions

Despite the first war's outcome, Western powers remained dissatisfied with the limited access to Chinese markets. France, seeking its own commercial advantages, joined Britain in pressing the Qing government for further concessions. The Chinese, meanwhile, resented the foreign presence and restrictions on their sovereignty, and some officials resolved to resist further Western encroachment.

In 1856, a minor incident provided the spark: Chinese officials seized the Arrow, a merchant ship sailing under British colors, and arrested its crew. Though the ship was registered in Hong Kong and its British connection was questionable, Britain used the incident as a pretext for renewed military action, allied with France.

### The Second War (1856–1860)

The Second Opium War (also known as the Arrow War or Second Anglo-Chinese War) initially focused on Guangzhou. Governor-General Ye Mingchen commanded the Chinese defense with determined stubbornness, posting proclamations that British soldiers were barbarians and refusing to negotiate. On December 28–29, 1857, British and French forces stormed and captured Canton. Ye Mingchen was seized and deported to Calcutta, India, where he died in April 1859—reportedly refusing to eat, having declared he would "neither fight, nor make peace, nor die, nor surrender."

In May 1858, allied forces attacked the Taku Forts guarding the approaches to Tianjin. The forts fell and the allies advanced to Tianjin, compelling the Qing court to sign the Treaty of Tientsin (June 1858), which promised eleven new treaty ports, permanent diplomatic residence in Beijing, and navigation rights on the Yangtze. When allied envoys returned in 1859 to ratify the treaty, however, the Qing had reinforced the Taku Forts under the Mongol general Sengge Rinchen. The British assault was repulsed with significant losses in a rare Chinese military success—delaying finalization of the treaty by a full year.

The Second Battle of the Taku Forts in August 1860 succeeded, and allied forces marched toward Beijing. Emperor Xianfeng fled the capital to his summer hunting retreat at Chengde, leaving his brother Prince Gong to negotiate.

The British and French then marched north, capturing the treaty ports and advancing toward Beijing itself. In 1860, during final desperate negotiations, Chinese officials agreed to allow a joint British-French military expedition to enter Beijing. Before final negotiations, Chinese officials seized a British diplomatic party—including Times correspondent Thomas Bowlby—under a flag of truce; several were tortured and killed. Lord Elgin (British commander and son of the man who had taken the Elgin Marbles from Athens) resolved on dramatic collective punishment. On October 18–19, 1860, British forces burned the Summer Palace (Yuan Ming Yuan), the magnificent Qing imperial garden complex built over 150 years on the outskirts of Beijing. The complex encompassed over 200 buildings, thousands of artworks, sculptures, and manuscripts accumulated over more than a century. The blaze burned for three days, visible as a pall of black smoke over the capital. Lord Elgin justified the destruction as retaliation for the murder of diplomatic envoys; Chinese historians have since characterized it as an act of cultural vandalism. The site today stands as deliberately preserved ruins—a symbol of the "Century of Humiliation" maintained to educate future generations.

### The Convention of Peking (1860)

The Treaty of Tientsin (1858) and the subsequent Convention of Peking (1860) imposed even more severe terms on China:
- **Eleven more treaty ports opened**: Further opening of Chinese markets to Western trade.
- **Kowloon ceded**: Britain gained the peninsula opposite Hong Kong Island.
- **Indemnities doubled**: The indemnity owed under the Treaty of Tientsin was raised to 8 million taels of silver each for Britain and France.
- **Legalization of opium**: Most humiliating, the Qing government was forced to legalize the opium trade in China, effectively legitimizing what they had tried to suppress.
- **Religious freedom imposed**: Christian missionary activity was legally permitted throughout China.
- **Yangzi River opened**: Foreign vessels gained the right to navigate China's most important interior waterway.

## Consequences and Legacy

### Immediate Impacts

The Opium Wars exposed the Qing military's technological and organizational inferiority to Western powers and shattered the myth of Chinese cultural and military supremacy that had long dominated Chinese thought. The vast indemnities imposed on the Qing government strained imperial finances and contributed to fiscal crisis. The legalization and proliferation of opium addiction devastated Chinese society, with millions becoming addicted and the drug trade enriching foreign merchants.

More fundamentally, the wars established the "unequal treaty system" that would define Chinese sovereignty in the coming decades. Extraterritoriality, Most Favored Nation status, and forced economic opening became templates for subsequent treaties with other Western powers and Japan. China transformed from an independent state regulating its own trade into a semi-colonial entity subordinate to Western economic and political interests.

### The "Century of Humiliation"

Chinese historians and political leaders have come to view the Opium Wars as the beginning of the "Century of Humiliation" (bainian guochi)—the period from 1840 to 1940 when China was progressively colonized, partitioned, and impoverished by foreign powers. The wars created a deep wound in Chinese national consciousness and became emblematic of the perceived arrogance and injustice of Western imperialism.

### Catalyst for Internal Reform and Rebellion

The military defeats prompted serious Chinese thinkers to examine why China had fallen behind the West. Some officials, such as Wei Yuan and Feng Guifen, advocated for "learning from the barbarians"—adopting Western technology and organizational methods while maintaining Chinese cultural and political integrity. Wei Yuan's *Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms* (Haiguo Tuzhi, 1844) catalogued Western military technology and urged China to "use barbarian methods to control barbarians"—one of the first serious Chinese attempts to grapple with Western superiority on Western terms.

The wars also contributed to domestic instability. The indemnities and trade disruptions weakened rural economies, and the social chaos of the opium epidemic created conditions for peasant rebellions, most notably the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), which would kill tens of millions of people and further erode Qing authority.

### The Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895)

Building on the intellectual groundwork laid by Wei Yuan and others, a generation of senior Qing officials launched the "Self-Strengthening Movement" (ziqiang yundong) in the 1860s. Led by officials Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, and Zhang Zhidong, the movement established modern arsenals (such as the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai, founded 1865), shipyards, telegraph networks, and military academies. Hundreds of Chinese students were sent to study engineering and military science in the United States and Europe.

Li Hongzhang built China's first modern naval force, the Beiyang Fleet, equipping it with German-built warships. Zhang Zhidong articulated the movement's philosophy in the phrase "Chinese learning as the essence, Western learning for practical use" (zhongti xiyong)—selective adoption of Western technique without surrendering the core of Chinese civilization.

Despite genuine achievements, the Self-Strengthening Movement ultimately failed to reverse China's military decline. Its limitations were catastrophically exposed in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895), when the Beiyang Fleet was destroyed in a single naval battle at the Yalu River (September 17, 1894). The humiliation of defeat by Japan—a country that China had long considered culturally subordinate—shocked the Qing elite and catalyzed a more radical generation of reformers who would eventually overthrow the dynasty in 1911.

### Foundation for Later Treaty System

The precedent established by the Opium Wars—that military force could compel China to accept foreign terms—emboldened other Western powers and Japan to pursue their own "unequal treaties." By the late 19th century, China was ringed by treaty ports, concessions, and spheres of influence, with foreign powers enjoying privileges that undermined Qing sovereignty.
