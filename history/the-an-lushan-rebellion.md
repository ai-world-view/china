# The An Lushan Rebellion

## Summary

The An Lushan Rebellion (755–763 CE) was a devastating military uprising that shattered the Tang Dynasty's political unity and presaged its eventual decline. Led by An Lushan, a half-Turkish, half-Sogdian general who had risen through the ranks of the Tang military apparatus, the rebellion erupted from the northeast frontier and swept southward, capturing the capital Chang'an and forcing the court into exile. Over the course of eight years of warfare, the rebellion cost an estimated one to two million lives in direct casualties alone. The Tang census of 754 CE counted approximately 52.9 million registered inhabitants; by 764 CE, after the rebellion's suppression, the registered population had collapsed to roughly 16.9 million—a decline of more than 35 million persons reflecting mass death, displacement, and the breakdown of provincial census administration. The rebellion fundamentally transformed the structure of Tang governance, military organization, and fiscal capacity. The uprising's suppression required the court to delegate enormous autonomous power to regional military governors (jiedushi) in order to raise armies capable of defeating the rebellion; this decentralization never fully reversed, rendering the central government permanently weaker. The An Lushan Rebellion marks the threshold between the Tang Dynasty's early cosmopolitan golden age and its slow dissolution into regional fragmentation, and it forced a profound recalibration of imperial statecraft and military strategy.

## Rise of An Lushan and Court Politics

An Lushan (704–757) was born to a family of mixed ethnicity—his father was reportedly a Sogdian or Turk from Central Asia, and his mother was a Turk of the Ashina clan. This mixed heritage was not unusual in the cosmopolitan Tang court, which recruited talent from across its vast realm. An Lushan rose through military ranks under the patronage of generals and court officials, proving himself a capable and aggressive commander on the northeastern frontier, particularly against the Khitan people. By his fifties, An Lushan held multiple military posts simultaneously, serving as commander of three separate regional military provinces: Fanyang, Pinglu, and Hedong. These three commands encompassed vast territories and populations in the northeastern reaches of the empire.

An Lushan's meteoric rise was enabled by the political context of the mid-Tang court. The Xuanzong Emperor (r. 712–756) had gradually concentrated power in his favorite concubine, Yang Guifei, and her relatives, particularly her cousin Yang Guozhong, who controlled the imperial bureaucracy. The emperor's infatuation with Yang Guifei—said to be one of the four beauties of ancient China—distracted the court from military and administrative affairs. Meanwhile, Yang Guozhong, as Chancellor, resented An Lushan's independent power base on the frontier and pursued policies that marginalized him from the central government's inner circle. An Lushan, in turn, cultivated enormous military resources and maintained a fiercely loyal personal army, sensing that the cosmopolitan, civilian-led Tang court could not long tolerate an independent-minded warlord.

The immediate catalyst for rebellion arrived in 755 when court factions moved to reassign An Lushan away from his cherished northeastern commands. Rather than accept diminishment, An Lushan declared a rebellion, proclaiming himself generalissimo and claiming that he was marching to the capital to eliminate the corrupt Yang family and restore proper governance. This revolutionary rhetoric—claiming to act against corruption rather than against the emperor—was a strategic move that enabled some of his subordinates and regional officials to join his cause without explicitly renouncing their loyalty to the throne.

## The Rebellion's Course and Capture of Chang'an

An Lushan's rebellion progressed with stunning speed. In 755, his armies swept southward from the northeast, overwhelming regular Tang forces and recruiting additional troops from defecting units and frontier peoples. By 756, less than a year into the rebellion, An Lushan's forces had captured the city of Luoyang (the eastern capital of the Tang dual-capital system) and were marching on Chang'an itself. The imperial court, panicked by the approaching armies and wracked by recrimination, dissolved into chaos. The Xuanzong Emperor, faced with military collapse, initially attempted to flee westward along the imperial highway toward Sichuan; his entourage included Yang Guifei and her relatives. However, the imperial guard, demoralized and angry at the corruption they associated with the Yang family, mutinied. In one of history's most poignant episodes, the soldiers refused to continue unless Yang Guifei and her cousin Yang Guozhong were executed as scapegoats for the disaster. The emperor, unable to resist his own army, reluctantly consented to Yang Guifei's strangulation at Mawei Station; Yang Guozhong was also killed.

The capture of Chang'an by An Lushan's forces in 756 marked the rebellion's apex. An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor, establishing a rival imperial court and attempting to legitimize his rule through dynastic ritual. For several years, substantial portions of northern China acknowledged An Lushan's authority, and the Tang court was reduced to a rump state in exile in Sichuan, deprived of the capital and most of its economic base.

## Turning Points and Military Stalemate

An Lushan's attempt to consolidate his new "dynasty" proved fragile. The general's health was declining (he was over fifty when the rebellion began), and he suffered from a painful eye condition. More significantly, his generals and subordinates—particularly Shi Siming and later An Qingxu—began to vie for power within the rebel forces, creating factional tensions that weakened the rebellion from within. In 757, An Lushan died (possibly murdered, though accounts vary), and these succession disputes intensified.

Meanwhile, the Tang court, regrouping in Sichuan and later establishing a shadow government, managed to mobilize counteroffensives. Critically, the Tang court also sought military assistance from outside the empire: they appealed to the Uighur Khaganate, a powerful confederation on the northern steppes. The Uighurs, motivated partly by Tang bribes and partly by their own interest in limiting the rise of a new Central Asian power, contributed cavalry forces that proved decisive. The alliance, however, came at a steep price: the Tang court promised the Uighur khan the right to plunder any city recovered from the rebels. In 757, the Tang forces, reinforced by Uighur cavalry, retook Chang'an. In 762 CE, Uighur forces under Moyu Khan assisted in a second major campaign to suppress the rebellion's remnants in Henan, after which they collected their promised tribute by sacking Luoyang—carrying off silk bolts, valuables, and captives by the thousands over several days of looting and leaving the former eastern capital economically devastated for years. The rebel forces, now led by the successors to An Lushan's command, retreated northward and established a rival state in the northeast.

The rebellion did not end with the recapture of Chang'an. Sporadic warfare continued for six more years as various rebel factions held out in the north. Shi Siming and later his son Shi Chaoyi maintained control of Hebei and Shandong provinces, resisting Tang efforts to reintegrate the region. The Tang court, unable to mount a decisive military campaign, instead enlisted various military governors to contain and gradually erode the rebel position. Only in 763, nearly a decade after An Lushan's initial uprising, did the remaining rebel forces finally submit. The end came when Shi Chaoyi's principal commanders in Hebei surrendered to the Tang; abandoned and pursued, he hanged himself, and the surviving rebel generals were pardoned and left in place as autonomous military governors of the northeast.

## Tibetan Expansion and the Empire's Western Crisis

The An Lushan Rebellion created a strategic vacuum on the Tang's western and northwestern frontiers that the Tibetan Empire (Tubo) exploited with devastating effect. As Tang armies were recalled from the Central Asian garrisons to fight the rebellion in the interior, Tibet moved rapidly to fill the void. By 763 CE—the very year the northeastern rebellion formally ended—Tibetan forces under Emperor Trisong Detsen had overrun the Gansu corridor, seized Dunhuang and other key Silk Road oasis cities, and briefly captured Chang'an itself. The Tibetan occupation of Chang'an lasted approximately fifteen days before a Tang counterforce assembled from scattered regional armies compelled their withdrawal; the symbolic shock of a second sack of the capital within a decade proved profound.

The long-term consequences for China's western position were severe. Tibet maintained control of Dunhuang and the Hexi Corridor for over a century; the Tang did not recover Dunhuang until 848 CE, when a local Chinese uprising expelled the Tibetan garrison. The disruption of the Silk Road severed Tang from Central Asian trade and diplomacy, accelerating the economic decline of the empire's western regions. The loss of the western frontier garrisons also meant the permanent end of the Tang's ambition to project power into Central Asia—a reach that had defined the early Tang's cosmopolitan expansionism and produced the dynasty's multicultural vitality.

## Poet Du Fu and the Rebellion's Human Cost

The An Lushan Rebellion was not merely a military and political crisis; it was a human catastrophe. The warfare, famines, and dislocations it caused killed an estimated one to two million people—roughly ten percent of the Tang population at the time. Cities were sacked, the countryside was devastated, and refugees fled in massive numbers. The rebellion's human toll left an indelible mark on Tang culture and memory.

The great Tang poet Du Fu (712–770) witnessed and documented the rebellion's horrors. His "Three Officials" poems describe the forced conscription of peasants into the imperial armies; his "Three Separations" poems lament the separations and deaths caused by warfare. In works like *Chunwang* ("Spring View"), composed when the capital was in rebel hands, Du Fu mourned the fall of Chang'an and the scattering of his family. His later Chengdu poems, written during the rebellion's final years, reflect on displacement, loss, and the gap between the court's indifference and the common people's suffering. Du Fu's poetry transformed the An Lushan Rebellion from a military-political event into a human tragedy, and his witness remains one of the most vivid literary records of the catastrophe.

Wang Wei (699–759), a contemporary and friend of Du Fu, experienced the rebellion from a wholly different vantage: captured when Chang'an fell in 756, he was pressed into service as an official under An Lushan's Yan court. Unable to resist without risking execution, Wang Wei complied—but he composed the poem *Coerced Service at the Freezing Pond* (凝碧池), lamenting his forced collaboration, a text that later served as evidence of his reluctance and helped secure his pardon when Tang authority was restored. Even so, he was demoted before eventual rehabilitation. Wang Wei's experience illustrates the impossible choices facing scholars, artists, and officials caught in the rebellion's crossfire: to survive was often to compromise, and the Tang restoration's justice was accordingly ambivalent.

## Administrative and Military Consequences

The rebellion's most enduring consequence was the transformation of Tang military and administrative structure. To defeat An Lushan and the rebel holdouts, the Tang court had been forced to establish increasingly autonomous regional military governors (jiedushi). These officials were granted control over territory, taxation, and military forces; in theory, they were imperial appointees, but in practice, they accumulated autonomous power that never fully reverted to the center.

By the late Tang, the regional military governors had become semi-independent warlords. The central government's direct taxation shrank as the provinces' revenues were retained locally. The imperial examination system, which had theoretically created a meritocratic officialdom loyal to the center, was undermined by the reality of regional power. Although the court salvaged some income by creating a centralized salt monopoly under commissioners such as Liu Yan, the land-tax base slipped from its grasp; the two-tax reform (*liangshui fa*) of 780 acknowledged the new reality by taxing property twice yearly rather than relying on the equal-field registers the rebellion had shattered.

This structural fragmentation presaged the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period (907–960), when the Tang dynasty finally collapsed and China fractured into competing regional states. In this sense, the An Lushan Rebellion marks the beginning of Tang's decline from a unified, centralized superpower into a fragmenting empire. The rebellion forced the court to trade short-term military survival for long-term structural decentralization—a bargain that ultimately proved unsustainable.

## Legacy and Tang Recalibration

The Tang Dynasty did survive the An Lushan Rebellion, but the empire that emerged was notably transformed. The Xuanzong Emperor, who had fled in disgrace, abdicated in favor of his son, the Suzong Emperor (r. 756–762). Suzong was succeeded by the Daizong Emperor (r. 762–779), who oversaw the final suppression of the rebellion and attempted to restructure central authority. The Zhenyuan Era (785–805), under the Dezong Emperor (r. 779–805), witnessed a partial restoration of central power and a brief flourishing of culture and scholarship, suggesting that the Tang had managed to stabilize and partially recover.

However, the fundamental shift in the balance of power between center and periphery proved irreversible. The military governors who had been granted autonomous power to fight the rebellion proved unwilling to relinquish it. The most powerful jiedushi maintained private armies, controlled taxation, and treated their territories as hereditary fiefs. This permanent decentralization ensured that no subsequent Tang emperor could exercise the kind of absolute authority that Xuanzong had possessed in the early eighth century.

Culturally, the An Lushan Rebellion marked a watershed. The cosmopolitan, hedonistic early Tang culture—exemplified by the court of Xuanzong and Yang Guifei—gave way to a more introspective, melancholic aesthetic. Later Tang poetry, art, and philosophy reflected a deeper awareness of transience, human suffering, and the fragility of imperial order. The rebellion's trauma thus left its mark not only on political institutions but on the Tang artistic and intellectual legacy itself.
