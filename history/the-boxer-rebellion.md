# The Boxer Rebellion

**Category**: History
**Key figures**: The Boxer Societies (義和團), Empress Dowager Cixi, Li Hongzhang, Lord Salisbury (British Foreign Secretary), Alfred von Waldersee (Alliance commander)

## Summary

The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) was a violent anti-foreign and anti-Christian uprising in northern China, erupting during the waning decades of the Qing Dynasty. The rebels, members of martial societies calling themselves the Boxers (義和團, Yihetuan — "Righteous and Harmonious Fists"), drew on traditional martial arts and ritual magic, believing invulnerability to bullets was attainable through spiritual cultivation. Targeting foreign missionaries, Chinese Christian converts, and Western traders, the Boxers attempted to drive out what they perceived as foreign contamination and restore what they understood as traditional Chinese civilization.

The movement originated in response to the cumulative humiliation of the Opium Wars, the "unequal treaties," foreign control of China's customs revenue, and missionary proselytization that had undermined Confucian social order. The Qing court, internally divided between reformers and conservatives, initially attempted to suppress the Boxers but found popular sympathy within the rural populace. Under the influence of Empress Dowager Cixi and conservative officials, the court issued a formal declaration of war against the foreign powers on June 21, 1900, and tacitly aligned imperial military forces with the rebellion. By mid-1900 the Boxers and some Qing troops had besieged foreign diplomatic compounds in Beijing; the siege of the Legation Quarter lasted 55 days (June 20 – August 14, 1900) and drew international attention.

In response, an eight-nation coalition — Japan, Russia, Germany, Britain, the United States, France, Italy, and Austria-Hungary — organized a joint military expedition of approximately 55,000 troops to relieve the diplomatic compounds. Coalition forces defeated the Boxer-Qing army after several engagements and occupied Beijing on August 14, 1900. Empress Dowager Cixi and Emperor Guangxu fled west to Xi'an. The rebellion was fully crushed by late 1901, but not before tens of thousands of Chinese lives were lost, a vast swath of the countryside was devastated, and foreign troops had occupied China's capital.

## Origins and Background

The Boxer movement emerged first in Shandong province in 1898–1899, where a combination of specific grievances converged. The German seizure of Jiaozhou Bay (1897) and the subsequent carving up of Chinese territory as "spheres of influence" by competing powers intensified popular anxiety. Simultaneously, severe flooding along the Yellow River in 1898 had driven tens of thousands of rural poor into bands of unemployed young men, providing a social base for the martial societies.

The Boxers drew on older traditions of popular religion, local militia culture, and heterodox spiritual practices. They believed that spirit possession and ritual practice rendered practitioners invulnerable to bullets — a claim that spread rapidly in an illiterate rural population with limited access to information about the actual outcomes of previous confrontations with foreign firepower. Women's groups called the "Red Lanterns" (紅燈照) played a parallel role, claiming special spiritual powers.

Initial Qing response was ambivalent. Governor Yuan Shikai suppressed early Boxer activities in Shandong, but when provincial authority in Zhili (the province surrounding Beijing) passed to officials sympathetic to the movement, the Boxers were effectively given free rein. By May 1900, Boxer bands entered Beijing itself.

## The Uprising and the Siege of the Legation Quarter

From May through June 1900, Boxer forces and their supporters killed hundreds of Chinese Christian converts and foreign missionaries across northern China. On June 20, 1900, Baron Clemens von Ketteler, the German minister, was shot dead in the streets of Beijing by a Qing soldier — an incident that precipitated the formal siege of the foreign legations. The following day, June 21, Empress Dowager Cixi issued her declaration of war against the foreign powers.

The Legation Quarter in Beijing — a walled area housing the diplomatic missions of eleven nations — became the focal point of the siege. Roughly 900 soldiers and civilians from multiple nations, alongside approximately 2,800 Chinese Christians who had sought refuge there, withstood the siege under the informal command of the British Minister Sir Claude MacDonald. The defenders improvised fortifications, rationed food, and repelled repeated assaults. Approximately 76 defenders were killed during the 55-day siege.

A secondary siege took place at the Beitang (North Cathedral), where Bishop Alphonse Favier and roughly 3,400 Chinese Catholics and 43 French and Italian sailors held out against Boxer and Qing forces for the same period, with approximately 400 Catholics killed.

A notable feature of the siege was the so-called "Mutual Protection of Southeast China" agreement (東南互保): Chinese governors in the prosperous Yangtze Delta provinces — including Zhang Zhidong and Liu Kunyi — refused to follow Cixi's declaration of war, instead reaching private agreements with foreign consuls to maintain order in their regions. This effectively limited the rebellion to the north and demonstrated that provincial authority could operate independently of the court when imperial prestige was sufficiently degraded.

## The Eight-Nation Alliance

The coalition's military response was organized with surprising speed. After an initial relief force of approximately 2,000 troops under British Admiral Edward Seymour was repulsed and forced to retreat in June 1900, a larger multinational force was assembled under the overall command of German Field Marshal Alfred von Waldersee, though the campaign was largely concluded before Waldersee's arrival.

Coalition forces — totaling approximately 55,000 troops, with Japan and Russia supplying the largest contingents — advanced from Tianjin, which they had captured on July 14, 1900. They entered Beijing on August 14, 1900, lifting the siege. The relief of the legations was followed by widespread looting by coalition troops, with soldiers from all nations participating in the pillaging of the Forbidden City, the Imperial Summer Palace, and private residences.

In the months that followed, coalition troops carried out punitive expeditions across northern China — burning villages, executing suspected Boxers, and imposing collective punishment on communities suspected of harboring rebels. German forces, following the Kaiser's notorious "Hun speech" of July 27, 1900 urging his soldiers to "make a name for themselves as the Huns of Attila," were particularly noted for severity. The combined military and punitive operations killed an estimated 100,000 or more Chinese — the precise death toll remains disputed.

## The Boxer Protocol and Aftermath

Li Hongzhang (1823–1901) — veteran diplomat and the most accomplished statesman of the late Qing — was recalled from semi-retirement to negotiate the peace settlement. The resulting Boxer Protocol, signed September 7, 1901, imposed terms that further humiliated China:

- **Indemnity**: 450 million taels of silver (approximately $333 million USD at the 1901 exchange rate), to be paid in annual installments over 39 years at 4% interest, totaling approximately 982 million taels with interest — an enormous sum relative to Chinese state revenues.
- **Military restrictions**: Foreign powers gained the right to garrison troops in Beijing along the Legation Quarter and along the corridor to the sea; the Chinese were prohibited from importing arms for two years.
- **Diplomatic humiliation**: China was required to post official apologies in Western capitals; the Zongli Yamen (foreign ministry) was reorganized as the more prominent Waijiao Bu.
- **Dismantlement of defenses**: Taku forts were demolished.

Li Hongzhang died on November 7, 1901, just weeks after signing the Protocol — widely perceived as broken by the ordeal of negotiating China's deepest humiliation.

In a striking exception, the United States eventually returned its share of the indemnity (approximately $17 million in excess of actual claims) to fund Chinese students studying in America — the Boxer Indemnity Scholarship Program, which began in 1908 and funded thousands of Chinese scholars, including many who would become leading scientists, educators, and officials in twentieth-century China. Other powers made partial or complete remittances over subsequent decades.

## Significance

The Boxer Rebellion crystallized China's subordination within the Western-dominated international system that had solidified through the Opium Wars. It represented the last major instance of organized popular resistance to foreign encroachment in the late Qing period and demonstrated the futility of such resistance against industrialized military power. The uprising's suppression by a foreign coalition, coupled with the Qing court's inability to either prevent or channel the movement, deepened the dynasty's loss of prestige and intensified the perception of the empire as a "sick man of Asia."

The rebellion accelerated the dynasty's decline and the legitimacy crisis that would culminate in the 1911 Xinhai Revolution and the fall of the Qing in 1912. The indemnity — and the symbolic occupation of Beijing — remained touchstones of national humiliation in Chinese political discourse throughout the twentieth century, shaping the nationalism that animated both the Republican and Communist movements.

The rebellion also exposed rifts within Chinese elite networks: reformer-modernizers (represented by Li Hongzhang and the southern governors) argued for accommodation with the West and selective modernization, while conservatives (aligned with the Empress Dowager) sought to resist foreign influence through tradition and martial mobilization. The rebellion's failure vindicated the reformers' diagnosis that purely military and cultural resistance could not prevail against the West's technological and organizational superiority — contributing to the intellectual ferment that led to the revolutionary movements of Sun Yat-sen and ultimately to the Chinese Communist Party.
