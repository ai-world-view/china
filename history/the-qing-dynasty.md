# The Qing Dynasty

The **Qing Dynasty** (清朝, 1644–1912 CE) was the final imperial dynasty of China, established by the Jurchen Manchu people from northeastern Manchuria. Lasting 268 years under twelve emperors, it expanded Chinese territory to its greatest historical extent, presided over extraordinary cultural and commercial achievement, yet ultimately failed to adapt to Western industrialism and imperialism, collapsing in the 1911 Xinhai Revolution that inaugurated the modern Chinese republic.

## Origins and Conquest of the Ming (1583–1644)

The Qing state originated with **Nurhaci** (努爾哈赤, 1559–1626 CE), a Jurchen chieftain who unified the Jurchen clans of Manchuria between 1583 and 1616, founding the Later Jin state and organizing his followers into the **Eight Banners** (八旗) military-administrative system. The Eight Banners divided the Jurchen population into eight colored units (plain and bordered versions of yellow, white, red, and blue) that served simultaneously as military regiments, social organizations, and census structures. Later, Mongol and Han Chinese banner units were added, creating a multiethnic military apparatus.

Nurhaci's son **Hong Taiji** (皇太極, r. 1626–1643) renamed the dynasty "Qing" in 1636 and the Jurchen people "Manchu," projecting a pan-Asian imperial identity. When the Ming collapsed in 1644 amid peasant rebellions—Li Zicheng's forces sacked Beijing and the last Ming emperor, Chongzhen, hanged himself on Coal Hill—Manchu armies under the regent Dorgon crossed the Great Wall at Shanhai Pass, invited by the Ming general Wu Sangui, and captured Beijing. Qing forces subsequently spent four decades subduing Ming loyalist resistance across southern China, conquering the last holdout on Taiwan in 1683.

## Consolidation Under the Three Great Emperors (1661–1796)

Three emperors spanning 135 years built Qing power to its apex:

### Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722)

**Kangxi** (康熙, personal name Xuanye) ascended the throne at age seven and ruled for 61 years—the longest reign in Chinese history. He personally led campaigns that subdued the **Three Feudatories Rebellion** (三藩之亂, 1673–1681), a major internal war against Han Chinese warlords who had aided the Manchu conquest but then turned against central authority. He annexed **Taiwan** in 1683 after defeating the Zheng family regime. He won decisive victories against the Dzungar Mongols, incorporating Outer Mongolia. Kangxi also concluded the **Treaty of Nerchinsk** (1689) with Russia—the first formal treaty between China and a European power—fixing the Sino-Russian border and establishing regulated trade.

Kangxi was a devoted patron of Chinese culture. He compiled the 50,000-character *Kangxi Dictionary* (1716), which standardized Chinese orthography, and sponsored the *Complete Library of the Four Treasuries* project's predecessors. He hosted Jesuit missionaries at court—notably the astronomer Ferdinand Verbiest and the painter Giuseppe Castiglione—employing their astronomical and military expertise while limiting their proselytization. Kangxi eventually issued an edict in 1721 banning Christian missionary activity after the Pope condemned Chinese ancestor veneration as superstitious.

### Yongzheng Emperor (r. 1722–1735)

**Yongzheng** (雍正) seized the throne under contested circumstances following Kangxi's death and ruled for 13 years with exceptional administrative intensity. He centralized fiscal management, standardizing the land-and-poll tax, creating the **Grand Council** (軍機處) as an inner-circle advisory body that bypassed the traditional ministries and became the Qing's most powerful governing institution. He cracked down on corruption among provincial governors and introduced the practice of sealed palace memorials, allowing officials to report directly to the emperor without bureaucratic interception. Yongzheng's reforms created a leaner, more efficient government that stabilized Qing finances.

### Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796)

**Qianlong** (乾隆) presided over the dynasty's territorial and cultural zenith. His ten military campaigns (自稱十全武功, "Ten Complete Military Victories") extended Qing control over **Xinjiang** (1759, defeated the Dzungar Khanate in a campaign that killed hundreds of thousands), **Tibet** (suzerainty consolidated), and parts of **Burma** and **Vietnam** (less successfully). By 1760, the Qing empire encompassed roughly 13 million square kilometers—the largest extent of any Chinese dynasty, incorporating Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet into a multiethnic empire.

Qianlong sponsored the massive **Siku Quanshu** (四庫全書, "Complete Library of the Four Treasuries"), completed in 1782—a compilation of approximately 3,500 texts selected from over 10,000 works, copied by hand in seven sets totaling an estimated 800 million characters. The project simultaneously preserved classical learning and, through its compilation process, destroyed or censored thousands of texts deemed subversive or anti-Manchu—a large-scale literary inquisition now known as the *wenziyu* (literary inquisitions).

Qianlong's famous reception of the British Macartney Mission in 1793 illustrated Qing confidence and myopia. Lord Macartney sought to negotiate a trade treaty and the opening of diplomatic relations; Qianlong famously replied that China "possesses all things" and had no need of British goods, declining to expand trade beyond the Canton System.

## The Canton System and Global Trade

Throughout much of the Qing era, **foreign trade** was channeled through the **Canton System** (十三行, "Thirteen Hongs"), established in 1757. All trade with Western nations was restricted to the port of **Guangzhou (Canton)**, conducted through licensed Chinese merchant guilds called *cohong* under government supervision. Western traders—including the British East India Company—were confined to the **Thirteen Factories** quarter along the Pearl River outside the city walls and permitted to reside only during the trading season (October–March).

Despite restrictions, the Canton trade was enormously lucrative. China exported silk, porcelain, tea, and lacquerware in exchange for silver bullion; in the early 18th century, China ran persistent trade surpluses with Europe, draining European silver eastward. Britain's East India Company alone purchased millions of pounds of tea annually. By the 1820s, Britain began offsetting its silver drain by exporting opium from Bengal to China—a trade illegal in China but tolerated by corrupt officials—setting the stage for the Opium Wars.

## Agricultural Economy and Population Explosion

The Qing agricultural system, particularly the introduction of New World crops including **sweet potatoes**, **maize**, and **peanuts** via the Philippines trade route in the 16th–17th centuries, dramatically expanded China's arable land and caloric base. These drought-resistant crops could grow on previously uncultivable hillsides and poor soils. Combined with relative peace under early Qing rule, China's population grew explosively: from approximately **100 million** in 1650 CE to **300 million** by 1800 CE and over **430 million** by 1850 CE—roughly one-third of global population. This demographic expansion created enormous pressure on land, food, and resources, fueling the social instability that would contribute to 19th-century rebellions.

## Decline and Collision with the West (1796–1860)

By the late 18th century, population pressure, administrative corruption, and declining land-to-person ratios bred widespread discontent. The **White Lotus Rebellion** (白蓮教起義, 1796–1804) in central China, suppressed only after eight years and massive expenditure, revealed the corruption of Qing provincial armies. The **Eight Trigrams Uprising** (1813) briefly seized part of the Forbidden City itself.

The **First Opium War** (1840–1842) marked China's first major military defeat by a European power. When Commissioner Lin Zexu seized and destroyed approximately 20,000 chests of British opium at Guangzhou in 1839, Britain responded with naval bombardment and amphibious landings along the Chinese coast. Qing coastal defenses, equipped with obsolete bronze cannon and poorly trained troops, proved unable to resist British steamships and modern artillery. The **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約, 1842)—China's first "unequal treaty"—ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain, opened five treaty ports (Guangzhou, Shanghai, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Xiamen), imposed a 21 million silver dollar indemnity, and granted extraterritorial legal privileges to British subjects.

The **Second Opium War** (1856–1860), sparked by the *Arrow* Incident (Britain) and the execution of a French missionary (France), resulted in Anglo-French forces burning the **Summer Palace** (圓明園) outside Beijing and imposing the **Treaty of Beijing** (1860), which opened additional ports, permitted missionaries to travel freely inland, legalized opium importation, and ceded Kowloon Peninsula to Britain. Russia exploited the crisis to annex approximately 1 million square kilometers north of the Amur River via the Convention of Peking (1860).

## The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864)

The most devastating of all 19th-century rebellions, the **Taiping Heavenly Kingdom** (太平天國) was founded by Hong Xiuquan (洪秀全, 1814–1864), a Hakka man from Guangdong who, after repeatedly failing the imperial examinations, experienced a religious vision that convinced him he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ. Hong built a millenarian Christian kingdom that captured Nanjing in 1853, renamed it Tianjing (Heavenly Capital), and held it for 11 years while controlling much of southern China.

The Taiping promoted radical social reforms: abolition of foot-binding, separation of sexes, communal land ownership, and an egalitarian governance based on their heterodox Christianity. Qing forces—supplemented eventually by the Western-led **Ever Victorious Army** under Frederick Townsend Ward and later Charles Gordon—suppressed the rebellion by 1864 after extraordinary military effort. Estimates of total deaths from the Taiping Rebellion range from **20 million to 30 million**, making it among the deadliest civil wars in world history.

## Self-Strengthening and Reform Attempts (1860–1895)

Chastened by the Taiping and the Opium Wars, the Qing launched the **Self-Strengthening Movement** (洋務運動, 1861–1895) under reformers including **Prince Gong** (奕訢) and officials Li Hongzhang (李鴻章), Zeng Guofan (曾國藩), and Zuo Zongtang (左宗棠). The movement sought to adopt Western military technology while preserving Confucian social order, articulated as "Chinese learning as the substance, Western learning for practical use" (*Zhongti Xiyong*). Arsenals were built (most notably the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai, 1865), a modern navy was assembled, and Western-style academies trained engineers and translators.

The **Sino-Japanese War** (甲午戰爭, 1894–1895) exposed the movement's limits. Japan, transformed by the Meiji Restoration since 1868, defeated the Qing both on land in Korea and at sea in the Battle of the Yellow Sea (September 1894), sinking much of the Beiyang Fleet—the modernized navy built at enormous cost over two decades. The **Treaty of Shimonoseki** (1895) forced China to cede Taiwan and the Pescadores Islands to Japan, recognize Korean independence (opening Korea to Japanese influence), and pay an indemnity of 200 million taels of silver.

## Boxer Rebellion and the Eight-Nation Alliance (1899–1901)

The **Boxer Uprising** (義和團運動) began as a rural anti-Christian and anti-foreign movement in Shandong Province, drawing members who believed traditional martial arts (*yihe quan*, "righteous harmony boxing") would make them invulnerable to bullets. The Boxers, initially suppressed by Qing forces, were eventually co-opted by the Qing court under Empress Dowager Cixi, who declared war on foreign powers in June 1900 and encouraged the Boxers' siege of the Beijing Legation Quarter.

An **Eight-Nation Alliance** (Britain, United States, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, Italy, Austria-Hungary) assembled an expeditionary force of approximately 20,000 troops that relieved the legations after 55 days and sacked Beijing. The resulting **Boxer Protocol** (1901) imposed an indemnity of 450 million taels of silver (approximately US$335 million)—to be paid over 39 years at 4% interest, totaling approximately 982 million taels—on the Qing government, occupied key forts guarding Beijing, and stationed foreign troops permanently in the capital.

## The Empress Dowager Cixi and Court Politics

**Empress Dowager Cixi** (慈禧太后, 1835–1908) dominated Qing politics for nearly five decades. A former imperial concubine who gave birth to the Tongzhi Emperor (r. 1861–1875), Cixi engineered a palace coup in 1861 to become co-regent alongside Empress Dowager Ci'an. After Ci'an's death in 1881, Cixi ruled effectively alone, controlling each subsequent emperor including her nephew Guangxu. When Guangxu attempted the radical **Hundred Days Reform** (百日維新) of 1898—issuing over 100 edicts in 103 days to modernize education, military, and administration along Meiji Japanese lines—Cixi staged a coup, placed Guangxu under house arrest, executed six leading reformers (the "Six Gentlemen of the Hundred Days"), and reversed most reforms.

Cixi's power rested on a coalition of conservative Manchu nobility and senior Han Chinese officials suspicious of rapid westernization. Her legacy is contested: defenders credit her with presiding over a period of relative stability and initiating post-Boxer reforms (the **New Policies**, including the abolition of the examination system in 1905 and the establishment of constitutional preparations); critics hold her responsible for squandering modernization opportunities and misallocating naval funds (reportedly diverting funds intended for the Beiyang Fleet to renovate the Summer Palace's **Marble Boat**).

Both Cixi and Guangxu died on consecutive days in November 1908, under circumstances that have long fueled speculation of poisoning.

## The Xinhai Revolution and Abdication (1911–1912)

The **Xinhai Revolution** (辛亥革命) began on October 10, 1911—still celebrated as "Double Ten Day" (雙十節) in Taiwan—when revolutionary soldiers at **Wuchang** (武昌, in present-day Wuhan) mutinied after an accidental bomb explosion revealed their conspiracy. Within weeks, province after province declared independence from the Qing. The Qing recalled **Yuan Shikai** (袁世凱), the most powerful Han Chinese general, to suppress the revolution; Yuan instead negotiated between the court and the revolutionaries, extracting maximum personal power from both sides.

**Sun Yat-sen** was elected Provisional President of the Republic of China in December 1911. Negotiations between Sun and Yuan resulted in Sun ceding the presidency to Yuan in February 1912 in exchange for Yuan's guarantee that the Qing emperor would abdicate. On **February 12, 1912**, the six-year-old emperor **Puyi** (溥儀) abdicated under the *Articles of Favorable Treatment*, which granted him the title "Emperor" in perpetuity, allowed him to remain in the Forbidden City, and guaranteed an annual stipend of 4 million silver dollars (later renegotiated).

The abdication ended 268 years of Qing rule, 2,000 years of imperial governance in China, and the world's longest-lived continuous political system.

## Legacy

The Qing left an indelible imprint on modern China:

- **Territory**: The Qing's territorial conquests—Xinjiang, Tibet, Mongolia, Manchuria—define the borders of the modern People's Republic of China. The PRC explicitly claims to inherit Qing territorial sovereignty, making Qing history politically consequential today.
- **Population and Demographics**: The Qing's Han-Manchu-Mongol-Tibetan-Uyghur ethnic framework created the official narrative of the "56 ethnic nationalities" (*minzu*) of modern China.
- **Art and Material Culture**: Qing porcelain (particularly the famille rose palette, developed c. 1720), lacquerware, silk embroidery, and furniture remain benchmarks of Chinese luxury arts. The imperial collection housed in the National Palace Museum in Taipei and the Palace Museum in Beijing is substantially Qing in assemblage.
- **Language**: Standard Mandarin (*Putonghua*), the national language of modern China, is based on the northern Chinese dialect of Beijing—the Qing capital—reflecting Qing linguistic influence.
- **Historical Warning**: The Qing's fate—internal rebellions exploited by foreign powers, too-slow modernization, and institutional rigidity in the face of technological change—haunts Chinese political consciousness and informs the CCP's emphasis on national unity and rapid modernization.
