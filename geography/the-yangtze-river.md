# The Yangtze River

The Yangtze River is the longest river in Asia and China's vital circulatory system—a 6,300-kilometer waterway flowing from the Tibetan Plateau to the East China Sea that has shaped settlement, culture, economy, and the nation's sense of itself for millennia. If the Yellow River is "China's Sorrow," the Yangtze is China's prosperity and connectivity.

## Geography and Course

The Yangtze originates in the Tanggula Mountains on the Tibetan Plateau at an elevation of approximately 5,800 meters, its source claimed by both the Tuotuo River and Dangqu tributaries depending on measurement methodology. From its headwaters, the river descends sharply through gorge-cut highland terrain before entering the Yunnan plateau, where it briefly runs in parallel with the Mekong and Salween rivers — a geological phenomenon unique in Asia.

The river then turns northeast to cut through the **Three Gorges** — Qutang (8 km), Wu (45 km), and Xiling (76 km) — a stretch of roughly 193 kilometers in which the river passes through sheer limestone walls rising 600–900 meters above the water. These gorges, stretching from Fengjie (Chongqing) to Yichang (Hubei), represent the most dramatic section of the river and captivated Chinese painters, poets, and later Western travelers for centuries.

As the Yangtze emerges from the gorges at Yichang it fans across the wide Middle Yangtze Plain, passing through Dongting Lake in Hunan before reaching Wuhan. The lower reaches flow through flatter alluvial terrain past Nanjing before spreading into the Yangtze River Delta in Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces. The delta region, averaging less than 10 meters above sea level, comprises some of the most densely populated and economically productive land on earth, encompassing greater Shanghai, Suzhou, Nanjing, and Hangzhou.

The Yangtze drains a basin of nearly 1.8 million square kilometers — about 20 percent of China's total land area — and carries an average annual discharge of approximately 30,000 cubic meters per second at its mouth, the fourth highest in the world.

## Major Tributaries

The Yangtze's hydrology is shaped by major tributaries joining along its length:

- **Jialing River** — joins at Chongqing, draining much of northern Sichuan; the city's strategic position at this confluence made it a wartime capital (1937–1945)
- **Min River** — joins at Yibin, Sichuan; debated as the effective source river by some hydrologists
- **Wu River** — joins near Fuling (Chongqing); drains southeastern Sichuan and northwestern Guizhou
- **Han River** — the longest tributary (1,532 km), joining at Wuhan; its northern reach was the key link exploited by the Grand Canal system to connect the Yangtze watershed to North China
- **Gan River** — drains through Poyang Lake (China's largest freshwater lake) in Jiangxi before feeding the Yangtze at Hukou
- **Huaihe River** — joins via a complex network of lakes and canals in Jiangsu

The Yangtze also receives runoff that fills Dongting Lake (Hunan) and Poyang Lake (Jiangxi), which act as natural flood buffers and are critical freshwater ecosystems.

## Civilization and Settlement

The Yangtze has served as a foundational axis of Chinese civilization as long as the Yellow River, though with different characteristics. While the Yellow River was the cradle of northern Chinese civilization, the Yangtze gave rise to the **Yangtze River civilization**, which evolved somewhat independently in the south before the rise of centralized Chinese states. Archaeological sites like Hemudu (c. 5500–4500 BCE) in Zhejiang reveal Neolithic rice cultivation and pile-dwelling settlements oriented toward wetland and riverine resources. The Liangzhu culture (c. 3300–2300 BCE), centered on the Yangtze Delta, developed jade-working of extraordinary refinement and apparently a complex proto-state polity.

Unlike the Yellow River's history of catastrophic flooding and course changes (which periodically devastated settled areas and killed millions), the Yangtze's flooding patterns, while occasionally destructive, more consistently deposited nutrient-rich sediment and supported high agricultural productivity. Rice cultivation flourished in the Yangtze valleys, making the river's basin the economic center of southern China from the Han Dynasty onward.

## Key Cities and Strategic Importance

Several of China's most historically significant cities lie along the Yangtze:

**Chongqing** — at the confluence of the Yangtze and Jialing rivers, Chongqing served as the wartime capital of the Republic of China from 1937 to 1945, when the Nationalist government retreated westward from Japanese advances. Today it is one of the world's most populous municipalities (approximately 32 million residents), and its position atop the upper Yangtze made it the eastern terminus of the Sichuan-Tibet corridor.

**Wuhan** — the "three-town" city of Wuchang, Hankou, and Hanyang at the confluence of the Yangtze and Han Rivers, Wuhan has been a commercial and strategic hub since the Tang Dynasty. It was here, on October 10, 1911, that the Wuchang Uprising ignited the Revolution that toppled the Qing Dynasty. Today Wuhan is a major industrial and educational center of approximately 11 million.

**Nanjing** — the "Southern Capital," Nanjing served as imperial capital of several dynasties (Eastern Jin, Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, Chen, early Ming). In the modern era, it was the capital of the Republic of China (1927–1937, 1946–1949) and the site of the Nanjing Massacre (1937), in which Japanese forces killed an estimated 200,000–300,000 civilians in six weeks.

**Yichang** — a formerly modest city that became the staging ground for the Three Gorges Dam project and now serves as the gateway between the middle and upper river navigation zones.

## Economic Importance

For most of Chinese history, the Yangtze has been the economic engine of southern China. Water transport has always been easier, cheaper, and more voluminous than overland routes, making the Yangtze China's natural highway. Goods, people, and ideas flowed along the river; the Grand Canal tied its prosperity to national governance.

The **Grand Canal**, engineered during the Sui Dynasty (581–618 CE) under Emperor Yang and extended under the Yuan Dynasty, connected the Yangtze at Zhenjiang (Jiangsu) directly to the Yellow River and northern capitals. At its peak under the Qing Dynasty, the canal carried approximately 400,000 tons of grain northward annually to feed Beijing and the northern armies. The canal system was so vital that the Qing maintained specialized bureaucracies — the Liangjiang and Caohe governorships — devoted entirely to dike maintenance and grain transport.

In the modern era, the Yangtze became a focal point of foreign imperialism. The Treaty of Nanking (1842) and subsequent "unequal treaties" forced China to open **treaty ports** — Chongqing, Wuhan, Nanjing, and Shanghai among them — allowing foreign steamships to navigate the river and foreign merchants to establish enclaves. British, French, American, and Japanese gunboats patrolled the river under the terms of extraterritorial agreements, a humiliation that colored Chinese nationalism for a century.

Today, the Yangtze is one of the world's busiest inland waterways. The river handles approximately 2.4 billion tons of cargo annually — more than the combined freight volume of all US inland waterways. The Yangtze River Economic Belt, designated a national-level strategy in 2016, encompasses 11 provinces and municipalities, produces roughly 46 percent of China's GDP, and is home to over 40 percent of its population — some 600 million people.

### The Three Gorges Dam

The **Three Gorges Dam** (三峡大坝), located at Sandouping in Yichang, Hubei, is the largest hydroelectric dam in the world by installed capacity. Construction began in December 1994, the dam wall was completed in May 2006, and the project was declared fully operational in July 2012 when the final turbines were commissioned.

Key statistics:
- **Installed capacity**: 22,500 MW (world's largest hydroelectric facility)
- **Annual generation**: approximately 88–100 billion kWh, supplying roughly 2 percent of China's total electricity consumption
- **Reservoir length**: approximately 660 km, stretching to Chongqing
- **Reservoir capacity**: 39.3 billion cubic meters (39.3 km³) total; 22.15 km³ flood-storage capacity
- **People displaced**: approximately 1.3 million, relocated from 13 cities, 140 towns, and over 1,300 villages
- **Dam wall dimensions**: 2,335 m wide, 185 m tall

The dam provides significant flood protection for the densely populated Middle Yangtze Plain, which had suffered catastrophic inundations in 1954 (roughly 33,000 deaths) and 1998 (3,656 deaths, 14 million displaced). It also raised navigation standards — the reservoir allows 10,000-ton vessels to reach Chongqing year-round, compared to small craft previously.

## Historical Conflicts on the Yangtze

The Yangtze has served as a military axis in several of China's defining conflicts:

**Battle of Red Cliffs (208 CE)** — At the narrow gorge stretch near modern Chibi in Hubei, the combined forces of Liu Bei and Sun Quan destroyed the fleet of the northern warlord Cao Cao, halting his bid to unify China. The battle, fought largely with fire-ships in a river naval engagement, determined the Three Kingdoms configuration that shaped China for the next six decades and became one of the most celebrated events in Chinese literature and popular culture.

**Taiping Heavenly Kingdom (1851–1864)** — The Taiping rebels, led by Hong Xiuquan, captured Nanjing in 1853 and renamed it Tianjing ("Heavenly Capital"), holding it for eleven years. The subsequent war for control of the Yangtze valley was among the bloodiest in human history, with estimated death tolls ranging from 20 to 70 million from battle, famine, and disease.

**The 1931 Yangtze Floods** — Massive flooding between July and November 1931 inundated approximately 180,000 square kilometers of the Yangtze and Huai River basins, killing an estimated 3.7 million people through drowning, disease, and famine. The disaster — which submerged the entire city of Wuhan for several months — remains one of the deadliest natural catastrophes in recorded history.

**The Chinese Civil War (1949)** — The People's Liberation Army's crossing of the Yangtze in April 1949, in a flotilla of junks and fishing boats under artillery cover, enabled the capture of Nanjing and effectively ended the Nationalist government's rule on the mainland. The crossing was celebrated in Mao Zedong's poem *Crossing the Yangtze* (渡长江), written immediately afterward.

## Cultural Significance

The Yangtze and its landscape have inspired Chinese arts and philosophy for over two millennia. The misty gorges and riverside cliffs became canonical subjects for landscape painting, particularly during the Song (960–1279) and later dynasties. The gorges appear in the *Odes* (Shijing), Qu Yuan's *Li Sao* (c. 300 BCE), and the poetry of Li Bai and Du Fu in the Tang Dynasty.

Qu Yuan (c. 340–278 BCE), the patriotic poet of Chu state whose suicide by drowning in the Miluo River (a Yangtze tributary) is commemorated at the Dragon Boat Festival, is inseparable from the river's cultural landscape. His *Chu Ci* (Songs of Chu) draw heavily on the supernatural geography of the Yangtze valley.

The Yangtze also figures prominently in Daoist and Buddhist pilgrimage culture. The Wudang Mountains in Hubei, a major Daoist center, overlook a Han River tributary feeding the Yangtze. Emei Mountain in Sichuan, one of the Four Sacred Buddhist Mountains, rises above the Min River watershed.

## Environmental Challenges and Modern Interventions

The twentieth and twenty-first centuries brought unprecedented demands on the Yangtze. Dam construction, industrial development, and urban growth have transformed the river and its ecosystem in ways that now require intensive management.

**Baiji (Yangtze River Dolphin)** — The *Lipotes vexillifer*, or baiji, was a freshwater dolphin endemic to the Yangtze that evolved in isolation for approximately 20 million years. By the 1980s, only approximately 400 individuals remained. A systematic six-week survey in November–December 2006 failed to find a single specimen; the species was subsequently declared **functionally extinct** — the first large vertebrate driven to extinction by human activity in over 50 years and the first dolphin species lost to human impact in history. Contributing factors included dam construction, propeller strikes, overfishing, and pollution.

**Yangtze Finless Porpoise** — The Yangtze finless porpoise (*Neophocaena asiaeorientalis asiaeorientalis*), a separate endemic subspecies, declined from an estimated 2,700 individuals in 1990 to approximately 1,012 in 2012 and roughly 1,249 by 2017 (slightly improved, possibly because of intensive ex-situ conservation and fishing bans). It is listed as critically endangered.

**Fishing Ban** — In January 2021 China implemented a ten-year fishing moratorium on the entire main stem of the Yangtze and its key tributaries, covering approximately 332,000 km² of water. An estimated 230,000 fishing households received government compensation and retraining.

**Pollution and Water Quality** — Industrial effluent, agricultural runoff (particularly phosphorus and nitrogen fertilizers), and urban sewage have degraded water quality in sections of the river. Algal blooms in Poyang and Dongting lakes have increased. The Chinese government's Yangtze Protection Law, enacted in March 2021, prohibits new industrial development along the river's shoreline and mandates stricter discharge controls.

**The 2022 Drought** — Unusually low precipitation in the upper and middle reaches during summer 2022 produced the most severe Yangtze drought in six decades. River levels at Wuhan fell to historic lows in late August; hydropower generation at the Three Gorges Dam dropped sharply, triggering power shortages across Sichuan and Chongqing. The drought exposed the interdependence of the river's hydrological, agricultural, and energy functions.
