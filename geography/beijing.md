# Beijing

## Summary

Beijing, the capital of the People's Republic of China since 1949, has served as a seat of political power, cultural sophistication, and imperial authority for over eight centuries. Located on the North China Plain at the southern edge of the Inner Mongolian plateau, the city sits at a strategic nexus between the fertile central plains and the less-populated northern steppes, making it a natural node for both defense and trade. Over its long history as Dadu under the Mongol Yuan, Beijing (the "Northern Capital") under the Ming and Qing, and Peking in European transliteration, the city has accumulated layers of imperial palaces, administrative institutions, temples, and walls that embody the ideological and architectural principles of Chinese civilization. The city's transformation from a walled fortress-city of roughly one million inhabitants under the Qing to a sprawling metropolis of over twenty million in the twenty-first century reflects both the continuities and ruptures of Chinese governance and cultural identity across modernization.

## Early Settlement and Strategic Importance

The Beijing region has been inhabited for thousands of years, with evidence of human settlement dating to the Paleolithic era. However, the site did not become a major center until much later. During the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, the area was loosely controlled by various northern states, and even under the early imperial dynasties—the Qin and Han—Beijing remained peripheral compared to the great capitals of Xi'an and Luoyang to the south. The city's strategic significance grew during the Tang Dynasty, when it served as a military outpost and trading hub at the northern frontier, but it was not yet a major imperial capital.

The shift toward Beijing's prominence accelerated during the Liao Dynasty (907–1125), when the Khitan people established their southern capital at Zhongdu (literally "Middle Metropolis"), near present-day Beijing. The city's location at the intersection of the Grand Canal's northern terminus and the overland Silk Road routes made it increasingly valuable for controlling commerce and military supplies. When the Jin Dynasty (1115–1234) conquered northern China from the Khitan, they continued to develop the city as a secondary capital, further strengthening its administrative and strategic infrastructure.

## Yuan Dynasty Foundation and Mongol Urban Planning

Beijing achieved its first full status as the imperial capital of all China under the Mongol Yuan Dynasty. Kublai Khan, the founder of Yuan rule over China, shifted his principal capital south from the steppe—first to Shangdu (Xanadu) and then, from the 1260s, to a wholly new city raised beside the ruins of the former Jin capital of Zhongdu. He named it Dadu ("Great Capital") in Chinese and Khanbaliq ("the Khan's City") in Turkic, laying it out according to both Mongol and Chinese principles. The new capital's design—directed by the adviser Liu Bingzhong, with the astronomer and hydraulic engineer Guo Shoujing responsible for its waterworks—created the magnificent walled metropolis that would define Beijing for subsequent centuries. The grid layout, with palace complexes at the center and administrative wards radiating outward, reflected both Mongol organization and Chinese cosmological principles. The Yuan capital held a population approaching one million by the fourteenth century, making it one of the world's largest cities.

The city's position at the terminus of the newly reorganized Grand Canal—whose northern link to the capital, the Tonghui Canal, Guo Shoujing engineered—meant that vast quantities of southern grain, silk, and manufactured goods flowed northward to supply the Yuan court and garrison. This integration with the hydraulic and commercial infrastructure of the empire solidified Beijing's economic and political centrality. Marco Polo visited Khanbaliq in the late thirteenth century and marveled at its grandeur, recording its scale and sophistication in his travels.

## Ming Dynasty Reconstruction and the Forbidden City

When the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644) overthrew the Yuan, the new Han-Chinese rulers initially kept their capital at Nanjing in the south and reduced the former Yuan capital to a provincial city renamed Beiping ("Northern Peace"). However, the Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424) made the strategically consequential decision to move the imperial seat north, restoring the city's rank and renaming it Beijing ("Northern Capital") in 1403, then undertaking a massive reconstruction that would define its physical and symbolic character for nearly six centuries. From 1406 to 1420, the Ming court constructed the Forbidden City—the vast walled palace complex of nearly 1,000 buildings arranged within concentric zones—at the heart of Beijing. Encompassing approximately 72 hectares (180 acres), the Forbidden City contained an estimated 980 buildings with roughly 8,886 individual rooms; imperial tradition held the figure at precisely 9,999 and a half rooms—one half-room short of the perfect ten-thousand attributed to heaven's celestial palace, a deliberate gesture of cosmic humility. This architectural masterpiece embodied the imperial bureaucratic order: the outer courtyards served administrative and ceremonial functions, the inner palace housed the imperial family, and the innermost halls represented the emperor's role as the mediator between heaven and earth.

The Forbidden City's design incorporated principles of feng shui, astronomical symbolism, and hierarchical social order. The four cardinal gates, the arrangement of halls in groups of three (representing the celestial structure), and the emphasis on the north-south axis connecting the emperor to the cosmos all reflected the cosmological basis of imperial legitimacy. The surrounding city walls, rebuilt and reinforced under the Ming, enclosed an area of roughly 50 square kilometers and contained multiple layers of defense. Sixteen gates allowed controlled access; the northern gates opened toward the steppes and Mongolia, while the southern gates managed trade and pilgrim traffic from the provinces.

Beijing's economic importance grew dramatically under the Ming. Although Zheng He's great treasure fleets were organized and launched from Nanjing and the Yangtze shipyards rather than the landlocked northern capital, the transfer of the court to Beijing in 1421 made the city the political nerve center of an empire then at the height of its maritime ambition. Beijing also served as the administrative hub for the vast, newly reorganized Grand Canal system that funneled southern resources northward. Merchants, officials, and petitioners streamed into Beijing, and the city swelled to a population exceeding one million by the end of the Ming period.

## Qing Dynasty and the Height of Imperial Power

The Manchu Qing Dynasty's conquest of Ming China in 1644 did not displace Beijing from its status as capital, but rather intensified it. The Qing emperors—particularly the Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722) and the Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796)—invested massive resources in Beijing's expansion, construction, and embellishment. The Forbidden City underwent multiple renovations and expansions; the emperors built the Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan) northwest of the city walls as a retreat that blended Chinese and European architectural and garden elements, and commissioned elaborate temple complexes that integrated Tibetan Buddhism with Chinese state ideology. In 1860, during the Second Opium War, Anglo-French forces under Lord Elgin and Baron Gros burned and looted Yuanmingyuan over three days, destroying thousands of irreplaceable artworks and architectural structures—an act that shocked Chinese civilization and remains a defining symbol of Western imperial violence in national memory. The Empress Dowager Cixi subsequently built the present Yiheyuan (New Summer Palace) on Kunming Lake as a partial replacement, its construction completed in 1888 controversially using funds allocated for naval modernization.

Under Qing rule, Beijing represented the zenith of imperial Chinese civilization. The city's population reached an estimated 1.5 million by the eighteenth century, making it one of the world's largest metropolitan centers. Officials, merchants, scholars, and craftspeople from across the empire and from foreign lands converged on Beijing to seek imperial favor, trade, or employment. The imperial examination system funneled the most talented scholars to Beijing for the palace examination, ensuring that the city remained the intellectual epicenter of the realm. The Kangxi and Qianlong emperors' court gatherings, their patronage of scholars and artists, and their collection of antiquities and rare texts made Beijing the cultural and intellectual heart of East Asia.

Yet Beijing's strategic vulnerability also became apparent. The city's location on the North China Plain, while providing access to trade routes, left it exposed to incursions from the north. The eight-nation relief force that marched on Beijing in 1900 to suppress the Boxer Rebellion found the walls and gates that had protected the city for centuries inadequate against modern artillery. The foreign occupation of Beijing (1900–1901) and the subsequent Boxer Protocol indemnities symbolized China's declining ability to defend its capital against Western military pressure and foreshadowed the political collapse that would follow.

## Urban Fabric and the Hutong Network

Throughout its imperial centuries, Beijing's urban character was defined as much by its dense residential alleys—hutongs—as by its palaces and temples. Hutong, a Mongolian-derived word originally meaning "water well," came to denote the narrow lanes connecting walled courtyard homes (*siheyuan*) that spread through the residential quarters surrounding the imperial core. By the Qing period, Beijing contained more than 3,000 hutongs, forming a dense grid of domestic life woven through the city's ring of walls and canals.

The siheyuan courtyard house lining these alleys was a microcosm of Confucian social order: the main hall at the north housed the senior generation; rooms for sons and daughters occupied the east and west wings; the southernmost structure served guests or lower-status members of the household. Extended families shared a single compound, with the courtyard serving as the center of collective domestic life and seasonal ritual. The commercial markets that lined the main alley junctions—selling silk, street food, medicinal herbs, and calligraphy supplies—sustained a vibrant urban economy beneath the imperial tier.

The hutong network survived, though greatly thinned, into the twentieth century. Modernization took a heavy toll: the construction of Tiananmen Square and Soviet-style boulevards during the 1950s eliminated swaths of hutong neighborhoods south of the Forbidden City, while later economic development leveled thousands of historic alleys to build apartment blocks and commercial zones. By 2005, fewer than 1,000 hutongs remained intact; today, a handful of preserved districts—notably Nanluoguxiang, Shichahai, and Houhai—offer a compressed echo of imperial-era residential Beijing, now largely serving as heritage tourist destinations and protected by municipal ordinance.

## Twentieth Century and the Republican Interregnum

When the Xinhai Revolution overthrew the Qing Dynasty in 1911, Beijing initially retained its status as a capital under the new Republic of China. However, the republic proved fractious; warlords vied for control, and the government shifted between Beijing and other cities. Sun Yat-sen preferred Guangzhou as the center of revolutionary authority; the Beiyang (Northern) warlord governments held the city through the 1910s and 1920s; and when the Nationalist Kuomintang established unified control in 1928, they relocated the capital to Nanjing and renamed Beijing to Beiping ("Northern Peace"), diminishing its political importance.

The Japanese invasion and occupation of China (1937–1945) placed Beijing under imperial Japanese military control for eight years. The city's cultural and intellectual life was suppressed, and its resources were redirected to support the Japanese war effort. The city's population declined as scholars and activists fled or were imprisoned. Only after Japan's defeat in 1945 did Beijing regain the possibility of a larger political role.

## People's Republic and Communist Beijing

When the Communist Party of China defeated the Nationalists in the Civil War and established the People's Republic in 1949, Mao Zedong chose the city—restored to the name Beijing after two decades as Beiping under Nationalist rule—as the capital. (In English the city continued to be written "Peking" in the older postal romanization until the international adoption of Hanyu Pinyin around 1979 made "Beijing" standard.) This decision reflected both the symbolic weight of Beijing as the historical seat of imperial authority—which Mao sought to claim and transcend—and the strategic advantage of controlling the north, including the border regions with the Soviet Union and Mongolia.

Under Communist rule, Beijing underwent a radical transformation. The ancient city walls, considered remnants of imperial feudalism, were partially demolished in the 1950s-1960s to make way for broad avenues and modern administrative buildings. Tiananmen Square, originally a royal ceremonial space in front of the Forbidden City, was vastly enlarged (from roughly 11,000 to 440,000 square meters) and repurposed as a venue for mass rallies and political demonstrations. The Great Hall of the People (completed 1959) and the Museum of the Chinese Revolution (built 1959) flanked the square, symbolizing the transfer of authority from the imperial court to the people's representatives—though ultimate power remained concentrated in the Communist Party leadership.

The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) subjected Beijing's cultural heritage to systematic assault: temples were desecrated, classical libraries were burned or scattered, and Red Guards demolished many traditional structures. Yet the Communist regime also invested heavily in Beijing's expansion: new industrial zones, residential neighborhoods (danwei compounds), and administrative centers transformed the city into a sprawling capital of over ten million by the 1980s. The Forbidden City, opened as the Palace Museum in 1925 and placed among the first Major National Historical and Cultural Sites in 1961, was preserved as a museum and symbol of China's past; during the Cultural Revolution it was reportedly spared the Red Guards' assault after Zhou Enlai ordered its gates closed and placed under army guard, embodying the regime's ambivalent relationship with imperial history.

The 1989 Tiananmen Square pro-democracy protests and military crackdown cemented Beijing's role as the crucible of contemporary Chinese political struggle. The student-led movement, culminating in the June 3–4 military intervention, unfolded on the symbolic stage that Mao had created, and the suppression asserted the Communist Party's ultimate authority over the city and the nation.

## Modern Beijing

Since the 1990s, Beijing has experienced unprecedented urban expansion, modernization, and economic growth. Special economic zones and technology parks (including the Zhongguancun district, known as China's Silicon Valley) have transformed the city into a global economic center. The hosting of the 2008 Summer Olympics accelerated infrastructure development: new subways, highways, and sports facilities reshaped the urban landscape at a reported cost of approximately $43 billion, making it the most expensive Olympics to that date. The 2022 Winter Olympics—held partly in Beijing and partly in nearby Zhangjiakou—made Beijing the first city in history to host both the Summer (2008) and Winter (2022) Olympic Games, a distinction the Chinese government promoted as evidence of the country's organizational and economic ascent. Today, Beijing is a sprawling megacity of over 21 million people (2020 census), with towering skyscrapers, shopping malls, and technology campuses coexisting with ancient temples, hutong neighborhoods, and the Forbidden City.

Beijing's role as the capital of a rising superpower has restored something of the geopolitical centrality it held under the Qing, though in a globalized context. The city hosts the Central Committee of the Communist Party, the National People's Congress, the State Council, and the presidium—making it the undisputed nerve center of Chinese governance. International business, diplomatic missions, and cultural institutions cluster in Beijing, reinforcing its position as China's premier metropolis and the gateway through which the world engages with Chinese civilization.
