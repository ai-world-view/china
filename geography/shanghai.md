# Shanghai

## Summary

Shanghai, located at the mouth of the Yangtze River on the East China Sea coast, has transformed from a minor trading port into Asia's most economically dynamic metropolis and China's gateway to the world. Rising to prominence in the nineteenth century following the Opium Wars and the treaty-port system, Shanghai became the preeminent center of commerce, industry, finance, and culture in modern China, housing China's first stock exchange, hosting the founding congress of the Communist Party of China (1921), and serving as the intellectual and cultural hub of the Chinese republican and revolutionary movements. Today, as China's principal financial center and a Special Economic Zone, Shanghai symbolizes the nation's economic reform and integration into global capitalism, with a registered population of approximately 24.8 million (2022 census) and a GDP equivalent to over USD 700 billion—larger than that of Switzerland or Saudi Arabia. The city's Pudong skyline of glass-and-steel towers stands across the Huangpu River from the Bund's art-deco European facades, encapsulating Shanghai's layered identity as simultaneously colonial heritage site, communist revolutionary crucible, and twenty-first century global financial command center.

## Early History and the Treaty-Port Era

Shanghai's name—meaning "above the sea"—appears in early Song Dynasty (960–1279 CE) records as a small fishing and salt-production settlement on the Huangpu River, which flows south through modern Shanghai before joining the Yangtze estuary. The area was formalized as a county (*xian*) under the Yuan Dynasty in 1291. During the Ming and Qing dynasties, Shanghai served as a regional textile market and coastal customs station, producing cotton cloth traded up and down the coast, but remained significantly smaller than Ningbo, Quanzhou, and Guangzhou (Canton) as a maritime trading center.

### The First Opium War and Treaty of Nanking (1842)

Shanghai's transformation began with the First Opium War (1839–1842), Britain's military coercion of China to open its markets to opium imports from British India. The Treaty of Nanking (August 1842) forced China to open five treaty ports—Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai—to foreign trade and residence, cede Hong Kong to Britain, and pay an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars. Shanghai's location at the Yangtze estuary, accessible to ocean-going vessels and positioned as the gateway to the river's enormous commercial hinterland, made it immediately attractive to foreign merchants. Within a decade, Shanghai had surpassed Guangzhou as China's leading trading port.

### The Foreign Concessions

By the 1850s, distinct foreign-governed districts had crystallized around the original Qing-administered walled city:

The **British Settlement** (from 1843) on the Bund—the western bank of the Huangpu River—quickly filled with trading houses, banks, and warehouses. The British Municipal Council governed this enclave independently, enforcing English common law and maintaining its own police force, fire brigade, and court system. The **American Settlement** established in 1848 north of Suzhou Creek merged with the British area in 1863 to form the **International Settlement**, which by 1900 covered 22.59 square kilometers and was governed by the Shanghai Municipal Council with elected British, American, and Japanese representatives. The **French Concession** (from 1849) lay to the south and west of the Chinese city, governed by the French Consul-General under French law; its tree-lined streets, French cafes, and Russian émigré communities gave it a distinctly European character persisting to the present day.

These foreign-governed areas, beyond the reach of Chinese imperial law under the principle of extraterritoriality, became havens for merchants, adventurers, political exiles, and criminals alike. Chinese entrepreneurs—especially compradors (Chinese agents facilitating foreign trade) who accumulated fortunes by bridging the worlds of foreign business and Chinese commerce—built mansions in the concessions and played crucial roles in the city's economic development.

## Economic Rise and the Golden Age (1850s–1930s)

By the early twentieth century, Shanghai had become the economic center of the Chinese world. The **Shanghai Share Broker's Association**, founded in 1891 and reorganized as the Shanghai Stock Exchange in 1904, provided Asia's principal venue for equity investment, handling shares in shipping lines, utilities, and early industrial enterprises. Foreign banks—HSBC (opened 1865), the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China, the Yokohama Specie Bank, and the Deutsch-Asiatische Bank—established imposing headquarters along the Bund, financing trade, railways, and industrial ventures across China.

### Industrialization

Cotton spinning and weaving were Shanghai's largest industries. By 1913, Shanghai's cotton mills employed approximately 170,000 workers; by the 1930s, the number had grown to over 300,000 in textile factories alone. Major Chinese cotton magnates—including the Rong family, whose Shenxin mill group became China's largest textile enterprise—competed with Japanese mill operators who established factories after gaining legal rights to operate industrial enterprises in China under the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895). Silk factories, cigarette plants (British American Tobacco opened its Shanghai plant in 1902), and pharmaceutical enterprises clustered in Shanghai's industrial zones, drawing hundreds of thousands of rural migrants.

### The Bund and Architecture

The famous **Bund** (*Waitan*)—Shanghai's mile-long waterfront promenade along the Huangpu River's western bank—was built up between the 1860s and 1930s into a gallery of neoclassical and art-deco architecture that announced Shanghai's status as an Asian financial capital. The HSBC Building (1923, designed by Palmer & Turner), its dome and neoclassical columns dominating the Bund's center, was celebrated at its opening as "the finest building east of Suez." The Customs House (1927) with its prominent clock tower, the Sassoon House (now the Peace Hotel, 1929), and the Bank of China Building (1937) create the ensemble that remains Shanghai's most iconic streetscape. Beyond the Bund, Shanghai's French Concession developed tree-lined streets of art-deco residential apartments and villas that today constitute some of China's most desirable real estate.

### The Green Gang and Shanghai's Shadow Economy

Shanghai's booming economy and legal plurality fostered an underworld of extraordinary reach. The **Green Gang** (*Qing Bang*), led by Du Yuesheng (1888–1951), controlled Shanghai's opium trade, gambling dens, and labor rackets from the 1910s through the 1940s. Du Yuesheng—nicknamed "Big-Eared Du"—maintained close alliances with both foreign concession authorities and the Nationalist government, lending the Green Gang a semi-official character that allowed it to operate openly. The gang's control of the dockworkers, transport workers, and industrial labor force gave it enormous political leverage, which Chiang Kai-shek would exploit decisively in 1927.

## Intellectual and Political Hub

Shanghai's wealth and legal complexity made it a crucible of Chinese modernity. The city hosted the founding congress of the **Communist Party of China** on July 23, 1921, held in a house at 76 Xingye Road in the French Concession to evade Nationalist authorities. Thirteen delegates attended, representing approximately 53 Communist Party members nationwide. Among the founders were Chen Duxiu, the party's first general secretary, and a young Mao Zedong representing Hunan province. The founding site is today a national monument and museum.

### The May Fourth and May Thirtieth Movements

Shanghai was the center of the **May Fourth Movement** (1919), the intellectual ferment over Chinese modernization triggered by the Versailles Peace Conference's award of German concessions in Shandong Province to Japan rather than China. Shanghai students, workers, and intellectuals organized strikes and boycotts. The city's newspapers, including *Shen Bao* (founded 1872, China's longest-running newspaper) and *New Youth* (*Xin Qingnian*), published the essays and translations that disseminated Western philosophy, feminism, and Marxism to Chinese readers.

The **May Thirtieth Movement** (1925) erupted after British police at the International Settlement's Louza Police Station shot and killed thirteen Chinese demonstrators protesting the killing of a Chinese worker in a Japanese-owned mill. The shootings triggered a nationwide boycott of British goods, a general strike in Shanghai, and sympathy strikes in Guangzhou (Canton) and Hong Kong, marking a turning point in Chinese anti-imperialist sentiment and accelerating the Nationalist-Communist alliance's mobilization for the Northern Expedition.

### The White Terror (April 12, 1927)

On April 12, 1927, Nationalist leader Chiang Kai-shek, alarmed by Communist influence over the Shanghai labor movement and seeking to secure elite business backing, unleashed the Green Gang against Communist organizers and workers. In overnight raids coordinated between Nationalist troops and Du Yuesheng's gangsters, Communist Party members and labor organizers were systematically arrested and executed. Thousands were killed in Shanghai; the massacre extended to other cities and marked the beginning of Chiang Kai-shek's decade-long White Terror against the Communist Party. The survivors—including Zhou Enlai—fled underground or to the Communist base areas in rural Jiangxi. The April 12 Massacre (known in Communist historiography as the "Counter-Revolutionary Coup") ruptured the First United Front and opened the Chinese Civil War.

## Jewish Refugees and Wartime Shanghai

During the 1930s and 1940s, Shanghai became a refuge for an unlikely population: European Jews fleeing Nazi persecution. Because Shanghai's International Settlement required no entry visa for immigrants (unlike the United States, Britain, and most other potential destinations), approximately 30,000 Ashkenazi Jewish refugees—largely from Germany and Austria—arrived between 1933 and 1941, creating the largest Jewish community in Asia. This community established synagogues, schools, newspapers, and cultural organizations; the Ohel Moshe Synagogue (1927) in Hongkou district became the spiritual center of the refugee community.

After the Japanese military closed Shanghai to further Jewish immigration in August 1941, the approximately 18,000 Jewish refugees concentrated in Hongkou were further confined to a designated area ("the Restricted Sector for Stateless Refugees") from 1943 onward. Despite considerable hardship, the community survived the war largely intact—making Shanghai one of the few places on earth where Jewish refugees found protection during the Holocaust. Chiune Sugihara, the Japanese vice-consul in Kaunas, Lithuania, issued thousands of transit visas in 1940 that allowed Jewish refugees—among them the students of the Mir Yeshiva—to travel across the Soviet Union and Japan to Shanghai, while the Sephardic Jewish Baghdadi community already established in the city (notably the Sassoon and Kadoorie families) helped sustain the newcomers. The Mir Yeshiva of Poland evacuated its entire faculty and student body to Shanghai in 1941, where it continued teaching Talmud throughout the war.

## War and Japanese Occupation (1937–1945)

The **Battle of Shanghai** (August 13–November 26, 1937) was one of the largest and bloodiest battles of the Second Sino-Japanese War. Japan launched a massive assault on Shanghai's Chinese districts following an incident involving Chinese and Japanese military units; Chiang Kai-shek committed China's best-trained divisions to defend Shanghai, hoping international attention would prompt Western intervention. Three months of combat—urban warfare of extraordinary ferocity, aerial bombardment, and amphibious landings—killed an estimated 200,000 Chinese soldiers and 10,000–20,000 civilians, along with approximately 40,000 Japanese soldiers. Chinese resistance forces ultimately could not hold the city.

Under Japanese occupation (1937–1945), the International Settlement nominally remained under Western administration until Japan's simultaneous attack on Pearl Harbor and British Malaya in December 1941; thereafter, Japan seized the foreign concessions and interned Western civilians in Camps like Lunghwa. Shanghai's economy was redirected to Japanese war production, foreign businesses seized or closed, and the city's cosmopolitan character compressed under military rule.

## Communist Shanghai and Economic Reform

When People's Liberation Army forces took Shanghai in May 1949, completing the Communist victory in the Chinese Civil War, the new government faced a city simultaneously hosting China's most sophisticated capitalist economy and serving as a symbol of foreign exploitation and colonial humiliation. The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) expropriated foreign concessions and enterprises, nationalized private industry through socialist transformation campaigns (largely completed by 1956), and redirected Shanghai's industrial output and fiscal revenue toward state planning objectives. Mao Zedong's government deliberately reduced Shanghai's economic primacy—extracting large fiscal transfers to fund inland industrial development—while simultaneously viewing the city's residual capitalist culture with ideological suspicion.

The **Cultural Revolution** (1966–1976) was particularly destructive to Shanghai's intellectual and cultural traditions. The "Gang of Four"—Jiang Qing (Mao's wife), Zhang Chunqiao, Wang Hongwen, and Yao Wenyuan—were based in Shanghai and used the city as a political base for radical Maoist policies. Shanghai's educated and cosmopolitan population suffered disproportionately; universities were closed, intellectuals sent to re-education camps, and cultural institutions shuttered.

### Deng Xiaoping's Reforms and Pudong

Shanghai's transformation from socialist industrial city back toward global economic hub began with Deng Xiaoping's "Reform and Opening-up" policy after 1978. The **Shanghai Stock Exchange** reopened on December 19, 1990—a symbolic and practical restoration of market capitalism that had been suspended since 1949. In the same year, the State Council designated the **Pudong New Area** across the Huangpu River from the Bund as a Special Economic Zone, opening a rural expanse of farmland to development. Within two decades, Pudong's **Lujiazui Financial District** rose into one of the world's most dramatic skylines: the Oriental Pearl Tower (468 m, 1994), the Jin Mao Tower (421 m, 1999), the Shanghai World Financial Center (492 m, 2008), and the **Shanghai Tower** (632 m, 2015—China's tallest and the world's second-tallest building) created a cluster of supertall skyscrapers visible for miles.

The **China (Shanghai) Pilot Free Trade Zone**, established in September 2013, granted experimental free-trade rules in a 29 km² zone within Pudong, testing financial liberalization, reduced foreign investment restrictions, and customs simplification intended to inform national policy. The zone expanded multiple times, reaching over 120 km² by 2019.

## 2010 World Expo

Shanghai hosted the **2010 World Expo** from May 1 to October 31, 2010, with the theme "Better City, Better Life"—the first World Expo held in China and, at the time, the largest in the event's history. The Expo attracted 246 participating nations and international organizations and recorded 73.08 million visitors over six months, surpassing all previous Expo attendance records. The event required the demolition and relocation of entire neighborhoods in Pudong and along the Huangpu River, and generated massive infrastructure investment in Shanghai's subway system, road networks, and riverside promenades. The former Expo site was subsequently redeveloped as a cultural and innovation district.

## Global Metropolis

Today, Shanghai is a global financial and commercial center with a registered population of approximately 24.8 million (2022) and a GDP equivalent to roughly USD 740 billion (2023)—representing approximately 4 percent of China's total GDP, a concentration of economic output without parallel in any other single Chinese city.

### Financial Infrastructure

Shanghai houses the **Shanghai Stock Exchange** (SSE), China's largest equity market with over 2,000 listed companies and a combined market capitalization exceeding USD 5 trillion (2023); the **Shanghai Futures Exchange** (SHFE), China's principal commodities market; and the **China Foreign Exchange Trade System** (CFETS), which manages the yuan's daily exchange-rate fixing. The **Shanghai International Energy Exchange** (INE), established in 2018, introduced yuan-denominated crude-oil futures, China's first internationally accessible commodity futures contract—a significant step toward internationalizing the renminbi.

### Transportation

Shanghai's transportation infrastructure matches its metropolitan scale. The **Shanghai Metro** had 20 operational lines and 508 stations as of 2023, covering 831 kilometers—the world's longest metro network by route length. The **Maglev train** connecting Pudong International Airport to Longyang Road Metro station—commissioned in 2004—operates at speeds up to 431 km/h (268 mph), the world's fastest commercial train service. Shanghai's two international airports, Pudong and Hongqiao, handle a combined capacity of over 120 million passengers annually. The **Port of Shanghai** has been the world's busiest container port since 2010, handling over 47 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) annually.

### Cultural Identity

Shanghai's cultural influence within China remains substantial. The **Shanghainese dialect** (a variety of Wu Chinese, mutually unintelligible with Mandarin) persists among older residents and in some cultural contexts, though Mandarin has increasingly dominated public life since 1949. **Shanghai cuisine**—characterized by sweetness, soy-sauce complexity, and the distinctive technique of *hongshao* (red-braising)—is recognized nationwide; dishes like *xiaolongbao* (soup dumplings) and *shengjianbao* (pan-fried dumplings) have achieved global popularity. The city maintains world-class cultural institutions: the **Shanghai Museum**, housing one of the finest collections of Chinese bronzes, ceramics, and calligraphy in the world; the **Shanghai Grand Theatre** (1998); and the **Shanghai Symphony Orchestra**, founded in 1879 and one of Asia's oldest professional orchestras.

Shanghai's identity as cosmopolitan, entrepreneurial, and globally oriented remains symbolically distinct from Beijing's bureaucratic and political character, marking an enduring cultural division within Chinese civilization between the commercial dynamism of the Yangtze Delta and the administrative authority of the northern capital.
