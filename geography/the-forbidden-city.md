# The Forbidden City

**Category**: Geography  
**Key figures**: Yongle Emperor (builder and first occupant, r. 1402–1424), Kuai Xiang (lead architect), Qianlong Emperor (expanded and renovated, r. 1735–1796), Kangxi Emperor (major renovator, r. 1661–1722), Puyi (last imperial resident)

## Summary

The Forbidden City is the imperial palace complex of Beijing, China, one of the world's largest and most architecturally significant palace compounds and the residence of Chinese emperors for nearly five centuries. Built beginning in 1406 CE by the Yongle Emperor of the Ming Dynasty and completed in 1420 CE, the Forbidden City comprises nearly 1,000 buildings arranged within concentric rectangular walls on a north-south axis within the city of Beijing (itself originally called Khanbaliq under the earlier Yuan Dynasty). The compound covers 720,000 square meters (about 170 acres), making it the world's largest palace complex by floor area. The name "Forbidden City" derives from the historical prohibition against commoners and unauthorized persons entering the compound—it was literally forbidden to those outside the imperial court. The architecture synthesizes principles of Chinese cosmological philosophy, feng shui, and centuries of imperial architectural tradition, with every building, courtyard, gateway, and bridge deliberately positioned to reflect hierarchies of power, the order of the cosmos, and the emperor's role as the intermediary between heaven and earth. The Forbidden City's design influenced palace architecture throughout East Asia and remains an archetypal expression of imperial Chinese civilization. Though emperors ceased living in the Forbidden City after the fall of the Qing Dynasty in 1912, the complex survived the Chinese Civil War and was converted into a museum in 1925. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 and remains the world's most visited museum, attracting millions of visitors annually.

## Location, Scale, and Layout

The Forbidden City occupies the center of Beijing, positioned on the meridian line that divides the city into east and west halves. The compound is rectangular, measuring approximately 961 meters north to south and 753 meters east to west. It is surrounded by a wall that stands 10.4 meters high and measures 7.9 meters thick at the base, and a 52-meter-wide moat whose total perimeter runs approximately 3,800 meters (in Ming times, this moat held water and served defensive and water-management functions). Four gates pierce the outer wall—the Meridian Gate (*Wumen*) on the south, the Gate of Spiritual Might (*Shenwumen*) on the north, and symmetrical gates on the east and west—each serving ceremonial and administrative purposes. The total enclosed area of 720,000 square meters contains 980 surviving buildings, with a total floor area exceeding 150,000 square meters.

Inside the outer wall, the palace is divided into two major sections: the Outer Court (*waichao*) in the southern two-thirds, which contained administrative buildings where the emperor conducted state business and held audiences, and the Inner Court (*neiting*) in the northern third, which served as the emperor's private residence, the imperial harem, and the domestic quarters for imperial family members and senior concubines.

The entire complex is organized on a precise north-south axis called the Central Axis or Dragon Throne line, reflecting cosmological principles in which the north represents the supreme center of power and authority in the universe. Major buildings and courtyards are arranged symmetrically on either side of this central axis—a layout expressing the principle of cosmic order reflected in imperial governance.

## Architectural Principles and Symbolism

The Forbidden City's architecture embodies Chinese cosmological and philosophical principles accumulated over centuries. The use of the number nine is pervasive: there are said to be 9,999 rooms (though the actual count is lower, closer to 8,700 when internal partitions are counted as separate rooms), the gates have nine rows of door nails, and the courtyard arrangements involve nine modules. In Chinese philosophy, nine was considered the number of the emperor and the maximum number of yang (positive, celestial force), symbolizing imperial supremacy. The roof tiles are glazed yellow, the color reserved for the emperor, distinguishing imperial buildings from those of officials and commoners, which used green, red, or other colors. Ridge ornaments (*chiwen*) shaped like mythological creatures—dragons, phoenixes, lions—protect against evil spirits and symbolically connect the building to celestial powers.

The color palette is carefully restricted: the outer walls are red (the color of good fortune and the south, symbolizing the throne), the roofs are glazed yellow tile, the columns are vermillion-painted wood, and the interior ceilings contain elaborate patterns in gold, red, and blue. These colors, combined with the architectural forms, create a comprehensive visual system encoding the hierarchies and sacred principles of imperial authority.

The gates and passages demonstrate a deliberate progression of increasing sanctity and restricted access. The Meridian Gate, the main southern entrance, is the most public space where imperial proclamations were read. Behind it lies the Outer Court with the Three Great Halls (*san da dian*)—the Hall of Supreme Harmony (*Taihe Dian*), the Hall of Central Harmony (*Zhonghe Dian*), and the Hall of Preserving Harmony (*Baohe Dian*)—progressively more restricted to state officials and where the emperor's most important ceremonies and audiences took place. Further north, the gate of Heavenly Purity (*Qianqing Men*) marks the boundary between state and private spaces. Beyond this gateway lies the Inner Court with the residential palaces, harem quarters, and gardens accessible only to the imperial family and authorized attendants.

Water management was integral to the design. The moat served defensive, hygienic, and aesthetic functions. Within the complex, artificial streams and ponds reflected principles of feng shui (geomancy) and provided cooling and water for the structures. The courtyards' gradient slopes allowed proper drainage, and wells distributed throughout the complex provided fresh water.

## Construction and the Yongle Emperor's Vision

The Forbidden City was built under the Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424 CE), a son of the Hongwu Emperor who founded the Ming Dynasty. The Yongle Emperor decided to move the imperial capital from Nanjing in the south to Beijing in the north, closer to the frontier with the Mongols. This relocation required constructing not only the imperial palace but also administrative buildings, military facilities, and the entire infrastructure of a capital city. The imperial court selected the site based on feng shui principles and its position on the meridian line that the Yongle Emperor's advisors identified as cosmically auspicious.

Construction began in 1406 CE and involved hundreds of thousands of workers—estimates range from 100,000 to 1 million laborers drawn from across the empire through corvée conscription. Architects, engineers, and master craftsmen were similarly conscripted from the provinces. A master carpenter and builder from Suzhou, Kuai Xiang (蒯祥, 1398–1481), is traditionally credited with directing the construction of its principal halls and rose to become a senior imperial construction official under several subsequent emperors. The project consumed enormous resources: nanmu (an exceptionally durable hardwood) was logged from forests in Yunnan and Sichuan provinces; massive marble slabs for the ceremonial platforms were transported from quarries near Beijing on sledges and rollers, with the largest single piece—the carved ramp behind the Hall of Preserving Harmony—measuring 16.57 meters long and 3.07 meters wide and weighing approximately 200 metric tons. Glazed tiles were fired in specialized kilns in Linqing (Shandong Province) and Suzhou, transported by canal and river to Beijing. The imperial treasury financed the effort over fourteen years, diverting resources that some historians argue contributed to the later Ming Dynasty's fiscal difficulties.

The Yongle Emperor intended the Forbidden City to manifest his understanding of cosmic order and his role as the Son of Heaven—the mediator between the celestial realm and human affairs. Every architectural choice—the placement of buildings, the symbolism of numbers, colors, and mythological creatures, the carefully graduated system of gateways and courtyards—was designed to encode the philosophy of imperial governance drawn from Confucian principles of hierarchy and ritual propriety (see [Confucius](../people/confucius.md)). The emperor, alone among mortals, could pass freely through all courtyards and chambers; all others had prescribed spaces reflecting their rank and function.

## The Three Great Halls of the Outer Court

The Hall of Supreme Harmony (*Taihe Dian*) is the Forbidden City's most iconic structure and the largest surviving wooden building in China, standing 35.05 meters tall on a three-tiered marble terrace (*danbi*). This hall, 64 meters wide and 37.2 meters deep, rests on a terrace of white Fangshan marble that raises it approximately 8 meters above the courtyard floor. The hall where the emperor conducted the most important state ceremonies—imperial enthronement, the celebration of the lunar new year, the reception of tributary missions, the announcement of major imperial decrees, and imperial weddings—was the symbolic center of the empire. The throne platform within the hall, set beneath an elaborate coffered ceiling with a carved dragon descending from the center, raises the emperor above courtiers and officials. The ceiling's central coffer contains a carved wooden *yuqiu* (jade ball), believed in later legend to be able to detect a false emperor by falling from its mount. Twelve massive gilded columns of nanmu wood, each 12.7 meters tall, support the roof structure.

The Hall of Central Harmony (*Zhonghe Dian*), a square pavilion behind Taihe Dian, served as the emperor's preparation chamber before state ceremonies and as a space for receiving congratulations from high officials. Here the emperor also reviewed the seeding tools and almanac tables used in the annual spring plowing ceremony, symbolically inaugurating the agricultural year as the empire's supreme farmer.

The Hall of Preserving Harmony (*Baohe Dian*) was used for banquets honoring tributary rulers and for the final stage of the imperial examinations' most prestigious category (*jinshi* examination), where the emperor personally met with successful candidates. Behind this hall descends the Outer Marble Ramp (*danbi shidiao*), the largest surviving single-piece marble carving in China, measuring 16.57 meters long and weighing approximately 200 metric tons. Its surface is carved in high relief with dragons, clouds, and waves, and the emperor's sedan chair was carried down it so that his feet never touched common ground.

The careful arrangement of these three halls—forming a sequence of increasing sanctity and restricted access—demonstrated that the most important imperial ceremonies happened in carefully controlled, progressively more restricted spaces, restricting entry based on rank and function.

## The Inner Court and Imperial Residences

Beyond the Gate of Heavenly Purity, the Forbidden City transitions from public ceremonial space to private imperial residence. The Palace of Heavenly Purity (*Qianqing Gong*) served as the emperor's primary bedroom and study, where he slept and conducted private business. The Palace of Earthly Tranquility (*Kunning Gong*) was the empress's residence, though emperors with multiple concubines often preferred the concubines' quarters in the Six Western and Six Eastern Palaces (*Liu Gong* and *Liu Dong*), elaborate sets of courtyard compounds housing the imperial consorts and their attendants.

These interior palace quarters contained not only sleeping chambers but also administrative offices, libraries, workshops where artisans produced luxury items for the imperial family, temples for Daoist and Buddhist worship, theaters for private performances, and gardens where the imperial family could walk in relative privacy. The Qianlong Emperor, who reigned during the 18th century, added elaborate private gardens to the western section, including the Garden of Peaceful Old Age (*Ningxie Huayuan*), where he planned to retreat in his later years. These gardens feature carefully composed views, artificial rockwork, pavilions, and inscribed stones, embodying principles of literati landscape gardening.

## Fire, Restoration, and the Qing Succession

Though the basic layout remained constant from its completion in 1420 CE, the Forbidden City's wooden structures proved vulnerable to fire throughout its history. Major conflagrations struck repeatedly: fires in 1420, 1557, and 1597 destroyed significant portions of the Outer Court, requiring reconstruction. The Hall of Supreme Harmony burned down in 1557 shortly after completion of an earlier restoration and again in 1597; the present structure dates from its reconstruction completed in 1695 during the Kangxi Emperor's reign. The Hall of Preserving Harmony was rebuilt multiple times. In total, the Forbidden City underwent reconstruction or major restoration at least eight times between 1420 and 1900, with each major restoration representing an enormous drain on imperial resources.

Over 24 emperors—14 of the Ming Dynasty and 10 of the Qing Dynasty—lived and governed from the Forbidden City during its nearly five centuries of imperial use, from the Yongle Emperor's residence in 1420 to the abdication of the Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) in 1912. The Qing rulers, though Manchus rather than Han Chinese, adopted the Forbidden City without fundamentally altering its layout, demonstrating the complex's adaptability to different ruling cultures while maintaining its symbolic meaning.

The scale of maintenance was enormous: teams of carpenters, painters, tile specialists, and gardeners worked continuously to preserve the buildings against weather, termite infestation, and the sheer wear of centuries. The use of vermillion paint required constant reapplication; the wooden structures required regular inspection and timber replacement. The Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722) sponsored major restorations after the devastating fires of the late Ming period, re-establishing the ceremonial architecture largely in its present form. The Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796) carried out some of the most extensive aesthetic renovations, adding inscribed tablet collections, new pavilions, and private garden spaces in the western quarters that reflected his personal tastes as a calligrapher and poet.

## The Fall of the Empire and the Palace Museum

With the fall of the Qing Dynasty in 1912, Puyi—the Xuantong Emperor, then six years old—abdicated the throne, and the Forbidden City entered a complicated transitional period. Under the Articles of Favorable Treatment (1912), Puyi was permitted to continue living in the Inner Court with his household and extensive staff, while the Outer Court was opened to limited public access. This arrangement persisted until November 1924, when the warlord Feng Yuxiang expelled Puyi from the Forbidden City entirely. The following year, on October 10, 1925 (the fourteenth anniversary of the Republic), the Palace Museum (*Gugong Bowuyuan*) was formally established and the complex opened to the public.

During the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945), the museum's most important artifacts—approximately 13,000 crates of objects—were evacuated south in 1933 and eventually transported to Taiwan, where they now form the core collection of the National Palace Museum in Taipei. The objects that remained in Beijing were subsequently supplemented by acquisitions under the People's Republic, established in 1949.

Today, the Palace Museum holds a collection of approximately 1.86 million artifacts, including paintings, calligraphy, ceramics, jade carvings, bronze vessels, lacquerwork, clocks, and ritual objects spanning over 5,000 years of Chinese civilization. The museum received approximately 19 million visitors in 2019 (the year before pandemic-related closures), making it the most visited museum in the world—more than four times the annual attendance of the Louvre. To protect the buildings from overcrowding, visitor numbers are capped at 80,000 per day, with timed-entry ticketing. The complex required significant modifications for public access—adding ramps for accessibility, creating designated circulation pathways, installing multilingual information placards—but the fundamental architecture remains intact. The Forbidden City was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 under the title "Imperial Palace of the Ming and Qing Dynasties in Beijing and Shenyang."

## Architectural Legacy

The Forbidden City influenced palace architecture throughout East Asia. The royal palaces of Korea, Vietnam, and the Ryukyu Islands incorporated design principles derived from the Forbidden City or deliberately modeled themselves on Ming and Qing imperial architecture. The Japanese Imperial Palace in Tokyo includes elements inspired by Chinese imperial palace architecture, including the use of red walls and axially organized courtyards, though the overall design differs significantly.

The Forbidden City also became a symbol in global architecture and art of the aesthetic and philosophical sophistication of pre-modern China. 20th-century Chinese architects and planners, even those influenced by Western modernism, frequently cited the Forbidden City's integration of natural and built environment, its sophisticated use of color and symbolism, and its human-scaled courtyards as models for thoughtful urban design. The complex's layering of public ceremonial spaces and private domestic quarters influenced thinking about how architecture can encode and reinforce social hierarchies and cosmological meaning.
