## Notable Facts about the country china

| Topic | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| [The Great Wall of China](geography/the-great-wall.md) | A series of fortifications spanning over 13,000 miles across northern China, built over millennia to defend against nomadic invasions and regulate trade, symbolizing Chinese civilization's engineering prowess and historical resilience. |
| [The Forbidden City](geography/the-forbidden-city.md) | The imperial palace complex of Beijing built from 1406–1420 CE by the Ming Dynasty, comprising nearly 1,000 buildings arranged within concentric walls, serving as the residence of Chinese emperors for nearly five centuries and representing an archetypal expression of imperial Chinese civilization. |
| [The Communist Party of China](government-politics/communist-party-china.md) | The founding and ruling political party since 1949, established as the sole governing force that transformed China from a fragmented state into a unified nation and global superpower through revolutionary struggle and economic reform. |
| [Confucianism](culture-society/confucianism.md) | An ancient philosophical system emphasizing morality, virtue, and social harmony through hierarchical relationships, which shaped Chinese governance, education, and values for nearly 2,000 years and remains foundational to Chinese cultural identity. |
| [Filial Piety (Xiao)](culture-society/filial-piety.md) | The cardinal Confucian virtue of respect, gratitude, and duty toward parents and ancestors; foundational to Chinese ethics, family structure, and governance for over two millennia, making it perhaps the most enduring principle in Chinese civilization. |
| [The Qin Dynasty](history/the-qin-dynasty.md) | China's first centralized empire (221–206 BCE), which unified the warring states under Qin Shi Huang, standardized writing, weights, measures, and currency, and forged the bureaucratic imperial model that governed China for over two millennia. |
| [The Tang Dynasty](history/the-tang-dynasty.md) | One of China's most prosperous and culturally golden ages (618–907 CE), known for flourishing in poetry, painting, cosmopolitan trade, the perfection of the civil service examination system, and influence throughout East and Central Asia. |
| [Economic Reform & Opening-up](economy/economic-reform-opening-up.md) | Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 reforms that transformed China from a centrally planned socialist economy into a market-oriented global power, lifting over 800 million people out of poverty. |
| [Mao Zedong](people/mao-zedong.md) | Founding leader of the People's Republic of China (1949–1976), a Marxist revolutionary whose unification of the country and radical campaigns—the Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution—profoundly and traumatically shaped modern China. |
| [Confucius](people/confucius.md) | Ancient philosopher (551–479 BCE) whose emphasis on virtue, moral exemplarity, and benevolent rulership founded Confucianism, the most influential philosophical system in Chinese history, which shaped governance, education, and ethics across East Asia for over two millennia. |
| [Deng Xiaoping](people/deng-xiaoping.md) | Paramount leader (1978–1992) and architect of Reform and Opening-up, whose pragmatic market reforms lifted an estimated 800 million people from poverty and made China the world's second-largest economy, while his rule also encompassed the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown. |
| [The Ming Dynasty](history/the-ming-dynasty.md) | A powerful, culturally prolific Han-Chinese dynasty (1368–1644 CE) that built the Forbidden City and the iconic Great Wall, sponsored Zheng He's Indian Ocean voyages, and produced celebrated porcelain and literature before turning inward and falling to the Qing. |
| [Taoism](culture-society/taoism.md) | A Chinese philosophical and religious tradition rooted in the Tao Te Ching and Zhuangzi, teaching wu wei and harmony with the Tao; the inward-turning, naturalistic complement to Confucianism that has shaped Chinese art, medicine, and thought for over two millennia. |
| [The Han Dynasty](history/the-han-dynasty.md) | The second major imperial dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) that unified China after the Qin's collapse, established Confucian governance, pioneered the Silk Road trade networks, and created a template for centralized imperial rule that persisted for nearly 2,000 years. |
| [The Silk Road](geography/the-silk-road.md) | A vast network of land and maritime trade and cultural exchange routes connecting China to Central Asia, India, Persia, and Rome from the 2nd century BCE onward, transmitting goods, technologies, religions including Buddhism, and ideas across Eurasia. |
| [Xi Jinping](people/xi-jinping.md) | Paramount leader of China since 2012 (General Secretary, President, Military Commission Chairman), who has consolidated unprecedented personal power, initiated the Belt and Road Initiative, reasserted Communist Party ideology, and positioned China as a superpower rival to the United States. |
| [The Qing Dynasty](history/the-qing-dynasty.md) | The last imperial dynasty (1644–1912 CE), established by the Jurchen Manchus, which expanded China to its greatest territorial extent, presided over a cultural and economic golden age, yet ultimately proved unable to adapt to Western military pressure, leading to its collapse and the birth of the modern Chinese republic. |
| [Sun Yat-sen](people/sun-yat-sen.md) | Revolutionary leader and founder of the Republic of China (1866–1925), who orchestrated the 1911 Xinhai Revolution that overthrew the Qing Dynasty and articulated the Three Principles of the People—nationalism, democracy, and social welfare—which shaped modern Chinese political thought. |
| [Buddhism in China](culture-society/buddhism-in-china.md) | A major philosophical and religious tradition that entered China via the Silk Road during the Han Dynasty and synthesized with Confucianism and Taoism over two millennia, profoundly shaping Chinese thought, art, literature, medicine, and daily spiritual practice across all social classes. |
| [Qin Shi Huang](people/qin-shi-huang.md) | Founder and first emperor of unified China (221–206 BCE), who conquered the warring states and established the centralized bureaucratic state model that defined Chinese governance for nearly two millennia, and whose mausoleum contains the famous Terracotta Army. |
| [Chinese Civil War](government-politics/chinese-civil-war.md) | Armed conflict (1927–1950) between the Nationalist Kuomintang and Communist Party of China for control of mainland China, ending with Communist victory in 1949 and the establishment of the People's Republic under Mao Zedong, while the defeated Nationalists retreated to Taiwan. |
| [Belt and Road Initiative](economy/belt-and-road-initiative.md) | China's flagship global infrastructure and economic integration program launched in 2013 by Xi Jinping, spanning over 140 countries across Asia, Africa, Europe, and Oceania through massive investments in ports, railways, roads, and energy infrastructure to integrate China's economy with regional and global markets. |
| [Zhou Enlai](people/zhou-enlai.md) | Paramount twentieth-century statesman and first Premier of the People's Republic (1949–1976), whose diplomatic skill and pragmatism bridged the revolutionary Mao era and presaged the reform period, orchestrating China's opening to the West and the historic 1971 U.S.-China rapprochement. |
| [Special Economic Zones](economy/special-economic-zones.md) | Designated experimental areas established beginning in 1979 in Guangdong and Fujian provinces to test market-oriented economic practices, with Shenzhen's dramatic transformation from fishing village to modern metropolis providing empirical proof that capitalist mechanisms could coexist with Communist Party control. |
| [Cultural Revolution](history/cultural-revolution.md) | A ten-year period of social and political upheaval (1966–1976) initiated by Mao Zedong to purge perceived capitalist and traditional elements, resulting in hundreds of thousands of deaths, the destruction of cultural artifacts, and widespread trauma that remains central to modern China's self-understanding. |
| [The Opium Wars](history/the-opium-wars.md) | Two armed conflicts (1839–1842 and 1856–1860) in which Britain, later joined by France, defeated Qing China over trade and the opium traffic, forcing open its ports, ceding Hong Kong, and imposing the "unequal treaty system" that opened China's "Century of Humiliation." |
| [Jiang Zemin](people/jiang-zemin.md) | Paramount leader of China (1989–2004) who rose to power in the Tiananmen crisis and presided over rapid economic growth, WTO accession, and the Hong Kong handover, pioneering the "Three Represents" theory and institutionalizing orderly, term-limited leadership succession. |
| [Hu Jintao](people/hu-jintao.md) | Paramount leader of China (2002–2012), a centrist technocrat who continued market-driven growth while promoting the "Harmonious Society" and "Scientific Outlook on Development" to address inequality and environmental strain, and upheld the collective-leadership norms his successor Xi Jinping later dismantled. |
| [Kangxi](people/kangxi.md) | The fourth Qing emperor (r. 1661–1722) and arguably the dynasty's greatest ruler, whose 61-year reign consolidated Manchu control, suppressed the Three Feudatories Rebellion, annexed Taiwan, and established the Qing at its territorial and cultural zenith through military prowess and Confucian patronage. |
| [The National People's Congress](government-politics/national-peoples-congress.md) | The chief legislative institution of the People's Republic of China and formally the highest state organ, whose theoretical supremacy is operationalized through Communist Party discipline and control, serving to ratify policies and provide institutional legitimacy for party rule. |
| [The Song Dynasty](history/the-song-dynasty.md) | A highly sophisticated imperial period (960–1279) notable for extraordinary achievements in philosophy (Neo-Confucianism), technology (printing, gunpowder, ceramics), urban commerce, and aesthetic arts, establishing models of statecraft and culture that profoundly influenced all subsequent Chinese dynasties. |
| [Chinese Medicine](culture-society/chinese-medicine.md) | An ancient and continuously transmitted healing system developed over 2,500 years, integrating philosophy, herbal pharmacology, acupuncture, and massage based on concepts of qi circulation and yin-yang balance, which shaped Chinese understanding of the body and remains a major therapeutic tradition worldwide. |
| [Zheng He](people/zheng-he.md) | Ming Dynasty Muslim eunuch admiral (1371–1435) who commanded seven state-sponsored "treasure fleet" voyages across the Indian Ocean to Arabia and the East African coast between 1405 and 1433—the apex of Chinese maritime power, before the Ming court abandoned oceanic exploration and turned inward. |
| [The Great Leap Forward](history/the-great-leap-forward.md) | Mao Zedong's utopian economic campaign (1958–1962) to industrialize China through people's communes and backyard steel furnaces, whose inflated production targets, coerced requisitions, and diverted labor produced history's deadliest famine—an estimated 30 million deaths—and discredited Maoist economics. |
| [The I Ching (Book of Changes)](culture-society/i-ching.md) | One of the oldest surviving Chinese texts, a divination manual and philosophy of change built on 64 six-line hexagrams of yin and yang; canonized as one of the Five Classics and central to the imperial examinations, it later drew Leibniz's attention for its resemblance to binary arithmetic. |
| [The Warring States Period](history/warring-states-period.md) | An era of intense interstate conflict (475–221 BCE) when pre-imperial China fragmented into competing kingdoms, generating revolutionary military theory, philosophical schools, and technological advancement before Qin Shi Huang's unification and the establishment of centralized imperial rule. |
| [The Yuan Dynasty](history/the-yuan-dynasty.md) | The Mongol empire's direct rule over China (1271–1368) established by Kublai Khan, representing a period of cultural synthesis, unprecedented Eurasian trade, and dual-system governance before the Ming restoration of Han-Chinese rule. |
| [The 1989 Tiananmen Square Pro-Democracy Movement](government-politics/tiananmen-square-1989.md) | A sustained student-led protest in Beijing (April–June 1989) demanding democratic reform and political freedom, violently suppressed by the People's Liberation Army on June 3–4, resulting in hundreds to thousands of deaths and marking a watershed moment in contemporary Chinese political history. |
| [The Tributary System](government-politics/tributary-system.md) | A hierarchical framework of diplomatic and economic relations that structured China's interactions with neighboring states for two millennia, formalizing their acknowledgment of Chinese suzerainty through rituals like the kowtow and tribute missions, reflecting the emperor's role as the center of civilization. |
| [The Sui Dynasty](history/the-sui-dynasty.md) | A short-lived but transformative dynasty (581–618 CE) that reunified fragmented China and engineered the Grand Canal, creating administrative and institutional foundations that the Tang Dynasty would elaborate into imperial China's golden age. |
| [The Boxer Rebellion](history/the-boxer-rebellion.md) | A violent anti-foreign and anti-Christian uprising (1899–1901) during the Qing's decline, exemplifying nationalist backlash against Western incursion; its suppression by a foreign coalition and the subsequent Boxer Protocol deepened China's humiliation and accelerated the dynasty's fall. |
| [The Long March](history/the-long-march.md) | The Communist Party's epic 6,000-mile military retreat (1934–1935) from the Nationalists' siege, during which Mao Zedong consolidated power and the revolutionary core survived to eventually triumph in the Civil War, creating foundational mythology for Communist rule. |
| [Legalism](culture-society/legalism.md) | An ancient Chinese philosophical school emphasizing law, meritocracy, and centralized state control over moral virtue, which emerged during the Warring States period and became state policy under the Qin Dynasty, profoundly shaping imperial governance despite the Han Dynasty's official adoption of Confucianism. |
| [Li Bai](people/li-bai.md) | Tang Dynasty poet (701–762 CE) widely regarded as one of the two greatest poets of the Tang and Chinese literature overall, who exemplified Daoist spontaneity, imaginative freedom, and emotional authenticity in verse, becoming the exemplar of the romantic poet-individual and profoundly shaping Chinese aesthetics and the ideal of the cultured literatus. |
| [The Yellow River](geography/the-yellow-river.md) | China's second-longest river, known as "the Sorrow" for its destructive power, which has shaped agricultural settlement, dynastic collapse, and Chinese philosophy since ancient times. |
| [The Standing Committee of the Politburo](government-politics/standing-committee-politburo.md) | The Communist Party's innermost decision-making body, comprising the paramount leader and senior officials, which formally embodies collective leadership but has increasingly concentrated power under Xi Jinping. |
| [State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs)](economy/state-owned-enterprises.md) | State-controlled corporations dominating China's strategic sectors—energy, telecommunications, banking—that persist from the planned economy but have been restructured under market reforms while remaining instruments of party control. |
| [The Yangtze River](geography/the-yangtze-river.md) | Asia's longest river (about 6,300 km), flowing from the Tibetan Plateau through the Three Gorges to the East China Sea; the economic artery of southern China whose basin holds over 40 percent of the population and is spanned by the world's largest hydroelectric project, the Three Gorges Dam. |
| [The Shang Dynasty](history/the-shang-dynasty.md) | China's earliest archaeologically and textually confirmed dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) and the apex of its Bronze Age, which produced the oracle-bone script ancestral to modern Chinese writing, monumental ritual bronzes, and a theocratic ancestor cult before falling to the Zhou. |
| [The Imperial Examination System](government-politics/imperial-examination-system.md) | The competitive written civil-service examination that selected imperial China's officials from the Sui and Tang until its abolition in 1905; grounded in the Confucian classics, it enabled social mobility, standardized administrative competence, and defined the scholar-official ideal for over a millennium. |
| [The Grand Canal](geography/the-grand-canal.md) | A vast system of waterways spanning roughly 1,776 kilometers and built in stages from the Sui through Ming dynasties, connecting northern China with the Yangtze River valley; engineered primarily to transport grain northward and serve as the commercial and strategic spine of imperial China for over a millennium. |
| [The Tea Trade](economy/the-tea-trade.md) | One of history's longest and most influential commodity exchanges originating in China and expanding into a global network, where Chinese tea became a staple export generating enormous revenue and shaping geopolitics, particularly triggering British demand that led to the Opium Wars and India's competitive tea cultivation. |
| [The Zhou Dynasty](history/the-zhou-dynasty.md) | China's longest-lasting dynasty (1046–256 BCE), which introduced the Mandate of Heaven doctrine to justify political succession, established feudalism with vassal states, and presided over the emergence of Confucianism and Daoism as foundational philosophical systems before fragmenting into the Warring States Period. |
| [The Xia Dynasty](history/the-xia-dynasty.md) | China's legendary first dynasty (c. 2070–1600 BCE), founded by the flood-taming culture-hero Yu the Great; occupying the border between myth and history, it established hereditary rule and the Mandate-forfeiture template of dynastic legitimacy, and is tentatively linked by many scholars to the Bronze Age Erlitou culture. |
| [Porcelain and Ceramics](culture-society/porcelain-and-ceramics.md) | China's ceramic tradition, refined over five millennia from Neolithic earthenware through Song celadon to Ming and Qing porcelain; the imperial kilns of Jingdezhen made "china" one of history's most valued exports, shaping global trade, table culture, and aesthetics until Europe finally replicated hard-paste porcelain at Meissen in 1708. |
| [The Hukou System](government-politics/hukou-system.md) | China's household-registration system, established in 1951 and formalized by the 1958 regulations; its binary urban-rural classification ties citizens to their birthplace and governs access to schooling, healthcare, and welfare—a cornerstone of state capacity that channeled resources to cities under the planned economy and, despite reform-era migration, still stratifies Chinese society. |
| [Pan Geng](history/pan-geng.md) | A Shang Dynasty king (r. c. 14th century BCE) whose relocation of the capital to Yin, near modern Anyang, ended generations of instability and founded the settlement whose oracle bones and royal tombs later confirmed the Shang archaeologically; his three speeches in the *Book of Documents* rank among the oldest surviving Chinese prose. |
| [The People's Liberation Army](government-politics/peoples-liberation-army.md) | The armed force of the Communist Party of China since 1927, evolved from a guerrilla force during the Chinese Civil War to a modern military with nuclear weapons and naval capabilities, uniquely subordinated to party control rather than formal state institutions, serving as an instrument of CPC power in military, security, economic, and ideological domains. |
| [Printing and Woodblock Printing](culture-society/printing-and-woodblock.md) | China's printing tradition, from Tang woodblock Buddhist texts and the 868 CE *Diamond Sutra* through Bi Sheng's eleventh-century movable type to the Song commercial boom and the Ming polychrome zenith; centuries ahead of Gutenberg, it standardized the classics, powered the examination system, and spread throughout East Asia. |
| [Regional Autonomy](government-politics/regional-autonomy.md) | China's system of ethnic-minority self-governance through five autonomous regions, thirty prefectures, and some 120 counties, established from 1947 and codified in the 1984 Law on Regional National Autonomy; it grants nominal cultural and legislative rights while parallel Communist Party committees retain effective control—a tension most acute in Tibet, Xinjiang, and Inner Mongolia. |
| [Mohism](culture-society/mohism.md) | The philosophical school founded by Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE) and the chief rival to Confucianism in the Warring States; its doctrine of *jian'ai* (impartial care), critique of aggressive warfare, meritocracy, and pioneering work in logic, optics, and mechanics made it one of the great unrealized alternatives in Chinese thought before its eclipse under the Han. |
| [Calligraphy](culture-society/calligraphy.md) | The art of Chinese brushwork (*shufa*), practiced continuously for over three millennia from oracle-bone and seal scripts through the regular, running, and cursive hands; inseparable from the literati ideal and moral self-cultivation, it produced canonical masterworks from Wang Xizhi's *Orchid Pavilion Preface* to the Tang and Song masters. |
| [Du Fu](people/du-fu.md) | Tang Dynasty poet (712–770 CE), the "Sage of Poetry" and "Poet-Historian," whose mastery of regulated verse and unflinching witness to the An Lushan Rebellion made him, with Li Bai, one of the two supreme figures of Chinese literature and the model of poetry as moral and historical record. |
| [The Mandate of Heaven](government-politics/the-mandate-of-heaven.md) | The foundational doctrine of political legitimacy in imperial China (*tianming*), holding that heaven grants the right to rule to the virtuous and withdraws it from the corrupt; articulated by the Zhou to justify their conquest of the Shang around 1046 BCE, it underpinned dynastic succession, sanctioned rebellion against tyranny, and shaped Chinese political thought until the 1911 Republican revolution. |
| [Beijing](geography/beijing.md) | China's capital for most of the past eight centuries and continuously since 1949; a strategic node on the North China Plain that grew from the Jin Zhongdu and Yuan Dadu into the Ming-Qing imperial city and today a megacity of over 21 million, holding the Forbidden City, Tiananmen Square, and the seat of the Communist Party and central government. |
| [Shanghai](geography/shanghai.md) | Asia's most economically dynamic metropolis and China's gateway to the world, transformed from a minor port into a global financial center by treaty-port concessions, colonial investment, and economic reform; symbol of modern China's integration into global capitalism. |
| [The An Lushan Rebellion](history/the-an-lushan-rebellion.md) | A devastating uprising (755–763 CE) led by the frontier general An Lushan that captured both Tang capitals, forced the emperor's flight and the death of Yang Guifei, and collapsed the empire's registered population; its suppression left provincial military governors permanently autonomous, marking the turning point from the Tang golden age to the dynasty's long decline. |
| [The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms](history/five-dynasties-ten-kingdoms.md) | A fractured interregnum (907–979 CE) between the Tang and Song dynasties when China fragmented into five short-lived northern dynasties and ten southern kingdoms; despite political chaos, the period witnessed rapid printing development, ceramic refinement, and eventually reunification under Song rule. |
| [Oracle Bones and Divination](culture-society/oracle-bones-divination.md) | The Shang Dynasty practice of heating animal bones and turtle shells to divine the will of ancestors and spirits, producing the earliest known Chinese writing system; oracle-bone inscriptions provide the first direct textual evidence of ancient Chinese civilization and remain fundamental to understanding early China. |
