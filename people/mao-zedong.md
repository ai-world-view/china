# Mao Zedong

**Category**: People  
**Born**: December 26, 1893, Shaoshan, Hunan province  
**Died**: September 9, 1976, Beijing  
**Key figures**: Zhou Enlai (premier and collaborator), Lin Biao (military aide and designated successor), Jiang Qing (wife and Cultural Revolution ally), Deng Xiaoping (subordinate, twice purged, eventual successor), Peng Dehuai (general, critic of Great Leap, purged)

## Summary

Mao Zedong (1893–1976) was a Chinese Marxist revolutionary, military strategist, and the founding father and paramount leader of the People's Republic of China (1949–1976). Rising to prominence as a communist organizer during China's warlord era and early republican period, Mao led the Communist Party of China through decades of civil war and Japanese invasion, ultimately unifying China under communist rule in 1949. As Chairman of the People's Republic, Mao pursued radical socialist transformation through land redistribution, industrialization, and collectivization, launching the Great Leap Forward (1958–62) and the Cultural Revolution (1966–76), campaigns whose implementation caused catastrophic famine, social disruption, and mass death. Scholarly estimates of deaths attributable to Mao's policies range from 40 to 80 million, making his era among the deadliest in Chinese and world history. Mao's ideology—a distinctive synthesis of Marxism-Leninism, peasant-based revolution, and Chinese nationalist assertiveness—differed from Soviet orthodoxy and shaped communist movements globally. His death in 1976 ended his rule, but his legacy remains contested: revered by some as a revolutionary hero who unified and modernized China, condemned by others for catastrophic policies and systematic repression. Understanding modern China requires grappling with Mao's towering influence over the nation's 20th-century trajectory, his ideological innovations, and the dramatic human costs of his rule.

## Early Life and Revolutionary Formation (1893–1935)

Mao Zedong was born on December 26, 1893, in Shaoshan, a village in Hunan province in central China. His father, Mao Yichang, was a relatively prosperous farmer who had risen from poverty to become a grain merchant; his mother, Wen Qimei, was a devout Buddhist. Mao received classical Chinese education but developed a rebellious temperament, fleeing an arranged marriage and resisting his father's authority. He was a voracious reader and developed nationalist sympathies early, inspired by China's humiliation by foreign powers in the Opium Wars, the Boxer Protocol, and the Twenty-One Demands.

In 1911, Mao participated briefly in the Xinhai Revolution that overthrew the Qing Dynasty and established the Republic of China. He served in a republican army for six months before pursuing education at Changsha First Normal School, graduating in 1918. He then traveled to Beijing, where he worked as a library assistant under Li Dazhao at Peking University and came into contact with Marxist ideas, anarchism, and key intellectuals of the early Chinese left. In 1921, Mao attended the founding First National Congress of the Communist Party of China in Shanghai (held clandestinely, partially on a boat on Nanhu Lake), which established the party with approximately 50 members.

During the 1920s, Mao worked as a communist organizer, initially within the United Front alliance with the Nationalist Party (Kuomintang). He conducted fieldwork in the Hunan countryside in 1927, producing the influential "Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan," which argued that peasants were a revolutionary force of overwhelming power—"Several hundred million peasants will rise like a mighty storm"—contradicting orthodox Marxism's focus on the urban proletariat.

### The Long March (1934–1935)

After the Nationalists broke the United Front in 1927 and conducted the "White Terror" massacring Communist Party members, surviving Communists established rural base areas (soviets) in southern China. The largest, the Jiangxi Soviet in southern Jiangxi province, became the party's main stronghold from 1931 to 1934. Under Nationalist military pressure—Chiang Kai-shek launched five successive "Encirclement Campaigns"—the Central Red Army was forced to abandon Jiangxi in October 1934 and undertake a strategic retreat.

The Long March covered approximately 9,600 kilometers (estimates vary; earlier Western accounts cited 6,000 miles) over 370 days, crossing 11 provinces, 18 mountain ranges, and 24 rivers. Of the approximately 86,000 who set out from Jiangxi, only around 7,000 to 8,000 reached Yan'an in Shaanxi. The march crossed the Luding Chain Bridge over the Dadu River (May 1935) under fire—a battle mythologized in Communist historiography—and traversed the treacherous "Great Grasslands" of the Tibetan Plateau. At the Zunyi Conference in January 1935, amid the march, Mao was elevated to leadership of the party's military commission, effectively becoming paramount leader. By the time the march ended in October 1935, Mao's authority within the party was consolidated and unrivaled.

## Civil War and the Road to Power (1945–1949)

After Japan's surrender in August 1945, Mao and Chiang Kai-shek competed for control of territory and legitimacy. Despite American support for the Nationalists—including $3 billion in aid—and an enormous numerical advantage (the Nationalist army numbered over 4 million versus the Communists' approximately 1.3 million), Chiang's government was plagued by corruption, hyperinflation (prices increased 85,000-fold between 1937 and 1949), and loss of urban support.

The civil war (1946–49) resulted in Communist victory through a combination of military strategy and political mobilization. Key campaigns included:
- **Huaihai Campaign (November 1948–January 1949)**: The largest land battle in military history by some measures, involving over one million troops on each side, resulting in the destruction of Nationalist forces in central China. Commanded by Liu Bocheng, Chen Yi, and Deng Xiaoping.
- **Pingjin Campaign (November 1948–January 1949)**: Lin Biao's forces captured Tianjin by assault and accepted Beijing's peaceful surrender.

By April 1949, People's Liberation Army forces crossed the Yangtze River and captured Nanjing. Nationalist forces fled to Taiwan by December 1949. On October 1, 1949, from the Tiananmen rostrum, Mao proclaimed the establishment of the People's Republic of China.

## Founding of the People's Republic and Early Reforms (1949–1957)

In February 1950, Mao traveled to Moscow and concluded the Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance with Stalin—his first and only foreign travel as head of state. The treaty provided $300 million in Soviet credits and technical assistance, and deployed approximately 10,000 Soviet advisers to China to assist industrial construction.

As founding chairman, Mao implemented land reform between 1950 and 1953, redistributing landlord-held land to approximately 300 million peasants. The process involved "struggle sessions" (*pidou*) in which landlords were publicly denounced, humiliated, and often executed; estimates of deaths in the land reform period range from 1 to 2 million. A parallel campaign against "counter-revolutionaries" (1950–51) executed an estimated 700,000 to 2 million "class enemies."

Early years achieved real accomplishments: hyperinflation was controlled by 1950, the economy began recovering from decades of war, mass literacy campaigns reduced illiteracy from approximately 80% to under 40% by 1960, and land reform delivered peasants property ownership for the first time in generations. Life expectancy rose from approximately 35 years in 1949 to 63 years by 1970, primarily through public health campaigns eliminating schistosomiasis, cholera, and other epidemic diseases.

### The Korean War (1950–1953)

When United Nations forces (primarily American) crossed the 38th parallel into North Korea in October 1950, approaching the Yalu River border with China, Mao decided to intervene despite opposition within the Politburo and Stalin's initial reluctance to provide air cover. The Chinese People's Volunteer Army (CPVA), commanded by Peng Dehuai, crossed the Yalu River on October 19, 1950, launching surprise attacks that drove UN forces back to the 38th parallel by early 1951. The war stalemated and ended with an armistice in July 1953, restoring the pre-war border. China suffered approximately 180,000–200,000 killed in action (Chinese government figures; Western estimates are higher), but the war established China as a military power capable of fighting the United States to a standstill.

### The Hundred Flowers Campaign and Anti-Rightist Movement (1956–1957)

In 1956, following Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" denouncing Stalin's cult of personality and responding to the Hungarian uprising, Mao encouraged open criticism of the party with the slogan "Let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend." Initially, intellectuals were hesitant; when criticism did emerge in spring 1957, it was far more pointed than Mao had anticipated—professors and journalists attacked party corruption, bureaucratism, and the Soviet model.

In June 1957, Mao abruptly reversed course, labeling the critics "poisonous weeds" and launching the Anti-Rightist Campaign. Over 550,000 people were labeled "rightists"—intellectuals, professors, party members—stripped of positions, sent to labor camps, or exiled to the countryside. The campaign silenced independent intellectual life for the next two decades and established a precedent of manufactured political conformity that would intensify during the Cultural Revolution.

## The Great Leap Forward and the Famine (1958–1962)

In 1958, convinced that revolutionary mass mobilization could overcome material constraints, Mao launched the Great Leap Forward. The policy aimed to industrialize rural areas, accelerate collectivization into massive "people's communes" averaging 5,000 households each, and boost agricultural and industrial production simultaneously through sheer mobilization.

The results were catastrophic:
- **Steel production**: Millions of peasants were mobilized to smelt iron and steel in backyard furnaces, producing mostly useless pig iron while neglecting the harvest. China claimed steel production doubled, but most output was unusable.
- **Grain falsification**: Local officials, facing political pressure and ideological enthusiasm, reported wildly inflated grain production figures. Based on false reports, the state extracted grain quotas that left insufficient food in villages.
- **Agricultural collapse**: Simultaneous mobilization for steel and massive irrigation projects denuded labor from farming; collective labor dampened individual incentive; famine set in by late 1959 and intensified through 1960 and 1961.

The resulting famine killed an estimated 15 million to 55 million people (the range reflects scholarly debate; Chinese historian Frank Dikötter's *Mao's Great Famine* (2010) estimates 45 million; Yang Jisheng's *Tombstone* estimates 36 million). The famine was worst in Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan, Anhui, and Hunan provinces.

At the Lushan Conference in July 1959, Defense Minister Peng Dehuai wrote Mao a private letter criticizing Great Leap policies as "petty bourgeois fanaticism" causing harm. Mao responded by distributing the letter to the full Politburo and labeling Peng an "anti-party element." Peng was purged, and the conference's reversal of cautious reconsideration intensified the famine's second phase (1960–61), arguably the deadliest. Critics estimate that had the campaign been halted in 1959 when Peng raised his concerns, millions of deaths could have been prevented.

By 1961, evidence of catastrophe was undeniable. Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping implemented pragmatic recovery policies—household farming was partially restored, grain imports were purchased—and the famine ended by 1962, but Mao's authority was wounded.

## The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

In 1966, Mao launched the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution (*wenhua da geming*), ostensibly aimed at purifying communist ideology and preventing capitalist restoration. In reality, it served to remove rivals (Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, who had sidelined Mao during recovery from the Great Leap) and reassert Mao's unchallenged dominance.

Mao mobilized students and young people as the **Red Guards** (*hong weibing*), who attacked party officials, intellectuals, and anything labeled "old customs, old culture, old habits, old ideas" (the "Four Olds"). Millions of Red Guards traveled to Beijing to attend mass rallies—Tiananmen Square gatherings in August–November 1966 saw 13 million Red Guards reviewed by Mao. Schools and universities were closed for years; millions of urban youth were subsequently "sent down" (*xiafang*) to rural areas for "re-education."

**The Little Red Book**: Compiled by Lin Biao as head of the People's Liberation Army, *Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung* (the "Little Red Book") was published in 1964 and became the Cultural Revolution's sacred text. Approximately 6.5 billion copies were eventually printed, making it possibly the most-printed book in human history after the Bible. Possessing and reciting it was mandatory; not carrying it was politically dangerous.

**Violence**: Red Guard factionalism generated extreme violence. Struggle sessions (*pidou*) publicly humiliated and often physically abused victims; deaths from direct Red Guard violence are estimated at 100,000 to 2 million. Millions more died in associated factional fighting, labor camp conditions, and forced marches. Liu Shaoqi, the Head of State, died in 1969 after being denied medical care while imprisoned. Deng Xiaoping was twice purged—in 1966 and again in 1976—and his son Deng Pufang was thrown from a building by Red Guards, permanently paralyzed.

**Cultural destruction**: Temples, historical sites, artworks, and libraries were vandalized or destroyed. The Confucian temple and cemetery at Qufu were targeted; the palace museum collections survived only because Zhou Enlai ordered military protection. An estimated 1 million Tibetan cultural artifacts were destroyed during the Cultural Revolution.

### The Lin Biao Incident (1971)

Lin Biao, designated Mao's successor in the 1969 party constitution, had accumulated enormous power through his control of the military and his promotion of the Mao cult. By 1970–71, Mao grew suspicious of Lin's power base. According to official Chinese accounts, Lin Biao planned an assassination attempt against Mao that failed in September 1971; Lin then attempted to flee to the Soviet Union. On September 13, 1971, his Trident aircraft crashed in the Gobi Desert near Öndörkhaan, Mongolia, killing Lin, his wife Ye Qun, and his son Lin Liguo. All nine aboard died.

The Lin Biao incident severely damaged Mao's authority—his personally designated successor had apparently turned traitor—and paradoxically made possible the gradual rehabilitation of Deng Xiaoping, who returned to politics in 1973 under Zhou Enlai's patronage.

### The Nixon Visit (1972) and Geopolitical Realignment

In February 1972, U.S. President Richard Nixon visited China in a breakthrough diplomatic event orchestrated partly by Nixon and Henry Kissinger and agreed to by Mao as a geopolitical counter to the Soviet Union. Mao met Nixon personally despite severe health problems—he had suffered a serious heart attack in 1972 and pulmonary edema by 1974 and was partly incapacitated by what may have been Lou Gehrig's disease (ALS). The visit produced the Shanghai Communiqué, which acknowledged (without accepting) China's position that there is "one China" and Taiwan is part of China, and established the framework for eventual U.S.-China diplomatic normalization in 1979.

Mao's willingness to engage the United States—ideologically framed as exploiting "contradictions" among imperialists—reflected strategic pragmatism overriding ideological consistency and influenced how later leaders, including Deng Xiaoping, would approach Western engagement.

## Ideology and Political Theory

Mao synthesized Marxism-Leninism with peasant-based revolution, nationalism, and voluntarism—the belief that revolutionary will could overcome material constraints—into a distinctive body of thought known as "Mao Zedong Thought" (*Mao Zedong Sixiang*) or Maoism. Key theoretical contributions include:

- **Peasant revolution**: Mao argued that in colonial and semi-colonial societies, peasants rather than urban workers constituted the revolutionary vanguard—a significant departure from orthodox Marxism.
- **Protracted warfare**: In *On Protracted War* (1938), Mao theorized a three-stage guerrilla warfare model: strategic defensive, strategic stalemate, and strategic offensive. This framework influenced revolutionary movements in Vietnam, Cuba, Angola, and Peru.
- **Mass line**: The concept that the party must synthesize ideas from the masses, refine them through Marxist analysis, then return them to the masses as policy—a participatory theory of party-mass relations.
- **Continuous revolution**: Mao argued that socialist transformation was an ongoing process requiring periodic upheavals to prevent capitalist restoration—the theoretical basis for the Cultural Revolution.
- **Sino-Soviet split**: By the early 1960s, Mao openly criticized Soviet leader Khrushchev as a "revisionist" betraying Marxism-Leninism through peaceful coexistence with the West and de-Stalinization. The split, formalized by 1963, divided world communist parties into pro-Moscow and pro-Beijing factions, with Maoist parties emerging in dozens of countries.

## Death and Legacy

Mao's health deteriorated rapidly from 1971 onward. He was diagnosed with a neuromuscular degenerative disease (possibly ALS), suffered multiple cardiac events, and was largely incapacitated by 1975. His final major political act was supporting the Gang of Four—Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen—against the pragmatist faction. He died on September 9, 1976, at 12:10 AM Beijing time, age 82.

Within a month of Mao's death, the Gang of Four was arrested in a coup orchestrated by Hua Guofeng with support from senior military figures. Jiang Qing was sentenced to death (commuted to life imprisonment) in 1981; she died by suicide in 1991. Deng Xiaoping, twice purged by Mao, returned to power and within two years had fundamentally reoriented China's policy trajectory toward market reform.

In June 1981, the Central Committee issued a formal assessment crediting Mao with "70% achievements and 30% errors." Major policies labeled errors: the Anti-Rightist Campaign, the Great Leap Forward, and the Cultural Revolution. The Mao cult—worshipping him as infallible—was condemned. Yet Mao's portrait continues to hang over Tiananmen Gate, his embalmed body lies in a crystal sarcophagus in the Mao Zedong Memorial Hall on Tiananmen Square, and the party's founding legitimacy remains anchored in his revolution.

Scholarly assessments vary dramatically. Supporters credit Mao with unifying China, ending feudalism and foreign domination, establishing the modern Chinese state, achieving rapid industrialization, and expanding literacy and healthcare to the countryside. Critics—including Chinese scholars who faced persecution under him—emphasize the tens of millions who died under his policies. Frank Dikötter's trilogy (*Mao's Great Famine*, *The Tragedy of Liberation*, *The Cultural Revolution*) documents systematic policy failure and deliberate cruelty. Philip Short's more sympathetic *Mao: A Life* (2000) contextualizes Mao within a century of Chinese crisis. Outside China, Mao is often ranked alongside Stalin in discussions of 20th-century political violence, though scholars debate comparability and emphasize the complexity of revolutionary ambition mixed with catastrophic judgment.

## Significance for Modern China

Mao's 27-year rule (1949–76) fundamentally shaped modern China. He ended China's "century of humiliation" (1842–1949), unified the country under a capable state, and asserted Chinese independence against both Western powers and Soviet domination. He also attempted total social transformation through radical revolution—collectivization, Great Leap Forward, Cultural Revolution—driven by ideology, voluntarism, and ruthless political will. The resulting human toll was catastrophic, yet the party structure he built survived and adapted, with successors pragmatically abandoning Maoist methods while preserving the state infrastructure.

Deng Xiaoping's reforms—the Economic Reform & Opening-up launched in 1978—were possible only because they came after Mao, offering a corrective to Maoist radicalism while maintaining Communist Party rule. The party's legitimacy rests on Mao's revolutionary victory and nation-building, yet its current performance is measured against the pragmatism that rejected Maoism. This tension—the need to honor Mao while repudiating his policies—shapes Chinese political discourse to the present. Xi Jinping has invoked Mao-era rhetoric of ideological struggle and national unity while pursuing economic modernization, demonstrating Mao's enduring symbolic power even as his specific policies remain officially discredited.
