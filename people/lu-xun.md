## Summary

Lu Xun (1881–1936), born Zhou Shuren, was the paramount intellectual and literary figure of twentieth-century China, whose piercing critique of Chinese traditions, satirical stories, and unflinching essays shaped the cultural consciousness of the Chinese left through the Republican era and the Communist revolution. Trained as a physician in Japan, Lu Xun abandoned medicine for literature, declaring that "the nation's spirit was sick" and required the "cure" of cultural criticism and artistic awakening. His pioneering vernacular short stories (*"A Madman's Diary"* and *"The True Story of Ah Q"*) dissected Chinese fatalism, servility, and "cannibalism" (the devouring of the weak by tradition and authority), while his essays and journals became rallying cries for the May Fourth cultural movement, the leftist intellectuals of the 1920s, and eventually the Communist Party itself. Lu Xun's uncompromising moral vision, his synthesis of Western modernism and Chinese folk tradition, and his refusal to be co-opted by any faction made him the conscience of modern Chinese letters and an enduring symbol of artistic integrity under political pressure.

## Early Life, Medical Studies, and Disillusionment (1881–1906)

Lu Xun was born **Zhou Shuren** (周树人) on September 25, 1881, in Shaoxing (绍兴), a town in Zhejiang province steeped in literati culture, opera tradition, and classical scholarship. His father Zhou Boyi was a classically educated gentleman who never passed beyond the lowest examination degree, and his mother Lu Rui was largely self-taught and a formative influence. Young Zhou Shuren received a rigorous classical education: the Four Books (*Analects*, *Mencius*, *Great Learning*, *Doctrine of the Mean*) and Five Classics, read under the rod in the formal manner of late Qing schooling. However, his father's prolonged illness (from approximately 1893)—treated by traditional doctors whose prescriptions bankrupted the family—and eventual death in 1896 exposed young Lu Xun to both grinding poverty and the hypocrisies of gentry respectability: relatives who offered condolences and then departed; creditors who pressed demands at the funeral; and doctors who prescribed expensive rare ingredients that made no difference.

This childhood experience—traditional medicine unable to save his father while draining his family—directly shaped his decision, a decade later, to study Western medicine. It also planted a deep skepticism of tradition's claims versus its consequences, a theme that runs through all his major work. His paternal grandfather Zhou Fuqing, a Hanlin academician imprisoned in 1893 for attempting to bribe examination officials, plunged the family into disgrace and added another layer of disillusionment with the Confucian establishment.

In 1898, aged seventeen, Lu Xun enrolled at the Jiangnan Naval Academy (南京) and later the Mining and Railway School—institutions that taught Western science alongside classical learning. In 1902, at twenty-one, he won a government scholarship to study in Japan, part of a wave of approximately 13,000 Chinese students studying in Japan by 1905, seeking to understand the secrets of Meiji Japan's rapid modernization. Initially enrolled in Japanese language preparation programs at the Tokyo Kobun Institute, he shifted to **Sendai Medical College** (仙台医学专门学校) in 1904, one of the first Chinese students admitted there.

In Sendai, Lu Xun was academically competent—his first-year examination score of 59.3/100 in anatomy sparked a famous controversy when his teacher Fujino Genkuro (藤野严九郎) was accused of raising his grade; the accusation, later shown to be false, burned in Lu Xun's memory as a microcosm of the contempt Chinese students faced. A pivotal incident crystallized a deeper crisis: while watching a **lantern-slide presentation** of images from the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) being shown in a physiology class, Lu Xun saw slides depicting Chinese civilians either assisting Russian intelligence or watching, apparently unmoved, as a Chinese "spy" was beheaded by Japanese soldiers while surrounding Chinese onlookers smiled and applauded. The image—real or apocryphal in its details—became for Lu Xun a shattering revelation: China's problem was not physical illness but **spiritual and cultural sickness**. A healthy body administered by modern medicine would do nothing to cure the passivity, servility, and complicity he saw in those watching faces. He abandoned his medical studies in 1906 and committed himself to literature and cultural criticism as the instrument of national awakening—a decision narrated in the famous preface to his first story collection *Call to Arms* (1923).

## Early Writing and the Birth of Modern Chinese Literature (1906–1923)

Returning to China in 1909, Lu Xun taught at the Zhejiang Normal School in Hangzhou and later at the Shaoxing Middle School, while beginning to write. In 1912, following the Xinhai Revolution (1911) that overthrew the Qing dynasty, he moved to Beijing and took a position in the Ministry of Education under Cai Yuanpei—a sinecure that left him time to read, translate, and brood. His early essays in journals like *Henan Monthly* attacked Confucian ethics and pleaded for a new culture; they attracted little attention.

More consequential were his translation projects. From 1909 onward, Lu Xun and his younger brother Zhou Zuoren (周作人)—himself a major essayist and translator—worked together to translate Eastern European and Russian literature, producing *Tales from Abroad* (*Yuwai Xiaoshuo Ji*, 1909), which introduced Chekhov, Andreyev, Maupassant, and Polish authors to Chinese readers. This translation work introduced Lu Xun to Gogol's "Diary of a Madman"—a direct inspiration for his own *"A Madman's Diary."*

Lu Xun's breakthrough came in **May 1918**. Under the pseudonym "Lu Xun" (鲁迅—the surname taken from his mother Lu Rui, the given element *xun* connoting swiftness), he published *"A Madman's Diary"* (*Kuangren Riji*, 狂人日记) in *New Youth* (*Xin Qingnian*, 新青年)—the journal founded in 1915 by Chen Duxiu that had become the intellectual epicenter of cultural reform. The story, written almost entirely in vernacular Chinese (*baihua*, 白话) rather than classical *wenyan*, depicted a narrator who has gone "mad"—that is, who has seen clearly—and realized that Chinese history, ethics, literature, and social relations constitute a system of **"eating people"** (吃人): the devouring of the weak, the individual, and the young by tradition, hierarchy, and the parasitic authority of the old. The story's final cry, "Save the children!" (救救孩子), became a rallying slogan for the youth movement.

The story's influence was immediate and enormous. It established Lu Xun as a major writer, validated *baihua* as a medium for serious artistic and philosophical expression, and framed the cultural problem in moral rather than merely political terms. The May Fourth Movement of 1919—the mass student protests against the Versailles treaty's failure to return Shandong from Japan to China, and more broadly against the "old culture" of Confucian tradition—drew on Lu Xun's framing of the conflict between the individual and tradition, consciousness and servility.

Further major stories followed in rapid succession:

- **"Kong Yiji"** (孔乙己, 1919): a failed Confucian scholar addicted to wine and self-deception, unable to survive in a modern world that has no use for his classical learning, ending in implied death.
- **"Medicine"** (药, *Yao*, 1919): a story of superstition and passivity in which a dying boy is treated with a mantou (steamed bun) soaked in the blood of an executed revolutionary—a double indictment of traditional medicine and the masses' failure to understand the sacrifices made on their behalf.
- **"My Old Home"** (*Gu Xiang*, 故乡, 1921): a lyrical meditation on estrangement and class distance through the narrator's return to his childhood village and his altered relationship with his childhood friend Runtu, now a servile peasant broken by poverty and hierarchy.
- **"The True Story of Ah Q"** (*Ah Q Zhengzhuan*, 阿Q正传, 1921–1922): Lu Xun's longest story and his most celebrated allegory—a novella serialized in the *Beijing Morning Post* depicting Ah Q, a landless laborer in a provincial town whose defining psychological trait is "spiritual victory" (*jingshen shengli fa*, 精神胜利法): an ability to reframe any defeat or humiliation as a moral triumph. Ah Q drifts into the 1911 Revolution hoping for loot; is executed by the new authorities on vague charges; and dies baffled by what is happening to him. The story is a savage allegory of Chinese passivity, false consciousness, and the revolution's failure to change the underlying social structure.
- **"Benediction"** (*Zhufu*, 祝福, 1924): a story of Xianglin's Wife, a peasant woman whose entire life is destroyed by superstition, poverty, and the cruelties embedded in folk religion and Confucian family structure.

These stories, collected in **Call to Arms** (*Nahan*, 呐喊, 1923) and **Wandering** (*Panghuang*, 彷徨, 1926), established a new literary tradition that combined Dostoyevskian psychological depth, Gogolian satire, Ibsenite social critique, Chinese vernacular vigor, and folk-tale compression into a powerfully original form. By the mid-1920s Lu Xun was teaching at Peking University and Beijing Normal University, influencing a generation of writers including Ding Ling, Xiao Hong, and Ba Jin.

## The Intellectual and the Revolution (1919–1927)

Lu Xun was not a political radical in the early May Fourth years (1919–1921); he was an apostle of cultural awakening and individual enlightenment. He championed science, foreign literature (especially Ibsen, Dostoevsky, and Russian revolutionaries), and women's emancipation. But he was also deeply pessimistic about the Chinese masses' capacity for self-transformation. His essays from the early 1920s oscillate between calls for spiritual revolution and expressions of despair at the masses' "numbness" and the old gentry's continued hold on power.

The turning point came in the mid-1920s. The 1924 campaign against opium and superstition, in which Lu Xun actively participated, was brutally suppressed by warlord authorities. More significantly, the 1927 Shanghai Massacre, when Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist forces turned on the Communist-allied labor movement and slaughtered thousands of workers and activists, shattered Lu Xun's faith in reformism and bourgeois revolution. Prominent leftist intellectuals were imprisoned and executed; Lu Xun, himself briefly in danger, underwent a radicalization.

From 1927 onward, Lu Xun's writing and politics moved decisively leftward, though always retaining his fierce independence. He became a founding figure of the League of Left-Wing Writers (founded 1930), a Communist-affiliated literary organization, but never joined the Communist Party itself—a choice born of his distrust of organizational discipline and his commitment to intellectual autonomy. He wrote dozens of polemical essays attacking both reactionary nationalism and revolutionary dogmatism, criticizing both the warlords and the Communists when he felt they betrayed the people or silenced dissent.

## Essays, Polemics, and the Critic's Uncompromising Vision (1927–1936)

From 1927 until his death in 1936, Lu Xun's primary output was essays and short articles, published in journals and newspapers under various pseudonyms to evade censorship. These essays—collected in volumes like *Hot Wind* (*Refeng*, 1925), *The Grave* (*Fen*, 1927), and the many *zawen* (miscellaneous-essay) collections of his Shanghai years—constitute a running commentary on contemporary politics, culture, and the intellectual's moral duty in a revolutionary age. They are often fragmentary, satirical, sometimes obscure to modern readers; yet they established Lu Xun as the supreme essayist of his era.

In these essays, Lu Xun:

1. **Defended creative freedom** against both state censorship and party discipline. When Communist organizations tried to subordinate writers to propaganda ends, Lu Xun resisted, insisting that the artist must remain independent to effectively critique power and illuminate truth.

2. **Attacked Chinese xenophobia and reactionary nationalism**, which he saw as a disguise for the old gentry's defense of hierarchy and oppression. Conversely, he was skeptical of blind Westernism and insisted on a critical assimilation of foreign thought.

3. **Dissected the genealogy of Chinese oppression**, tracing it not primarily to foreign imperialism but to China's own traditions of hierarchical ethics, filial piety, and the subordination of the individual to cosmic-moral order. He refused simplistic anti-imperialist narratives that ignored domestic exploitation.

4. **Championed the revolutionary impulse while warning against revolutionary dogmatism**. He believed revolution was necessary but feared it would be betrayed by the Communist bureaucracy or corrupted into a new form of authoritarianism.

Lu Xun's tone in these essays was often biting and dark. He expressed horror at the brutality inflicted on the masses and at the masses' own passivity and delusion. He expressed contempt for the liberal reformers' naïveté and for the Communist cadres' doctrinairism. He was suspicious of grand narratives and slogans, preferring irony and dissection to affirmation.

Yet beneath the pessimism ran a deep ethical commitment: Lu Xun believed that **consciousness and language were inseparable from freedom**, that the writer's task was to **name and critique oppression** even at personal cost, and that **artistic integrity was itself a form of resistance**. He lived this conviction, continuing to write and publish despite threats from both Nationalist censors and Communist intimidation, and despite the illness (likely tuberculosis) that dogged his final years.

## Personal Life and the Woodblock Print Movement

Lu Xun's personal life was complicated and often painful. In 1906, returning to Shaoxing under family pressure, he entered into an arranged marriage with **Zhu An** (朱安)—a traditional woman with bound feet whom he barely knew and never regarded as a companion. He treated her with formal courtesy and provided financially for her and his mother, but never consummated the relationship in any real emotional sense. Their estrangement was total; Zhu An spent decades in the shadow of Lu Xun's fame and died in poverty in 1947, largely forgotten.

His true companion was **Xu Guangping** (许广平), a student at the Beijing Women's Normal College where he lectured, with whom he began corresponding in 1925. Despite a significant age gap (she was born in 1898, he in 1881), they became deeply attached through an extensive correspondence later published as *Letters Between Two* (*Liangdi Shu*, 两地书, 1933). After moving to Shanghai in 1927, they lived together openly—a significant act of social defiance for the era. Their son **Zhou Haiying** (周海婴, 1929–2011), born in September 1929, later became a radio engineer, photographer, and memoirist.

Lu Xun's late career included a significant turn toward the visual arts. From the late 1920s onward, he became the central figure in promoting the **New Woodblock Print Movement** (新兴木刻运动)—inspired by Käthe Kollwitz's German expressionist prints and Soviet constructivist graphic art. He organized woodblock print exhibitions, funded the publication of print portfolios, mentored young printmakers like Li Hua and Chen Tiegeng, and wrote extensively on the social and political power of visual art. He saw woodblock printing—a technique deeply rooted in Chinese [printing tradition](../culture-society/printing-and-woodblock.md) but now wielded with modernist European technique—as an ideal medium for working-class political art: cheap to produce, immediate in impact, rooted in craft.

## Legacy and Death (1936)

Lu Xun died on **October 19, 1936**, in Shanghai, from pulmonary tuberculosis (likely complicated by emphysema from heavy smoking), at age fifty-five. His death was a moment of national mourning: his funeral procession drew thousands; writers, students, and workers draped his coffin with a banner reading "Soul of the Nation" (民族魂). Prominent writers, including Ba Jin, were among the pallbearers.

The Communist Party, which he had criticized and never formally joined, moved immediately to claim him. Mao Zedong, in his 1940 essay *On New Democracy*, praised Lu Xun as "the greatest and most courageous standard-bearer of this new cultural force"—placing him alongside Marx and Lenin as intellectual founders of the revolutionary project. After 1949, Lu Xun was canonized in the People's Republic curriculum: his stories were required reading in middle and high school language textbooks; museums were established in Shanghai and Shaoxing; postage stamps, statues, and street names honored him across China.

This canonization, however, systematically distorted Lu Xun's actual work. The essays in which he criticized Communist Party censorship, warned against revolutionary tyranny, and insisted on the artist's independence were downplayed or selectively anthologized. His friend and fellow writer **Qu Qiubai** (瞿秋白)—a Communist leader who was captured and executed by Nationalists in 1935—was glorified as a martyr; their genuine friendship was used to establish Lu Xun's Communist credentials. During the Cultural Revolution, Red Guards initially attacked Lu Xun as a "bourgeois individualist," but Mao's explicit praise of him provided protection from the worst denunciations; Lu Xun's canonization survived 1966 largely intact, though the nuances of his critique did not.

In post-Mao China, Lu Xun's presence in school curricula has gradually contracted. Several of his stories were removed from the mandatory middle-school reading list between 2010 and 2020—a change attributed by educators to their difficulty and dark tone, but interpreted by many as reflecting official discomfort with their subversive content. Among Chinese dissidents and liberal intellectuals, Lu Xun remains a touchstone: his warning that revolution often produces new forms of authoritarianism, his insistence that the writer must maintain independence from state power, and his dissection of the mechanisms by which ideology subjugates consciousness are cited repeatedly in discussions of censorship, political repression, and intellectual complicity. Writers from Gao Xingjian to Liu Xiaobo have invoked Lu Xun's legacy in arguing for the freedom to write honestly about Chinese society.

His life and work represent the enduring tragedy of twentieth-century Chinese intellectualism: the tension between the revolutionary imperative to transform society and the intellectual's need for freedom to think and speak honestly; between national salvation and individual integrity; between the demand for utopia and the recognition that all systems of power—revolutionary and reactionary alike—harbor their own forms of oppression.
