# Li Bai

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Li Bai (701–762 CE), Du Fu (712–770 CE), Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (712–756 r.), Emperor Daizong of Tang (762–779 r.)

## Summary

Li Bai (701–762 CE) was a Chinese poet and cultural figure widely regarded as one of the two greatest poets of the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE) and, by extension, all of Chinese literature. Born in what is now Sichuan province (possibly in Central Asia during his infancy, according to some biographical accounts), Li Bai exemplified the Daoist ideal of spontaneity, naturalness, and freedom from conventional constraint. His poetry, characterized by vivid imagery, emotional intensity, imaginative flights, and direct expression of personal experience rather than learned allusion, represented a revolutionary departure from the more formal and regulated verse style dominant among contemporary court poets and examination scholars. Li Bai lived during the Tang's golden age of cosmopolitan prosperity and cultural flourishing, befriending fellow poets, officials, and thinkers, including his great rival and contemporary **Du Fu** (712–770). Though less successful in official career than in literary fame—his appointments were brief and turbulent—Li Bai became an exemplar of the romantic poet-individual who dared to challenge social convention and who found truth and beauty in wine, friendship, nature, and imagination. His legend grew after his death, shaping the ideal of the *wenren* (literatus) for subsequent generations and profoundly influencing Chinese aesthetics, philosophy, and the very definition of poetic genius.

## Life and Career

Li Bai was born in 701 CE. Traditional accounts trace his ancestry to the Longxi Li clan of present-day Gansu in northwestern China—the lineage from which the Tang imperial house also claimed descent—but hold that the family had settled in Central Asia (the region of Suyab, or Broken Leaf, near modern Kyrgyzstan), moving east to Sichuan when Li Bai was still a young child. Whether Li Bai was born during this migration or in Sichuan remains uncertain, but the biography emphasizes his diverse geographic and cultural exposure from childhood—an unusual cosmopolitanism for a Chinese poet of his era.

Sichuan, rich in natural beauty and relative distance from Chang'an (modern-day Xi'an), the Tang capital, was a cultural backwater compared to the imperial center. Yet Li Bai's early years there were formative. He received classical education in Confucian texts and studied both the literary arts and military strategy. As a young man, Li Bai developed his distinctive poetic voice and philosophical orientation: he was drawn to Daoism, Buddhism, and the ideal of *xianren* (immortal)—the untethered wanderer free from worldly constraints.

In his twenties, Li Bai left Sichuan and began a period of wandering and poetic composition throughout China. He traveled the Yangtze River valley, visited the sacred mountains (which feature prominently in his verse), and cultivated a reputation as a poet of exceptional talent and eccentric personality. Unlike examination scholars who aspired to bureaucratic office through merit and Confucian mastery, Li Bai cultivated relationships with aristocrats and princes through poetry and persuasive patrons, seeking the patronage of rulers and nobility rather than competing for civil-service positions.

A pivotal moment came before the imperial summons. During his travels to the capital area, Li Bai was introduced to **He Zhizhang** (659–744 CE), a celebrated senior court official and poet who was himself famous for his wine-drinking and unconventional personality. He Zhizhang read Li Bai's poetry and reportedly exclaimed that this stranger must be a *zhéxiānrén* (謫仙人)—a "banished immortal" sent down from heaven. He Zhizhang introduced Li Bai to influential court circles and helped arrange his summons to the capital. The older poet and the younger became fast friends; He Zhizhang figures in several of Li Bai's elegiac poems after his death.

Around 742 CE, at age 41, Li Bai attracted the attention of **Emperor Xuanzong** (712–756 CE), who at that time presided over the Tang at its height of prosperity and cultural refinement. Xuanzong—himself a musician and enthusiast of the arts—summoned Li Bai to Chang'an and granted him a position as a *Hanlin* academician (a prestigious but informal role as a poet-in-residence at court, composing verse for imperial entertainments). During this time Li Bai also became associated with the loose circle of eight poets and officials celebrated for their prodigious wine consumption and eccentric brilliance: Du Fu would later memorialize this group as the **"Eight Immortals of the Wine Cup"** (*Yǐn Zhōng Bā Xiān*, 飲中八仙) in a famous poem, with Li Bai's entry capturing his charismatic excess: "Li Bai can compose a hundred poems after a single gallon of wine / He sleeps in a tavern in Chang'an's market; when the emperor calls, he does not board the boat."

However, the court appointment was brief. Li Bai's eccentric behavior—his drinking, his disregard for protocols, his frank manner—offended powerful court factions. After approximately two years (c. 742–744 CE), he was eased out of the capital with a polite dismissal and a gift. Xuanzong's favor did not extend to protecting Li Bai from court politics and the jealousy of rival officials and established poets.

After leaving Chang'an, Li Bai entered his greatest period of poetic productivity and wandering. He traveled widely—across the Yangtze valley, into the Shandong peninsula, through Henan—and in 744 CE, in Luoyang, he first met **Du Fu**, beginning one of the most celebrated literary friendships in Chinese history. The two traveled together briefly in 744–745 CE, visiting Daoist mountains and engaging in the shared wine-drinking and poetry recitation that characterizes Tang literati friendship.

The catastrophe of the **An Lushan Rebellion** (755–763 CE)—in which the military commander An Lushan launched a massive revolt that sacked Chang'an and drove the Tang court into exile—shattered the prosperous world Li Bai had known. Emperor Xuanzong fled to Sichuan; the rebellion split the empire. Li Bai, in Anhui province, initially withdrew from political life. But in 757 CE he joined the military staff of **Prince Yong** (Li Lin), one of the emperor's sons who had established a court in the Jiangnan region—apparently intending to help restore Tang order. Prince Yong's enterprise, however, was viewed as rebellious by the new emperor, Suzong, who had taken power in the north. When Prince Yong was defeated and his court dissolved, Li Bai was arrested as a collaborator, tried, and sentenced to permanent exile in **Yelang** (present-day Guizhou province, then a remote, malaria-ridden frontier). En route to exile, he received an amnesty in 759 CE due to a general pardon, turned back, and settled in the Anhui region.

Li Bai died in 762 CE at the home of a relative (his cousin Li Yangbing) in Dangtu county, Anhui province, in greatly reduced circumstances and likely in poor health. Later romantic legend claimed he drowned in the Yangtze River while reaching to embrace the moon's reflection in the water—a poetic end befitting his life of imaginative excess and Daoist ideals. In historical reality, Li Yangbing's preface to a collection of Li Bai's poems written shortly after the poet's death describes him dying of illness. He was celebrated even immediately after his passing as a poet of unparalleled genius and a symbol of individual creative freedom.

## Poetry and Literary Achievement

Li Bai's poetry redefined Chinese literary aesthetics. While the Tang developed a sophisticated examination system in which poetic composition (in regulated verse forms) was a requisite skill, Li Bai's work largely escaped the rigid constraints of regulated verse (*lüshi* — parallel couplets with tonal patterns). Instead, he excelled in *yuefu* (folk-song style) poetry and *gu shi* (ancient-style) verse, genres that allowed greater freedom of form, rhyme, and emotional expression.

**Themes and Style**: Li Bai's poetry is characterized by:
- **Nature imagery**: Vivid descriptions of mountains, rivers, the moon, clouds, and landscape, often as metaphors for human emotion and aspiration
- **Immediacy and spontaneity**: First-person voice and emotional directness rather than learned allusion and formal restraint
- **Imagination and fancy**: Flights of imaginative fancy, mythology, and paradox—dreams, immortals, cosmic journeys
- **Daoism and philosophy**: Explicit engagement with Daoist concepts of naturalness, freedom from constraint, and harmony with the Tao
- **Wine and friendship**: Celebrations of wine-drinking, friendship, and the transcendence of social hierarchy through shared poetry and fellowship
- **Individualism**: An unapologetic assertion of personal feeling and perception over conventional propriety

**Famous Poems**: Among his most celebrated works are:

- **"In the Quiet Night"** (*Jìng Yè Sī*, 静夜思) — Only 20 Chinese characters in four lines, this lyric captures a traveler awakening at night, seeing moonlight on the floor, mistaking it briefly for frost, then gazing up at the moon in homesickness. It is perhaps the single most memorized poem in the Chinese literary canon; virtually every Chinese schoolchild learns it. Its power comes from extreme compression: emotion rendered through pure image with no abstraction.
- **"Drinking Alone Under the Moon"** (*Yuè Xià Dú Zhuó*, 月下獨酌, four poems in a series) — Li Bai imagines the moon and his own shadow as drinking companions, since no human friend is present. The poem transcends loneliness by expanding friendship to the cosmos itself, ending with a hope to meet again at the Milky Way—a characteristic move of cosmic scale from intimate personal emotion.
- **"The Road to Shu Is Hard"** (*Shǔ Dào Nán*, 蜀道難) — One of the longest and most technically ambitious *yuefu* poems in the language, describing the perilous mountain route between Chang'an and Sichuan through vivid, escalating imagery. The refrain—"The road to Shu is hard, hard as climbing to the sky" (*蜀道之難，難於上青天*)—became a cultural byword for extreme difficulty. He Zhizhang reportedly read it and was astounded, proof of Li Bai's early genius.
- **"Send-Off at Yellow Crane Tower"** (*Huáng Hè Lóu Sòng Mèng Hào Rán Zhī Guǎng Líng*, 黃鶴樓送孟浩然之廣陵) — A brief farewell poem to the poet Meng Haoran, watching his friend's boat disappear into the horizon: "The solitary sail, a distant shadow, vanishes into the blue void; / All I see is the Yangtze flowing to heaven." Exemplifies Li Bai's ability to find the cosmic in the personal.
- **The moon poems** — Beyond "In the Quiet Night," the moon recurs in hundreds of Li Bai's poems as a symbol of permanence, beauty, purity, longing, and transcendence; his treatment of it was so distinctive that he is sometimes called the "Moon Poet" in Chinese critical tradition, and later legend fittingly imagined him drowning while reaching for its reflection.

His collected works, preserved over centuries and edited by subsequent scholars (notably the Song dynasty edition), number over 1,000 surviving poems—a vast body of work remarkable for its consistency of genius and diversity of form. Some scholars estimate he composed as many as 3,000 poems, of which roughly one-third survive.

## Li Bai and Du Fu: The Twin Stars of Tang Poetry

Li Bai's great contemporary was **Du Fu** (712–770 CE), eleven years his junior. Where Li Bai exemplified romantic freedom and imaginative flight, Du Fu represented scholarly rigor, social conscience, and the examination-based literatus ideal. The two poets knew each other and held each other in high regard, though they represented different poetic traditions and temperaments.

Du Fu's poetry is more formally complex, densely allusive, morally serious, and concerned with historical and political themes. Du Fu was also more ambitious for official career and suffered greater persecution during the chaos of the An-Lushan Rebellion (755–763 CE). While both are celebrated as the dual peaks of Tang poetry, later literati tended to see them as complementary: Li Bai for genius, freedom, and imaginative power; Du Fu for moral seriousness, craft, and historical witness.

## Legacy and Cultural Influence

**Literati Ideal**: Li Bai became the model of the *wenren*—the cultured individual poet-thinker—for subsequent dynasties. The ideal of the poet-official, combining political engagement with artistic sensitivity, derived partly from Li Bai's example (even though his political career was unsuccessful). Literati of the Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties invoked Li Bai as a standard of poetic achievement and spiritual independence.

**Aesthetics**: Li Bai's emphasis on spontaneity, imagination, and emotional authenticity influenced not only Chinese poetry but painting and calligraphy. The ideal of *xinyun* (spirit consonance)—capturing the essential spirit of a subject through intuitive, seemingly effortless technique—owes much to Li Bai's poetic practice and later interpretations of it.

**Philosophy**: Li Bai's deep engagement with Daoism and Buddhism, expressed through poetry, helped shape the Sino-Buddhist synthesis and the literati tradition's philosophical eclecticism. His vision of freedom from convention and alignment with natural spontaneity (*wu wei*) became a touchstone for artists and thinkers skeptical of state authority or examination orthodoxy.

**International Influence**: Li Bai's poetry was transmitted to Japan and Korea, where it influenced East Asian literary traditions. Modern Western poets, upon encountering translated selections, found in Li Bai a precedent for Romantic and Modernist poetics—spontaneity, emotional intensity, vivid imagery, and rebellion against convention—and he has become one of the most translated Chinese poets in English and European languages.

**Myth and Legend**: Following his death, Li Bai's life was elaborated with romantic legends. Later stories added miracles, celestial visits, and a kind of apotheosis. Painters depicted him wine-cup in hand, reaching for the moon. The historical Li Bai—already remarkable—became mythologized as the ultimate embodiment of artistic genius, freedom, and ecstatic communion with nature and the cosmos.
