# Xi Jinping

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Deng Xiaoping (predecessor, mentor), Jiang Zemin (previous general secretary), Hu Jintao (previous president)

## Summary

Xi Jinping (born June 15, 1953) is the paramount leader of the People's Republic of China since 2012, serving as General Secretary of the Communist Party of China, President of the People's Republic of China, and Chairman of the Central Military Commission—the three positions that consolidate supreme power in the Chinese system. Xi represents the fifth generation of Communist Party leadership since 1949, succeeding Hu Jintao, and has fundamentally reshaped China's domestic governance, foreign policy, and global posture. He succeeded Hu Jintao, who served as General Secretary from 2002 to 2012. Within China, Xi has launched the most aggressive anti-corruption campaign in decades, justifying it as necessary to restore Party legitimacy but also using it to eliminate rival factions and consolidate personal power. He has promoted the concept of the "Chinese Dream," framing economic growth and national rejuvenation as the nation's collective purpose. Internationally, Xi initiated the Belt and Road Initiative (2013), an ambitious infrastructure and investment program connecting China to Asia, Africa, and Europe, positioning China as a major superpower with global influence. He has strengthened China's military capabilities, asserted Chinese claims in the South China Sea and Taiwan Strait, and reshaped China's relationship with the United States from accommodation to strategic competition. His domestic policies have included mass surveillance and social credit systems, enhanced state control of the private sector, and controversial policies toward minority groups including the Uyghurs in Xinjiang. In 2018, Xi consolidated power by removing term limits from the Chinese presidency, suggesting his tenure could extend indefinitely. Xi's leadership represents a decisive break from the pragmatism and economic-first focus of Deng Xiaoping and his successors; under Xi, the Communist Party's ideological authority and centralized control have reasserted primacy, and China's global role has shifted from a rising power accommodating the existing international order to a rival superpower challenging it.

## Early Life and Political Ascent

Xi Jinping was born in Beijing in 1953 to Xi Zhongxun (1913–2002), a high-ranking Communist Party official who served as vice premier and Party chief of Guangdong Province, and Qi Xin (born 1926), a Party member who had joined the Communist cause as a young woman. As the son of a senior cadre, Xi was privileged by birth, but the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) disrupted his privileged life. During the Cultural Revolution, his father was purged and persecuted for alleged capitalist sympathies; Xi Jinping, as a teenager, was sent to the countryside in Shaanxi Province as part of the "Down to the Countryside" movement, where he performed manual labor and lived among peasants for seven years (1969–1975).

This period of exile, known as Xi's *zhiqing* (sent-down youth) experience, became central to his public biography and self-presentation. Official Party accounts credit his years in the Shaanxi countryside with instilling humility, connecting him to China's rural masses, and developing his pragmatism. His willingness to engage in manual labor and live as a peasant was cited as evidence of moral character and revolutionary commitment—a narrative that echoes Maoist ideology while distancing him from the privileged cosmopolitanism of intellectuals. Whether Xi's Shaanxi years fundamentally shaped his governance philosophy or were retrospectively dramatized by Party propaganda remains debated by analysts, but the narrative functioned effectively to legitimize his leadership.

After the Cultural Revolution ended in 1976 and his father was rehabilitated, Xi's political career advanced rapidly. He studied chemical engineering at Tsinghua University (1975–1979), one of China's most elite institutions, graduating just as Deng Xiaoping's reform era opened. He entered the Party bureaucracy in Hebei Province, serving in various administrative roles, then moved to Fujian Province (1985–2002), where he rose through a succession of posts to deputy governor, acting governor, and governor, overseeing the development of special economic zones and positioning Fujian as a center of market-oriented reforms and foreign investment. During his Fujian years, Xi became known for pragmatism and openness to business, consistent with Deng Xiaoping's reform agenda. It was in Fujian, in 1987, that Xi married folk singer Peng Liyuan (born 1962)—one of China's most famous entertainers, a renowned soprano who held a civilian rank equivalent to major general in the People's Liberation Army's arts establishment—a partnership that would later become central to Xi's public image and to Chinese soft-power diplomacy, with Peng serving as a prominent first lady at international events.

In 2002, Xi transferred to Zhejiang Province, one of China's most economically dynamic coastal provinces, as Party Secretary—a significant promotion. Zhejiang was home to some of China's most entrepreneurial private-sector companies (including early Alibaba, based in Hangzhou), and Xi's five-year tenure there (2002–2007) coincided with Zhejiang's emergence as a manufacturing and export powerhouse. Xi developed a governance approach he called the "Zhejiang experience" (*zhejiang jingyan*), emphasizing pragmatic economic development, support for small and medium enterprises, and anti-corruption at the local level. In March 2007, Xi was appointed Party Secretary of Shanghai—a position widely regarded as a final test before elevation to national leadership—though his Shanghai tenure lasted only seven months. In October 2007, at the 17th National Congress of the Communist Party, Xi joined the Politburo Standing Committee and was widely recognized as the designated successor to Hu Jintao.

## Rise to Paramount Leadership and Consolidation of Power

In November 2012, Xi Jinping was elected General Secretary of the Communist Party at the Party Congress, and in March 2013, he became President of the People's Republic. Unlike his predecessors, who had been explicitly designated as successors years in advance, Xi's assumption of power was presented as a transition following established rules, but his subsequent consolidation of power was far more aggressive than his predecessors'.

Within months of taking office, Xi initiated a sweeping anti-corruption campaign that has been the most extensive Party purge in decades. Hundreds of thousands of officials and businesspeople have been investigated for corruption, with senior figures including government ministers, military generals, and business tycoons imprisoned or executed. The campaign's stated goal was to restore public confidence in the Party and clean house; however, critics argue that it functioned as a mechanism to eliminate rivals and consolidate Xi's personal authority. The campaigns targeted factions loyal to his predecessors, particularly former Politburo Standing Committee member Zhou Yongkang and others associated with rival power bases, removing potential opponents and ensuring Xi's dominance within the Party.

A critical moment came in 2018, when the National People's Congress voted to remove term limits from the presidency, allowing Xi to potentially serve beyond the previous two-term limit. This change, while formally voted on by the legislature with 2,958 votes in favor, 2 against, and 3 abstentions, was understood as enabling Xi to remain in power indefinitely. Official accounts framed it as necessary to ensure continuity of his leadership agenda; critics saw it as a consolidation of authoritarianism. The removal of term limits marked a decisive break from the Deng Xiaoping-era reform that had limited leaders to two terms, intended to prevent the personality cults and indefinite tenures of Mao.

In October 2022, at the 20th National Congress of the Communist Party, Xi secured an unprecedented third five-year term as General Secretary—breaking the informal convention that had governed leadership succession since Deng Xiaoping. He also filled the Politburo Standing Committee entirely with loyal allies, sidelining figures associated with rival factions; Li Qiang, who had served under Xi in Zhejiang, became Premier, replacing the reformist Li Keqiang. Foreign observers noted that the resulting leadership lineup contained no evident succession arrangement, suggesting Xi may intend to lead China indefinitely. The 20th Congress also formally elevated "Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era" to parity with Mao Zedong Thought in the Party constitution—an ideological status no leader between Mao and Xi had achieved.

## Domestic Governance and the "Chinese Dream"

Xi's domestic agenda has centered on the concept of the "Chinese Dream" (*zhongguo meng*), a nationalist narrative emphasizing national rejuvenation, economic prosperity, and restoration of China's "rightful place" as a great power. The Chinese Dream appeals to historical grievance (China's "century of humiliation" at the hands of Western and Japanese imperialism from the mid-19th to mid-20th centuries) and frames the Communist Party's continued rule as the guarantor of China's recovery and greatness.

**Ideological reassertion**: Xi has reasserted the Party's ideological authority after decades of pragmatism and de-emphasis of Communist theory under Deng Xiaoping, Jiang Zemin, and Hu Jintao. Party cadres are required to study "Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era," and his ideas have been incorporated into the Party constitution. This represents a return to personality-centric ideology reminiscent of Mao, though Xi's ideology emphasizes order, modernization, and national strength rather than the revolutionary fervor of Maoism.

**Mass surveillance and social credit**: The Chinese state, under Xi's tenure, has implemented unprecedented surveillance capabilities, particularly in Xinjiang, where over 1 million Uyghurs and other ethnic minorities have been detained in "re-education camps" according to Human Rights Watch and international researchers. Facial recognition systems, ubiquitous security cameras, and smartphone tracking create pervasive monitoring throughout urban China. The government has implemented a "social credit system" that tracks individuals' financial behavior, legal compliance, and moral conduct, rewarding those who comply with government policies and punishing dissenters through travel bans, public shaming, and exclusion from employment. These systems represent a level of technological surveillance unprecedented in Chinese history.

**Control of the private sector and technology companies**: While Deng Xiaoping's reforms encouraged private business and market competition, Xi has increased Party control over major private corporations. Tech billionaires such as Jack Ma (Alibaba founder) have been brought under regulatory scrutiny; tech companies are required to embed Communist Party committees within their corporate structures; and the Party has intervened in tech sector mergers, education industries, and property markets to subordinate business to Party-defined national interests. This shift from market-first to Party-first governance represents a significant ideological reversal.

**Uyghur and ethnic minority policies**: Xi's policies toward ethnic minorities, particularly the Uyghurs in Xinjiang, have been highly controversial internationally. The government has detained over 1 million Uyghurs in vocational training and "re-education" camps, arguing that they are necessary to combat terrorism and extremism; international observers and governments, particularly the United States, Canada, and the European Union, have characterized the detention system as genocide. The camps involve forced assimilation, Han Chinese settlement in Uyghur regions, restrictions on religious practice, and suppression of Uyghur language and culture. These policies have been justified as counterterrorism but represent the most significant human rights violations in contemporary China.

## Foreign Policy and the Belt and Road Initiative

Xi's foreign policy has been far more assertive than his predecessors, positioning China not as a "peaceful rise" accommodating the existing Western-dominated international order, but as a rival superpower challenging it. The centerpiece of his foreign policy is the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI, or "One Belt, One Road"), launched in 2013.

The BRI is an ambitious infrastructure and investment program involving tens of billions of dollars in Chinese loans and direct investment across Asia, Africa, Europe, and the Pacific. The program finances ports, railways, highways, energy projects, and telecommunications networks, officially aimed at facilitating trade and economic development in recipient countries. However, critics argue that the BRI creates debt dependency, allowing China to acquire strategic assets (ports, key infrastructure) as collateral when countries default on loans, and expands Chinese political influence throughout the developing world. Several countries, including Sri Lanka and Kenya, have had difficulty servicing BRI debts, leading to Chinese acquisition of long-term control of strategic infrastructure.

The BRI represents an inversion of the Silk Road model: where the historical Silk Road was a distributed, merchant-driven network of exchange, the BRI is a state-directed, hierarchical system of infrastructure and finance that positions China as the central hub and recipient countries as dependent nodes. Nevertheless, the historical Silk Road rhetoric is deployed in official Chinese narratives, framing the BRI as a return to historical patterns of cooperation and mutual benefit.

**South China Sea and territorial assertiveness**: Xi's administration has been more aggressive in asserting Chinese territorial claims in the South China Sea, expanding and militarizing artificial islands, building military installations on disputed features, and challenging freedom of navigation by other countries' navies. These actions have increased tension with Southeast Asian nations, Japan, South Korea, and the United States, but Xi's government has framed them as restoration of China's legitimate sovereign territory and pushback against Western encroachment.

**Taiwan and military modernization**: Xi has strengthened the People's Liberation Army and modernized China's military capabilities, positioning Taiwan reunification as a paramount national goal. While previous leaders had emphasized peaceful resolution and indefinite status quo, Xi has suggested that reunification may be necessary and has not ruled out the use of force. Military exercises near Taiwan have increased in frequency and scale under Xi's tenure, raising regional tensions.

**U.S.-China relations and strategic competition**: Under Xi, China-U.S. relations have shifted from accommodation and strategic partnership (as framed in the Nixon-Kissinger era opening and reinforced by economic interdependence) to strategic competition and rivalry. Xi's government views the United States as attempting to contain and constrain China's rise, and Chinese foreign policy has emphasized self-sufficiency and reduced dependence on Western technology and markets. Trade tensions, technology sanctions, and military posturing have characterized Xi-era U.S.-China relations, with both nations competing for influence in Asia, Africa, and globally.

**The Belt and Road Initiative's scale**: By 2023, the BRI had signed memoranda of understanding with more than 150 countries and international organizations, making it the largest multilateral infrastructure program in history. Chinese state banks and investment funds had committed approximately $1 trillion in loans and direct investments, though actual disbursement was significantly lower. Projects span railway lines in East Africa, deep-water ports in Sri Lanka (Hambantota Port, leased to China for 99 years in 2017 after Sri Lanka defaulted on loans), hydroelectric dams in Pakistan, pipelines in Central Asia, and telecommunications infrastructure connecting Southeast Asia to Europe. Critics have characterized the BRI as "debt trap diplomacy," a term disputed by Chinese officials, who maintain the program is voluntary, commercially structured, and mutually beneficial.

**Russia and the Ukraine War**: Xi has cultivated a close personal relationship with Russian President Vladimir Putin, with the two leaders meeting more than 40 times between 2012 and 2023. In February 2022, just days before Russia invaded Ukraine, Xi and Putin declared a "no limits" partnership between China and Russia, committing to unlimited cooperation across all domains. China has not condemned the Ukraine invasion and has abstained from United Nations resolutions condemning Russia; at the same time, China has avoided direct military or financial sanctions-busting assistance, likely to protect Chinese banks' access to Western financial systems. China has benefited materially from the war—Russian energy exports to China increased substantially at discounted prices after Western sanctions diverted Russian energy eastward—while maintaining rhetorical neutrality. The China-Russia "no limits" partnership has significantly complicated Chinese relations with Europe and deepened Western distrust of Beijing's declared neutrality.

## Economic Policy and Reform Limits

Despite his nationalist rhetoric and Party-first ideology, Xi has not reversed Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms. China's economy remains market-oriented, though with increased state supervision. Xi has emphasized economic efficiency and innovation, promoting "Made in China 2025," a program to make China the world's leader in advanced manufacturing and technology. However, his subordination of business to Party ideology, increased regulation of tech companies, and restrictions on capital outflow represent constraints on economic liberalization that earlier reformers had pursued.

The COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2023) revealed contradictions in Xi's governance: while he initially suppressed information about the outbreak (consistent with Party control of information), he later implemented a "zero-COVID" policy of extreme lockdowns and mass testing that, though praised for limiting infections, severely disrupted economic activity and generated significant public discontent. In late November and early December 2022, rare public protests erupted in multiple cities—including Shanghai, Beijing, Chengdu, and Wuhan—with demonstrators holding up blank white sheets of paper as a symbol of censored speech in what became known as the "A4 revolution" or "white paper protests." Though relatively small in scale, these protests were among the most widespread expressions of public discontent China had seen since the 1989 Tiananmen Square demonstrations. Within days, Xi's government abruptly abandoned zero-COVID, though official accounts attributed the change to the policy's success rather than public pressure. The rapid abandonment led to a large "reopening wave" of infections in early 2023, causing tens of thousands of deaths that zero-COVID had previously delayed.

## Legacy and Significance

Xi Jinping's leadership since 2012 has fundamentally transformed China's domestic governance and global posture. Domestically, he has reasserted Communist Party ideological authority, increased state control over the economy and society, implemented unprecedented surveillance and social control systems, and consolidated personal power beyond that of his immediate predecessors. Internationally, he has made China a more assertive superpower, launched the Belt and Road Initiative, challenged Western liberal international order, and positioned China as a rival to the United States. Whether these changes will prove beneficial or detrimental to China's long-term development and international standing remains contested and will likely be debated for decades.

Xi's tenure represents a decisive departure from Deng Xiaoping's philosophy of pragmatism, market orientation, and limited state ideology. Whether returning to Party-first governance and ideological assertion strengthens or weakens China remains an open question; equally uncertain is whether Xi's removal of term limits and consolidation of personal power signals a healthy stability or a return to the personality-cult authoritarianism that weakened China under Mao Zedong.
