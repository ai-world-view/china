# Du Fu

**Du Fu** (杜甫, 712–770 CE), known as the "Sage of Poetry" (*shi sheng*, 詩聖) and the "Poet-Historian" (*shi shi*, 詩史), was a Tang Dynasty poet whose technical mastery, moral conscience, and unflinching engagement with human suffering rank him alongside Li Bai as one of the two supreme figures of Chinese literary history. Where Li Bai embodied Daoist spontaneity and imaginative flight, Du Fu represented Confucian duty, structural precision, and the poet's obligation to bear witness to the sorrows of his age. His collected works number over 1,400 poems; they established the forms, moral vision, and thematic depths that became canonical for all subsequent Chinese literature and that defined what it meant to be a poet in the full cultural sense.

## Early Life and Education

Du Fu was born in 712 CE in Gongxian (modern Gong'yi, Henan Province), into a literary family with impeccable scholarly credentials. His grandfather Du Shenyan (杜審言, c. 645–708 CE) was a celebrated court poet under the early Tang emperors who helped define the regulated verse forms that the grandson would later perfect. Du Fu's father Du Xian served as a minor official, and the family, though not wealthy, afforded him an education in the Confucian classics, histories, and poetry.

From approximately 726 to 741 CE, Du Fu traveled widely through eastern China — Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shandong — in the manner of a cultivated young scholar seeking patrons, forming literary friendships, and composing verse. In 735 CE he attempted the imperial examination (*jinshi*) in Chang'an but failed, an outcome attributed not to deficient learning but to the high competition and the political currents that governed examination results under Emperor Xuanzong's increasingly corrupt court. He tried again in 747 CE in a special examination personally convened by the emperor but, together with all other candidates, was cynically failed en masse by the prime minister Li Linfu, who reportedly declared that "no talent remained outside the court" — meaning he wished to prevent any rival to his own influence from gaining office through merit.

## Meeting Li Bai and the Literary Friendship

The event that most shaped Du Fu's poetic identity was his meeting with Li Bai (701–762 CE) in 744 CE in Luoyang. The two greatest poets of the Tang Dynasty, whose temperaments and aesthetics were in many ways antithetical, formed a profound and lasting intellectual friendship. Li Bai was already famous, twenty years Du Fu's senior, his name synonymous with the Daoist-inflected lyric of transcendent freedom; Du Fu was still an unknown young poet, earnest and Confucian. They traveled together through Henan, drinking wine, composing poetry, and, in Du Fu's case, sitting at the feet of the older master.

Du Fu subsequently wrote no fewer than fifteen poems addressed to or about Li Bai, many of them after the two had separated and Li Bai had been exiled. These poems — "Dreaming of Li Bai" (*Meng Li Bai*), the "Spring Day" pieces — express a quality of devoted admiration that is almost without parallel in Chinese literary history, and suggest that for Du Fu the friendship defined something essential about the poetic vocation: the possibility of friendship across aesthetic difference, the way two poets could together stand for the full range of human poetic possibility.

## Court, Failure, and the Approach of Catastrophe

After his second examination failure, Du Fu remained in Chang'an for nearly a decade, eking out a precarious existence while seeking patronage, submitting memorial poems to the court, and waiting for official appointment. In 751 CE he composed three rhapsodies (*fu*) for a court occasion that caught the emperor's notice, and in 755 CE he was finally granted a minor position as a surveillance officer in Henan — a result that came too late to do much good. By 755 CE, the Tang Dynasty was hurtling toward catastrophe.

## The An Lushan Rebellion and Its Consequences (755–763 CE)

The An Lushan Rebellion, which began in December 755 CE when the Sogdian-Turkic general An Lushan (安祿山) led his northeastern armies in revolt against the Tang court, shattered the high Tang world and gave Du Fu his subject matter. Chang'an fell to rebel forces in 756 CE; Emperor Xuanzong fled west to Sichuan, and his son, crowned as Emperor Suzong, rallied loyalist resistance from the northwest. Du Fu, attempting to reach the loyalist court, was captured by rebel forces and held for about a year in Chang'an under rebel occupation. He escaped in 757 CE, made an extraordinary journey through rebel-held territory, and rejoined the Tang court at Fengxiang, where Emperor Suzong granted him the position of Reminder (*zuo shiyi*, 左拾遺) — an advisory post.

This period of capture, flight, and reunion produced Du Fu's most celebrated poetry. The famous five-character regulated verse *Chunwang* ("Spring View," 春望, 756 CE) was composed in occupied Chang'an:

> *Guopo shan he zai* — The nation shattered, mountains and rivers remain  
> *Chengchun cao mu shen* — The city deep in spring, grass and trees grow thick  
> *Gan shi hua jian lei* — Moved by the times, even flowers draw tears  
> *Hen bie niao jing xin* — Pained by separation, birds startle the heart

This poem, eight lines in total, packs into its tonal architecture an entire vision of history: the permanence of nature confronting human catastrophe, the beauty of spring made unbearable by political ruin.

The "Three Officials / Three Separations" sequences (composed 759 CE) are perhaps Du Fu's most sustained engagement with the human cost of war. The "Three Officials" (*san li*, 三吏) — *Xin'an Official* (*Xin'an Li*), *Tongguan Official* (*Tongguan Li*), and *Shihao Official* (*Shihao Li*) — depict scenes of official conscription of young men, forced labor, and the grief of families separated by military service. The *Shihao Official* poem, in which Du Fu watches in silence as officials seize an old woman's last remaining son for the army, enacts the poet's moral position: he witnesses but does not intervene; his role is to record and transmit. The "Three Separations" — *Newly Wed Separated* (*Xin hun bie*), *Aged Separated* (*Chui lao bie*), *No-Family Separated* (*Wu jia bie*) — extend this vision across the full range of war's domestic costs.

## Disillusionment and Departure from the Court

Despite his hard-won court position, Du Fu's tenure as Reminder was short-lived. In 757 CE, he submitted a memorial to the emperor defending the disgraced general Fang Guan, whose reputation Du Fu considered unjustly attacked by political enemies. Emperor Suzong, affronted by what seemed like Du Fu's overreach beyond his minor advisory role, demoted him and sent him away on minor external assignments. Du Fu served briefly in Huazhou before resigning in 759 CE, frustrated by court politics, ill, and increasingly committed to the proposition that poetry, not politics, was his true calling.

The resignation at 759 CE marks a turning point. Du Fu left official life permanently and began the period of wandering — through Gansu, into Sichuan — that would define his final decade.

## The Chengdu Years and the Thatched Cottage (759–765 CE)

Arriving in Chengdu in 760 CE, Du Fu was assisted by the local governor Yan Wu (嚴武), an old acquaintance, and built a modest dwelling — the famous Thatched Cottage (*Caotang*, 草堂) — on the bank of the Pujiang River west of the city. These years, though marked by poverty and illness, were among his most productive. He composed the *Caotang* sequence and other poems celebrating the small domestic pleasures of his retreat: planting vegetables, watching cranes, visiting the river in the morning. Yet even here political consciousness never left him. The poem *Mao Wu Wei Qiu Feng Suo Po Ge* ("The Thatched Cottage Overthrown by the Autumn Wind") ends with a famous vision of social longing:

> *An de guang sha qian wan jian, Da bi tianxia hanshi ju huan yan!*  
> "If only I could have thousands of vast halls to shelter all the poor scholars of the world, they would smile in the teeth of every wind and rain!"

When Yan Wu died in 765 CE, Du Fu lost his patron and protector. He left Chengdu and began traveling east down the Yangtze River toward his original homeland, hoping to return to the north. He would never complete the journey.

## Poetic Achievement and Forms

Du Fu's decisive technical contribution was the perfection of *lüshi* ("regulated verse"), an eight-line form with strict rules of tonal alternation (between level and oblique tones), required internal parallelism (lines 3–4 and 5–6 must be syntactically and semantically parallel), and fixed rhyme. Where earlier poets had found these constraints awkward, Du Fu revealed their full expressive power. His couplets achieve a density and precision that Chinese critics describe as placing ten thousand *jin* of meaning into a single line (*yi zi qian jin*).

He also mastered and renovated ancient-style verse (*guti*), five-character verse (*wuyan*), seven-character verse (*qiyan*), and the extended narrative *yuefu* ballad forms. The "Ballad of the Army Wagons" (*Bingche xing*, 兵車行), composed c. 750 CE before the rebellion, is an early masterpiece of the *yuefu* form: a conscripted soldier's wife addresses a chariot that is carrying her husband away, and the poem builds through concrete domestic detail to an excoriating critique of the frontier wars that were exhausting the empire even before An Lushan's revolt.

Du Fu's major poem collections and sequences:

| Work | Date | Form | Subject |
|------|------|------|---------|
| *Bingche xing* (Ballad of the Army Wagons) | c. 750 | *yuefu* | Anti-war critique |
| *Chunwang* (Spring View) | 756 | five-char. *lüshi* | Occupied Chang'an |
| *Three Officials / Three Separations* | 759 | ancient-style | War's human cost |
| *Qiu Xing* (Autumn Meditations, 8 poems) | 766 | seven-char. *lüshi* | Exile, memory, time |
| *Caotang* sequence | 760–765 | various | Domestic life in Chengdu |
| *Deng Gao* (Climbing the Heights) | 767 | seven-char. *lüshi* | Old age, exile, decline |

The *Qiu Xing* (秋興) sequence of eight seven-character regulated verses, composed at Kuizhou (Chongqing) in 766 CE, is by many critics considered his supreme achievement — eight interlocking meditations on memory, exile, the passage of time, and the poignancy of beautiful landscape that cannot be enjoyed because the heart is too full of grief.

## Themes and Moral Vision

Du Fu's poetry is characterized by a Confucian moral seriousness rare in contemporaneous verse. He wrote about the death of a child, separation from loved ones, aging, poverty, the ravages of warfare, official corruption, and the plight of soldiers and farmers. His famous line "*Zhu men jiurou chou, lu you dong si gu*" — "Behind the crimson gates wine and meat are left to rot; on the road lie the bones of those who froze to death" — from the *Yong Huai* poem of 755 CE, compresses into fourteen characters a vision of social injustice that remains as powerful as any statement in the Chinese poetic tradition.

Du Fu's engagement with nature, deeply aesthetic, never becomes escapist. A solitary egret on a river bar pivots into meditation on exile; autumn leaves evoke fallen dynasties; river mist dissolves the boundaries between private grief and historical catastrophe. This yoking of beauty and sorrow, the personal and the historical, became definitional for the Chinese poetic tradition — what the Japanese term *mono no aware* (the pathos of things) owes partly to its engagement with the Du Fu corpus.

## Later Life, Death, and Legacy

After leaving Chengdu in 765 CE, Du Fu traveled east by river, settling for two years at Kuizhou (modern Fengjie, Chongqing) on the Yangtze. His health declined through recurring illnesses — lung disease, partial paralysis, and possibly diabetes. He finally departed Kuizhou in 768 CE, reached Hunan, and died in 770 CE during a river journey, reportedly on a boat near Tanzhou (modern Changsha). He was fifty-eight years old.

Du Fu's influence on Chinese poetry cannot be overstated. The regulated forms he perfected became the standard for serious poetry through the end of the imperial era. Within one generation of his death, Tang critics such as Yuan Zhen (779–831 CE) were calling him the greatest poet in Chinese history. The Song Dynasty Neo-Confucians revered him as a moral exemplar. Su Shi (1037–1101 CE) studied his couplets obsessively. Huang Tingjian (1045–1105 CE) founded the Jiangxi poetry school partly on a systematic imitation of Du Fu's craft. The twentieth-century scholar Qian Zhongshu's *Limited Views* (*Guanzhui bian*), with its close readings of Du Fu's imagery among many other classics, remains a landmark of classical Chinese criticism.

To know Chinese poetry is to know Du Fu. Every major poet of subsequent dynasties engaged with his corpus — either emulating his forms, inheriting his moral seriousness, or consciously departing from him to define their own position. He stands, in the judgment of the tradition itself, as the poet who most fully realized the possibilities of the art.
