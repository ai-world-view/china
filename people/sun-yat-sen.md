# Sun Yat-sen

**Sun Yat-sen** (孫逸仙 / 孫中山, 1866–1925) was a revolutionary leader, physician, statesman, and founding father of the Republic of China. As the principal architect of the 1911 Xinhai Revolution that overthrew the Qing Dynasty, Sun envisioned a democratic, unified China and articulated the political philosophy of the **Three Principles of the People** (三民主義)—nationalism, democracy, and social welfare—which shaped modern Chinese political thought in both the People's Republic of China and Taiwan.

## Early Life and Education

Sun was born **Sun Wen** (孫文) on November 12, 1866, in Cuiheng village, Xiangshan County (present-day Zhongshan City), Guangdong Province, to a Cantonese peasant family. At age thirteen (1879), he traveled to Honolulu, Hawaii, to join his elder brother Sun Mei, who had emigrated and established a prosperous ranch. In Honolulu, Sun attended **Iolani School**, an Anglican institution where he received instruction in English, mathematics, and Christianity, winning a prize in English grammar at graduation. He subsequently enrolled briefly at Oahu College (now Punahou School).

Returning to Guangdong, Sun was baptized as a Christian (1883) and later studied medicine at the **Canton Hospital Medical School** and then at the **Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese** (established 1887), from which he graduated in 1892. He was the school's first cohort of graduates and earned high marks, but the degree was not recognized by the British colonial medical licensing board, a slight he reportedly felt keenly.

During his Hong Kong years, Sun encountered Western political philosophy, met Chinese reformers, and became increasingly convinced that China's problems required institutional transformation rather than mere technical modernization. His exposure to Hong Kong's relatively orderly, legally governed colonial society intensified his critique of Qing misrule.

## Radicalization and the Revive China Society

The **First Sino-Japanese War** (1894–1895), in which the Qing's modernized Beiyang Fleet was destroyed and Korea and Taiwan were ceded to Japan, was a shattering demonstration of imperial failure. While the war was still in progress, Sun submitted a petition to the powerful reformist official Li Hongzhang in Tianjin, proposing a program of Western-style modernization. Li declined to grant an audience. Convinced that reform through the system was impossible, Sun resolved to overthrow the dynasty.

In November 1894, in Honolulu, Sun founded the **Revive China Society** (興中會, *Xingzhonghui*), the first modern Chinese revolutionary organization. Its oath pledged to "expel the Manchus, restore China, establish a unified government." The society drew support from overseas Chinese merchants and laborers in Hawaii, then expanded networks across Southeast Asia and North America.

Sun organized the **Canton Uprising** of October 1895, his first attempt at armed insurrection. The plot was betrayed before it could begin; Qing authorities executed several co-conspirators, and Sun fled to Japan, then to Britain. While in London in 1896, Qing agents abducted Sun and held him in the Chinese Legation, intending to ship him to China for execution. Sun managed to smuggle a note to his former medical professor James Cantlie, who alerted the British Foreign Office and press; public outcry forced the Legation to release Sun after 12 days. The "Kidnapping Affair" made Sun internationally famous and a celebrated symbol of Qing oppression. He subsequently spent several months in the British Museum reading political philosophy, deepening his knowledge of Western liberalism, socialism, and land reform theory—particularly the influence of Henry George's single-tax theory on his social welfare principle.

## Organizing Revolution from Exile

Between 1895 and 1911, Sun organized **ten armed uprisings** against the Qing, all of which failed. He spent most of this period in exile, traveling between Japan, Southeast Asia, North America, and Europe, raising funds among overseas Chinese communities (*huaqiao*). Japan was his most important base: Sun cultivated relationships with Japanese pan-Asianists including **Miyazaki Toten** (宮崎滔天), who admired Sun's revolutionary vision and connected him to influential Japanese politicians and businessmen. Japan's victory over Russia in 1905 electrified Chinese reformers as proof that Asian nations could defeat European powers through modernization.

In August 1905, Sun convened a meeting of revolutionary organizations in Tokyo and unified them into the **Chinese Revolutionary Alliance** (中國同盟會, *Tongmenghui*), an umbrella organization bringing together Sun's Revive China Society, Huang Xing's (黃興) Huaxinghui, and other groups. At this meeting Sun articulated the **Three Principles of the People** (*Sanmin Zhuyi*) as the Alliance's ideological foundation:

1. **Minzu** (民族主義, Nationalism): Ending Manchu rule and resisting Western and Japanese imperialism to build a strong Chinese nation-state.
2. **Minquan** (民權主義, Democracy): Establishing a republican government with popular sovereignty, including civil liberties and elected institutions. Sun later elaborated a "five-power constitution" (*wuquan xianfa*) that added examination and censorate (control) branches to the Western three-branch model.
3. **Minsheng** (民生主義, Social Welfare / People's Livelihood): Ensuring equitable economic development, preventing monopoly capital, and achieving "equalization of land rights" (*pingdi quan*) drawing on Henry George's land value tax theory.

The Alliance published the journal *People's Report* (*Minbao*) from Tokyo, which circulated across Chinese communities worldwide and debated rival reform visions including Kang Youwei's monarchist constitutionalism.

The uprisings of 1906–1911 were costly in lives—hundreds of revolutionaries died—but maintained military pressure and kept the revolution visible. The **Huanghuagang Uprising** (April 27, 1911, Guangzhou) was the bloodiest failed attempt; seventy-two of the fallen were recovered and buried at Yellow Flower Mound (*Huanghuagang*)—commemorated ever after as the "Seventy-two Martyrs," though later research identified 86 dead—and their memorial became a pillar of republican mythology.

## The 1911 Xinhai Revolution and Provisional Presidency

The revolution Sun had worked toward for seventeen years erupted not from one of his planned uprisings but from an accidental explosion in a revolutionary bomb-making workshop in **Wuchang** (武昌) on October 10, 1911. New Army soldiers, discovering their conspiracy had been exposed, mutinied that night, seizing the Wuchang arsenal and government buildings. The Wuchang Uprising ignited a cascade of provincial declarations of independence: within six weeks, fifteen of China's twenty-two provinces had seceded from Qing rule.

Sun was in Denver, Colorado, fundraising when the uprising began. Learning the news from American newspapers, he decided to travel to Europe first—not to China—to attempt to cut off Qing access to international loans by lobbying the British government. He arrived in Hong Kong in late December 1911 after brief stops in London and Paris.

On **December 29, 1911**, delegates of the provincial assemblies meeting in Nanjing elected Sun **Provisional President of the Republic of China**. He was inaugurated on January 1, 1912, the date celebrated as the founding of the Republic of China. Sun served as provisional president for just over two months before ceding the position to **Yuan Shikai** (袁世凱), who was inaugurated on March 10, 1912.

The decision to yield to Yuan—the powerful Qing general who had the loyalty of the Beiyang Army, China's most effective modern military force—was strategic rather than naive. Sun recognized that the revolutionary forces lacked the military power to prevail in a prolonged civil war and that Yuan could guarantee the emperor's abdication. On **February 12, 1912**, Puyi's abdication ended the Qing dynasty. Sun believed that Yuan, constrained by the provisional constitution and parliament, could serve as a transitional figure until democratic institutions matured. He was wrong.

## Struggle Against Yuan Shikai and Warlordism

Yuan Shikai moved rapidly to consolidate personal power, transferring the capital from Nanjing to Beijing, imposing his military loyalists throughout the government, and assassinating Sun's principal political partner, **Song Jiaoren** (宋教仁), in March 1913. Song had reorganized the Alliance into the **Kuomintang** (國民黨, KMT, Chinese Nationalist Party) and was positioned to lead a parliamentary majority when he was shot at Shanghai railway station—the first major political assassination of the republican era.

Sun led the **Second Revolution** (*Erci Geming*, July 1913), an armed uprising of southern provinces against Yuan. It was rapidly crushed; Sun fled to Japan once more. Yuan declared the KMT illegal and dissolved parliament, ruling as dictator. In late 1915, Yuan announced the restoration of the monarchy with himself as emperor—an act that provoked a nationwide rejection and rebellion. Yuan died of kidney disease in June 1916, having reigned as emperor for only 83 days. His death left China fragmented among competing **warlords**, each commanding regional armies and extracting revenue through force.

From his base in **Guangzhou (Canton)**, Sun continued to advocate for constitutional government and national unification. Between 1917 and 1922, he organized three successive Guangzhou governments, each plagued by dependency on unreliable warlord allies. His position was repeatedly undermined by the very warlords he cultivated; in 1922, the Guangzhou military commander Chen Jiongming bombarded Sun's presidential residence, forcing Sun and his wife **Soong Ching-ling** (宋慶齡) to escape by gunboat.

## Soong Ching-ling and Family

Sun married his first wife, Lu Muzhen (盧慕貞), in an arranged marriage in 1884; they had three children. In 1915, while in Tokyo, Sun married **Soong Ching-ling** (宋慶齡, 1893–1981)—the daughter of Charles Jones Soong, a wealthy Shanghai businessman and Methodist missionary who had earlier supported Sun's revolutionary work—over the objections of her family and despite not being divorced from Lu Muzhen (who agreed to a divorce). The marriage to Soong Ching-ling was genuinely romantic and politically consequential: Soong Ching-ling became a prominent political figure in her own right, serving as a vice-president of the PRC until her death. Her sister **Soong Mei-ling** (宋美齡) married Chiang Kai-shek, creating a family dynasty intertwined with 20th-century Chinese history.

## Alliance with the Soviet Union and the First United Front

By 1922, Sun had concluded that Western liberal democracies would not support Chinese nationalism against imperialism—having been rebuffed repeatedly when seeking Western financial or diplomatic assistance. He turned to the new Soviet Union, which offered ideological solidarity against imperialism and concrete organizational expertise.

In January 1923, Sun and Soviet representative **Adolf Joffe** issued the **Sun-Joffe Declaration**, agreeing that the Soviet Union would support China's national unification. The Soviet Comintern sent **Mikhail Borodin** (鮑羅廷) to Guangzhou as political adviser; he helped reorganize the Kuomintang along Leninist lines—a disciplined, centralized party with mass organization capacity.

The reorganized KMT held its **First National Congress** in January 1924, establishing the **First United Front** (第一次國共合作) with the nascent Chinese Communist Party (CCP). Individual CCP members were permitted to join the KMT while maintaining CCP membership. Sun justified this alliance as a temporary tactical necessity: China needed national unification before the social contradictions between the classes could be resolved. He appointed the Soviet-trained military officer **Chiang Kai-shek** (蔣介石) as commandant of the **Whampoa Military Academy** (黃埔軍校, founded May 1924), the school that trained the officer corps for the planned **Northern Expedition** to reunify China by force.

## Death and Unfinished Work

In November 1924, Sun traveled to Beijing at the invitation of warlord Feng Yuxiang to negotiate national reunification—a mission his deteriorating health made harrowing. Diagnosed with liver cancer in January 1925 at Peking Union Medical College (established by the Rockefeller Foundation), Sun died on **March 12, 1925**, at age 58. His final written words were reportedly: "Peace, struggle, save China."

Sun died before the Northern Expedition (1926–1928) that unified China under Kuomintang rule, before the CCP-KMT split (1927) that turned his allied parties into bitter enemies, and before the Japanese invasion (1937) that tested the national unity he had devoted his life to building.

His body lay in state at the Azure Cloud Temple (碧雲寺) in Beijing before being interred in 1929 at the **Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum** (中山陵) in Nanjing, designed by architect Lü Yanzhi in a style blending Chinese imperial and Western neoclassical elements. The mausoleum remains one of China's most visited sites.

## Political Philosophy and Lasting Influence

Sun's Three Principles of the People remain the official ideology of the **Republic of China (Taiwan)**—enshrined in the ROC constitution and taught in schools. The People's Republic of China also honors Sun as a "forerunner of the revolution" (*geming xianxing*), though the CCP claims Mao Zedong and the Communist Party completed what Sun began. This bipartisan reverence across the Taiwan Strait—each claiming Sun as their legitimate heir—makes him uniquely central to Chinese political identity.

Sun's influence extended throughout Asia: Ho Chi Minh admired his anti-imperialist nationalism; Filipino nationalists drew on his example; Korean independence activists found parallels with their own anti-Japanese struggle.

His limitations are equally significant. He never achieved the stable democratic government he envisioned; his practice of relying on military strong-men to achieve political goals helped normalize the pattern of military dominance over civilian politics that plagued the Republic of China throughout its mainland period (1912–1949).
