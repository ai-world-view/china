# Kangxi

**Category**: People
**Key figures**: Kangxi (Xuanye, 1654–1722), Nurhaci, Hong Taiji, Shunzhi, Shang Kexi, Wu Sangui, Oboi, Zheng Jing, Galdan, Ferdinand Verbiest

## Summary

Kangxi (r. 1661–1722) was the fourth emperor of the Qing Dynasty and arguably its greatest ruler. Born Xuanye on 4 May 1654 to Emperor Shunzhi and a Manchu consort from the Donggo clan, he ascended to the throne at age seven following the death of his father, initially ruling under the regency of the Oboi clique. By his early twenties, Kangxi had consolidated personal power, purged rival regents, and launched a 61-year reign marked by territorial expansion, military consolidation, cultural patronage, and the solidification of Manchu rule over the Chinese majority. His reign, spanning 1661 to 1722, remains the longest of any emperor in Chinese history, and transformed the Qing from a recently established conquest dynasty into a stable, prosperous empire at its zenith.

## Early Life and Regency (1654–1669)

Kangxi's childhood was shaped by danger and political instability. Emperor Shunzhi died in 1661, likely from smallpox (or, in some accounts, at age 23 after an emotional collapse following the death of a beloved concubine). Kangxi himself had survived smallpox as an infant, leaving him immune—a crucial consideration for the regency council, which selected him partly because a smallpox-immune emperor was less vulnerable to epidemic. He was raised for a time by his paternal grandmother, the formidable Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang (1613–1688), who proved a decisive influence on his education and political formation.

Real power during Kangxi's childhood rested with four regents, of whom **Oboi** emerged dominant by the mid-1660s. Oboi was a ferocious Manchu warrior who had served Nurhaci and Hong Taiji; he ruled imperiously, purging rivals and executing the reformist regent Suksaha in 1667. Kangxi tolerated Oboi's dominance while quietly cultivating a circle of loyal young bannermen. In 1669, at age 15, Kangxi arranged for his bodyguards—young men whom Oboi dismissed as children playing at wrestling—to seize Oboi while he attended court. Oboi was arrested, tried on more than thirty charges, and placed in lifetime imprisonment (he died in confinement, though the exact year is disputed). With the purge of Oboi, Kangxi began personal rule.

## The Three Feudatories Rebellion (1673–1681)

The most dangerous internal crisis of Kangxi's reign arose from the three powerful Han Chinese generals who had helped the Manchus conquer southern China: **Wu Sangui** (who opened the gates at Shanhai Pass in 1644), **Shang Kexi**, and **Geng Jimao** (succeeded by his son **Geng Jingzhong**). These men had been rewarded with semi-autonomous fiefdoms in Yunnan, Guangdong, and Fujian respectively—the "Three Feudatories"—with their own armies, revenues, and administrative apparatus. By 1673 they controlled resources and military forces that rivaled the Qing imperial establishment itself.

Kangxi, then 19 years old, made the bold decision to demand the voluntary surrender of these fiefdoms rather than allow them to perpetuate indefinitely. Shang Kexi requested retirement; Kangxi granted it but ordered the abolition of his feudatory. Wu Sangui and Geng Jingzhong, alarmed, launched coordinated revolts in November 1673. Wu Sangui declared himself emperor of a new Chinese dynasty and swept north through Hunan; at one point rebel forces held most of south and southwest China. Several Qing officials and generals defected.

The war lasted eight years and was ruinously expensive—the Qing treasury reportedly spent some 200 million taels of silver on the campaigns. Kangxi coordinated a multi-front military response, dispatched capable Manchu and Han Chinese generals, and refused Wu Sangui's peace overtures even when the military situation looked bleak. Wu Sangui died in 1678 before his rebellion collapsed; his grandson Wu Shifan committed suicide in 1681 when Kunming fell to imperial forces. The suppression of the Three Feudatories Rebellion definitively established that the Qing emperor—not powerful regional military men—was the supreme authority in the empire.

## Annexation of Taiwan (1683)

After the fall of the mainland Ming loyalists, the last resistance centered on **Taiwan**, held by the Zheng family—the descendants of Koxinga (Zheng Chenggong), the Ming loyalist who had seized the island from the Dutch in 1662. By the early 1680s the island was ruled by Koxinga's young grandson **Zheng Keshuang**, following the death of his father Zheng Jing in 1681. In 1683, Kangxi authorized a naval campaign under the Fujian admiral **Shi Lang**, himself a former Zheng subordinate who had defected to the Qing. Shi Lang's fleet defeated the Zheng navy decisively at the Battle of Penghu (July 1683), and Zheng Keshuang surrendered. Kangxi incorporated Taiwan into Fujian Province—making it Chinese imperial territory for the first time—though some officials advised abandoning it as an expense. The Qing established a garrison of some 8,000 troops on the island.

## The Treaty of Nerchinsk and Russian Relations (1689)

Russia's eastward expansion through Siberia brought Russian Cossack settlers and forts into the Amur River region by the 1640s–1650s, creating friction with Manchu hunters and settlers. Kangxi initially focused his frontier attention elsewhere, but in the 1680s mounted two sieges of the Russian fort at **Albazin** (1685 and 1686), forcing the Russians to negotiate.

The **Treaty of Nerchinsk** (27 August 1689), negotiated near the Siberian town of Nerchinsk, was a landmark document—the first border agreement between China and a European power reached through direct diplomatic negotiation rather than tributary protocol. The Jesuit missionaries **Thomas Pereira** and **Jean-François Gerbillon** served as interpreters for the Qing side, working in Latin. The treaty:

- Established the Russo-Chinese border along the Argun River and the Stanovoi Mountains.
- Required Russia to demolish Albazin.
- Permitted Russian Orthodox Christian caravans to trade periodically in Beijing.
- Was drawn up in Latin—the authoritative text—with Russian and Manchu versions.

Nerchinsk remained the legal framework for Russo-Chinese relations for nearly 170 years, until Russia extracted further territorial concessions in the unequal treaties of 1858–1860.

## The Dzungar Campaigns

The western frontier posed the greatest strategic challenge of Kangxi's later reign. The **Dzungar** (Western) Mongolian confederacy, unified under the ambitious **Galdan Boshugtu Khan** (c. 1644–1697), posed a credible threat to Qing hegemony over Outer Mongolia and Central Asia. Galdan had defeated the Eastern Mongolian Khalkha tribes in 1688, driving thousands of refugees into Qing territory and appealing to the Kangxi Emperor for protection. Kangxi responded by gathering the Khalkha leaders at a great assembly at **Dolon Nor** (1691) and incorporating them formally into the Qing banner system—the largest single addition of Mongolian subjects in Qing history.

Kangxi then personally led three major military campaigns against Galdan:

1. **1690**: The Battle of Ulan Butung, where Qing forces defeated Galdan in Inner Mongolia, though Galdan escaped.
2. **1696**: Kangxi led a three-pronged advance deep into the Mongolian steppe. His army of some 80,000 men converged on Galdan's forces at **Jao Modo** (near Urga, modern Ulaanbaatar), inflicting a decisive defeat. Galdan's wife was killed in the battle; he fled with a remnant force.
3. **1697**: A final campaign; Galdan died, reportedly by suicide or poison, in April 1697 before imperial forces could capture him.

These campaigns, conducted over some of the most difficult terrain on earth—the Mongolian steppe and Gobi Desert—demonstrated Kangxi's personal command capability and secured Qing supremacy over Outer Mongolia. The Dzungar threat was not finally eliminated until the 1750s under the Qianlong Emperor, but Kangxi had broken Galdan's power and incorporated the Khalkha Mongols permanently into the Qing state.

## Cultural Patronage and Intellectual Life

Kangxi was a genuinely learned ruler with a documented interest in mathematics, astronomy, and classical Chinese literature. His cultural patronage was extensive and politically purposive—he sought to demonstrate that the Manchu conquerors were not barbarians but worthy inheritors of Chinese civilization.

**The Kangxi Dictionary** (*Kangxi Zidian*, 1716): Compiled at imperial command by a team of thirty scholars over five years, this lexicographic monument contains 47,035 Chinese characters organized under 214 radicals—a classification system still used in dictionaries today. It remained the authoritative reference for Chinese character definitions and pronunciations for centuries.

**The *Peiwen Yunfu*** (1711): An enormous rhyming dictionary and literary reference work of some 444 volumes, invaluable for composing classical poetry. Compiled by over 300 scholars, it anthologized quotations from classical literature organized by rhyme.

**The Sacred Edict** (*Shengyu*, 1670): A moral manifesto of sixteen maxims expounding Confucian virtues—filial piety, proper relationships, frugality, respect for learning—directed at the entire population through official readings at local assemblies. The Yongzheng Emperor later expanded it with commentaries in 1724.

**Jesuit Relations**: Kangxi had a genuine fascination with Western science transmitted through the Jesuits at his court. The Belgian Jesuit **Ferdinand Verbiest** (1623–1688) reformed the imperial calendar and constructed new astronomical instruments for the Beijing Observatory; Kangxi studied European geometry, music, and medicine with him. This Jesuit presence led to significant diplomatic engagement with Rome over the **Chinese Rites Controversy**—whether Chinese Christians could maintain Confucian ancestor veneration rites. When Pope Clement XI issued the bull *Ex illa die* (1715) prohibiting Chinese Rites, Kangxi expelled most Jesuits from China (1721), ending a century-long era of Jesuit influence at the Qing court.

## The Succession Crisis

Kangxi's final decade was marred by a bitter struggle over succession—the worst crisis of his reign's later period. He had named his second son **Yinreng** Crown Prince in 1675, but Yinreng proved unstable, dissolute, and autocratic. Kangxi deposed him in 1708, reinstated him in 1709, and deposed him again definitively in 1712, placing him under permanent house arrest. The resulting vacuum among Kangxi's more than twenty adult sons produced fierce factional maneuvering. His fourth son **Yinzhen** (later the Yongzheng Emperor) cultivated a reputation for diligent loyalty while quietly outmaneuvering rivals. On Kangxi's death on 20 December 1722, Yinzhen was declared emperor—amid persistent rumors, never conclusively proven, that the succession had been altered in the final moments.

## Significance

Kangxi's reign marks the apex of Qing imperial power and the height of territorial and administrative consolidation. By eliminating internal military threats—the Oboi regency, the Three Feudatories, the Taiwan loyalists, and Galdan's Dzungars—securing the frontiers through both diplomacy (Nerchinsk) and force, and incorporating Han Chinese administrative and cultural practices through his own deep Confucian scholarship, he ensured that the Qing Dynasty would become not merely a conquest regime but a lasting imperial polity accepted by the Han Chinese elite.

His 61-year reign established a template for orderly succession and set the conditions for the subsequent expansion under the Qianlong Emperor. The Kangxi-Yongzheng-Qianlong period (1661–1795) is considered the "High Qing"—the dynasty's century-long zenith. Kangxi's consolidation of power over Chinese territory ensured the continuity of the territorial state that modern China inherited, including Inner Mongolia, Taiwan, and the Russo-Chinese border framework that shaped northeastern Asian geography until the mid-nineteenth century.
