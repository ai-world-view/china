# Confucianism

**Category**: Culture & Society  
**Key figures**: Confucius (Kongzi, 551–479 BCE), Mencius (Mengzi, 372–289 BCE), Xunzi (310–235 BCE), Zhu Xi (1130–1200 CE), Wang Yangming (1472–1529 CE)

## Summary

Confucianism is a philosophical and ethical system originating in ancient China, founded by Confucius (551–479 BCE), a thinker and educator from the state of Lu (present-day Shandong province) who lived during the Spring and Autumn Period of the Zhou Dynasty. The philosophy emerged from Confucius's teachings on morality, virtue, social hierarchy, governance, and ritual propriety. Rather than focusing on supernatural or religious dimensions, Confucianism emphasizes practical ethics, proper conduct, and social harmony through adherence to hierarchical relationships: ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder-younger, and friend-friend. Central concepts include *ren* (humaneness/benevolence), *li* (ritual propriety/etiquette), *yi* (righteousness), *zhi* (wisdom), and *xin* (trustworthiness).

After Confucius's death, his teachings were compiled into texts like the *Analects* and developed by disciples into a systematic philosophy. Later thinkers, particularly Mencius and Xunzi, expanded and debated Confucian ideas. During the Han Dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE), Confucianism became the state ideology and the foundation for the imperial examination system, creating a scholar-official class selected through rigorous testing of Confucian texts. This system persisted for nearly 2,000 years, shaping Chinese governance, education, and intellectual culture. Confucianism also spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, becoming a pan-East Asian philosophical tradition. The philosophy experienced periods of suppression, most notably during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), when the [Communist Party of China](../government-politics/communist-party-china.md) criticized Confucius as a feudal thinker. However, in recent decades, the Chinese government has rehabilitated Confucius as a cultural symbol, establishing more than 500 Confucius Institutes globally to promote Chinese language and culture.

## Historical Origins and the Life of Confucius

Confucius was born around 551 BCE in the state of Lu into a family of minor nobility that had fallen into poverty. According to traditional accounts, he lost his father at age three and was raised by his mother. His given name was Kong Qiu; the Latinized "Confucius" derives from the honorific *Kong Fuzi* (Master Kong), coined by Jesuit missionaries in the 16th century.

Confucius lived during an era of political fragmentation and chronic warfare known as the Spring and Autumn Period (771–476 BCE), when the Zhou royal house had lost effective authority and dozens of feudal states competed for dominance. He diagnosed the era's turbulence as a moral crisis rooted in the abandonment of *li* (propriety and ritual), the Zhou dynasty's traditional framework for social order. His solution was to revive and systematize the ethical content of Zhou rites rather than rely on law or coercion.

Confucius served briefly as an official in Lu, including a stint reportedly as Minister of Crime around 501 BCE, but grew frustrated with the state's rulers and embarked on a thirteen-year journey through the states of Wei, Chen, Cai, and others, seeking a ruler receptive to his ideas. None proved willing to implement his program. He returned to Lu around 483 BCE and devoted his final years to editing classical texts and teaching. By his death in 479 BCE, he had accumulated some 3,000 students, of whom 72 were credited as particularly close disciples.

## Core Concepts

**Ren (仁) — Humaneness / Benevolence**  
Ren is the cardinal virtue in Confucian thought, broadly translatable as humaneness, benevolence, or moral goodness. In the *Analects*, Confucius describes ren as loving others and as requiring constant, effortful cultivation. It is both the source of all particular virtues and their integration: a person of ren treats others with empathy, generosity, and care. The *Golden Rule* formulation Confucius articulates — "Do not impose on others what you yourself do not want" (*Analects* 15.24) — anticipates parallel ethical maxims in other traditions.

**Li (禮) — Ritual Propriety / Etiquette**  
*Li* encompasses ritual, ceremony, manners, and social norms governing behavior in all contexts, from ancestral rites and court ceremony to everyday courtesies. Confucius held that internalizing *li* shapes moral character; external form and inner virtue were inseparable. Observance of *li* expressed and reinforced the hierarchical relationships that Confucians considered the natural structure of society.

**Yi (義) — Righteousness / Morality**  
*Yi* is moral rightness — doing what is appropriate in a given situation. It contrasts with self-interest and profit (*li*), and Confucians prized subordinating personal gain to moral duty.

**Zhi (智) — Wisdom / Moral Knowledge**  
The capacity to distinguish right from wrong, recognize virtue, and apply principles correctly. Mencius later counted *zhi* among four innate moral sprouts present in all humans.

**Xin (信) — Faithfulness / Trustworthiness**  
Integrity in speech and action; the alignment of words with deeds. Considered essential to both personal character and effective governance.

**The Five Relationships (Wu Lun)**  
Confucianism structures society around five hierarchical dyads: ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder brother–younger brother, and friend-friend. Four of five are hierarchical, though all entail mutual obligations; even the ruler owes subjects benevolent governance, and the father owes the son moral instruction.

## Rival Thinkers and the Confucian Tradition

**Mencius (372–289 BCE)** extended Confucius's ethics in an optimistic direction, arguing that human nature is fundamentally good: all people are born with four moral sprouts (*duan*) — compassion, shame, deference, and right-wrong discrimination — that correspond to the four virtues of ren, yi, li, and zhi. Moral failure results from environmental damage to those sprouts, not inherent wickedness. Mencius also developed a proto-social contract theory, holding that a ruler who fails his people forfeits the Mandate of Heaven and may legitimately be overthrown.

**Xunzi (310–235 BCE)** took the opposite view: human nature is originally morally neutral or inclined toward self-interest, and virtue is achieved only through education, effort, and the civilizing discipline of ritual. Xunzi's naturalistic and rationalistic emphasis influenced the Legalist tradition; two of his students, Han Fei and Li Si, became architects of Qin Legalism. Despite their disagreement on human nature, both Mencius and Xunzi accepted the Confucian ethical framework.

The *Thirteen Classics* codified over successive dynasties form the canonical textual foundation of Confucianism, including the *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Doctrine of the Mean*, *Great Learning*, *Book of Rites*, and six other classical texts.

## State Confucianism and the Imperial Examination System

In 136 BCE, Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty designated Confucianism the official state ideology, establishing the Imperial Academy (*Taixue*) to train officials in the Five Classics. The decision made Confucian learning the prerequisite for government service, institutionalizing the link between moral cultivation and political authority that Confucius had theorized.

The imperial examination system (*keju*) formalized this connection. Inaugurated in its recognizable form under the Sui Dynasty (581–618 CE), the examinations tested candidates exhaustively on classical Confucian texts. The Tang and Song dynasties refined the system; by the Song (960–1279 CE), examinations had become the dominant path to officialdom, opening service theoretically to any male subject regardless of birth. The exams required mastery of the *Four Books* (identified by Zhu Xi) — the *Great Learning*, *Doctrine of the Mean*, *Analects*, and *Mencius* — and composition in regulated verse forms. The system persisted until its abolition in 1905, a period of nearly 1,300 years.

## Neo-Confucianism: The Song and Ming Renaissance

Between the 9th and 17th centuries CE, Chinese scholars undertook a systematic synthesis of Confucian ethics with Buddhist metaphysics and Daoist cosmology, producing what Western scholars call Neo-Confucianism.

**Zhu Xi (1130–1200 CE)** was the dominant figure of the Cheng-Zhu school. He developed a comprehensive metaphysics organized around two principles: *li* (principle or pattern — here a different homophone from ritual *li*) and *qi* (material force). Every thing embodies principle; the task of moral cultivation is to *gewu* (investigate things) to discern their principle and thereby extend moral knowledge. Zhu Xi's commentaries on the *Four Books* became the authorized curriculum for the examination system from 1313 until 1905, giving him unparalleled influence on Chinese education for six centuries.

**Wang Yangming (1472–1529 CE)** founded the rival Lu-Wang school, emphasizing the unity of knowledge and action (*zhixing heyi*) and the innate moral faculty of *liangzhi* (moral intuition). Wang held that principle is not external but resides within the mind, and that moral cultivation consists in activating the mind's innate knowledge rather than investigating external objects. His thought influenced the Japanese samurai tradition, particularly through the Ōyōmei school, and later inspired reformist and revolutionary thinkers in both China and Japan.

## Spread Across East Asia

Confucianism spread beyond China through trade, diplomacy, and the adoption of Chinese writing systems and institutional models.

**Korea**: Confucianism entered the Korean peninsula during the Three Kingdoms period (c. 300–668 CE). Under the Joseon Dynasty (1392–1897), Neo-Confucianism became the state ideology, shaping governance, education, the examination system, social hierarchy, and family ritual in ways that profoundly mark Korean culture today.

**Japan**: Confucian texts arrived in Japan via Korea in the 4th and 5th centuries CE. In the Tokugawa period (1603–1868), the Hayashi school of Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism provided the official philosophy of the shogunate. Confucian concepts of social harmony, hierarchy, loyalty, and education shaped Tokugawa governance and informed the *bushido* (warrior code) ideology.

**Vietnam**: Confucian influence followed Chinese administrative incorporation of northern Vietnam and persisted after Vietnamese independence. The Vietnamese imperial examination system, modeled on the Chinese, ran from 1075 to 1919 CE.

## The Cultural Revolution and Rehabilitation

The [Communist Party of China](../government-politics/communist-party-china.md) initially tolerated Confucianism but launched the "Criticize Lin, Criticize Confucius" (*Pi Lin Pi Kong*) campaign in 1973–74 during the Cultural Revolution, portraying Confucius as a defender of feudal hierarchy and a symbol of backward tradition. Confucian temples were vandalized, scholars persecuted, and classical learning suppressed.

From the 1980s onward, as Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms proceeded and the party sought cultural legitimacy, the official attitude shifted toward rehabilitation. By the 2000s, Confucius had become central to the government's "harmonious society" (*hexie shehui*) discourse. In 2004, the first Confucius Institute opened in Seoul, South Korea; by 2023 more than 500 had opened worldwide, though several Western universities subsequently closed them over concerns about academic freedom.

## Legacy and Contemporary Relevance

Confucianism's influence on East Asian societies is pervasive and ongoing. Emphasis on education as the path to social mobility remains characteristic of Chinese, Korean, and Japanese societies. Concepts of collective harmony over individual assertion, filial piety, and respect for authority continue to shape family structure, workplace culture, and political values. Scholars debate whether "Confucian capitalism" — the convergence of market economics with these social values — contributed to the rapid development of South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and eventually mainland China. The relationship between Confucian tradition and the [Communist Party of China](../government-politics/communist-party-china.md)'s model of development represents a continuing negotiation between ancient ethical frameworks and modern political economy.
