# Buddhism in China

**Buddhism in China** represents the integration and transformation of the Buddhist tradition within Chinese civilization over two millennia. Entering China via the Silk Road during the Han Dynasty, Buddhism was adapted, reinterpreted, and synthesized with Confucianism and Taoism, becoming a fundamental pillar of Chinese intellectual and spiritual life that shaped philosophy, art, literature, medicine, and daily religious practice across all social classes.

## Arrival and Early Integration (1st–5th centuries CE)

Buddhism arrived in China gradually during the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, brought by monks and merchants traveling the Silk Road from Central Asia and India. A Han court record, the *Mou-tzu li-huo lun* (c. 200 CE), describes Buddhism already present in the capital Luoyang by the Eastern Han period (25–220 CE). Emperor Ming (r. 57–75 CE) reportedly sent envoys to India after a dream of a golden figure; they returned with the monks Kashyapa-matanga and Dharmaratna (竺法蘭, Zhu Falan), who translated the *Sutra of Forty-Two Chapters* at the White Horse Temple (白馬寺) in Luoyang—traditionally considered the first Buddhist monastery in China, established c. 68 CE.

Early systematic translators included **An Shigao** (active c. 148–170 CE), a Parthian prince-turned-monk who rendered over 30 Hinayana texts into Chinese, and **Lokaksema** (active c. 167–186 CE), who produced the first Chinese translations of Mahayana sutras, including the *Pratyutpanna Samadhi Sutra*. These translations required Chinese neologisms: Buddhist concepts were initially conveyed through Taoist vocabulary (a practice called *geyi*, 格義, "matching meanings"), enabling Chinese audiences to grasp ideas like *nirvana* through the Taoist concept of *wu* (non-being).

The most prolific early translator was **Kumarajiva** (344–413 CE), a Kuchan monk brought to Chang'an in 401 CE. Working with a team of hundreds of scholars, Kumarajiva translated over 70 texts including the *Lotus Sutra*, the *Diamond Sutra*, and the *Vimalakirti Sutra*, establishing a literary standard that shaped Chinese Buddhism for centuries.

## The Pilgrim-Scholars and Indian Connection

The 4th through 7th centuries CE produced a series of Chinese monks who traveled to India seeking original scriptures, validating local traditions against their source. **Faxian** (法顯, c. 337–422 CE) journeyed overland to India c. 399 CE, spending 15 years collecting Vinaya texts and returning by sea via Sri Lanka, leaving a famous travelogue. His journey demonstrated that Chinese Buddhist communities were eager to remedy gaps in canonical transmission.

The most celebrated pilgrim was **Xuanzang** (玄奘, 602–664 CE), who traveled to India during the Tang Dynasty, studying for over a decade at **Nalanda University** (the world's foremost Buddhist academy, located in present-day Bihar) and returning in 645 CE with 657 Sanskrit texts loaded on 20 horses. Emperor Taizong gave Xuanzang a state welcome and established a translation bureau at Chang'an's Ci'en Temple (慈恩寺), where Xuanzang produced authoritative translations of 75 texts and founded the **Consciousness-Only School** (唯識宗, Yogacara), introducing sophisticated Indian epistemology and psychology into Chinese thought. The 7th-century **Wild Goose Pagoda** (大雁塔) in Xi'an, built to house Xuanzang's translations, survives today as an icon of Tang Buddhist patronage.

**Yijing** (義淨, 635–713 CE) followed later, studying at Nalanda and at Srivijaya (Sumatra) before returning with 400 texts in 695 CE. His accounts of Buddhist practices across Southeast Asia provide invaluable documentation of 7th-century Buddhist geography.

## Golden Age During the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE)

The Tang Dynasty represented Buddhism's golden age in China. Five Tang emperors—especially **Taizong** (r. 626–649 CE), **Gaozong** (r. 649–683 CE), and the empress **Wu Zetian** (r. 690–705 CE)—were active patrons. Wu Zetian, China's only female emperor, promoted Buddhism explicitly to legitimate her rule, commissioning the colossal Vairocana Buddha at **Longmen Grottoes** (龍門石窟) near Luoyang—a 17-meter-tall sandstone sculpture carved c. 672–676 CE that remains among the greatest Buddhist monuments in the world. She also sponsored the translation of the *Avatamsaka Sutra* (Flower Garland Sutra), the doctrinal foundation of Huayan Buddhism.

The Tang court employed hundreds of Buddhist monks as translators, teachers, and court advisors. Monasteries numbered in the thousands; by one estimate, there were over 4,600 major Buddhist temples across the empire during the dynasty's peak. Buddhist festivals attracted enormous popular participation; the **Ullambana Festival** (盂蘭盆節), derived from the story of the monk Mulian rescuing his mother from hell, became one of China's most beloved celebrations and persists today.

During the Tang era, major schools of Chinese Buddhism crystallized:

- **Pure Land Buddhism** (淨土宗): Founded by Huiyuan (334–416 CE) and systematized by Shandao (613–681 CE), Pure Land taught that devotion to Amitabha Buddha (阿彌陀佛) and recitation of his name (*nianfo*) would secure rebirth in the Western Pure Land, a paradise from which enlightenment was easily achieved. Its simplicity made it the most widely practiced form of Buddhism in China.
- **Chan Buddhism** (禪宗): Attributed traditionally to the Indian monk Bodhidharma (達摩, c. 5th–6th century CE), Chan emphasized meditation (*dhyana*), koans (paradoxical riddles), and direct transmission beyond scripture. **Huineng** (638–713 CE), the Sixth Patriarch, articulated sudden enlightenment over gradual cultivation in the *Platform Sutra*, creating a distinctively Chinese tradition that spread to Korea (Seon), Japan (Zen), and Vietnam (Thiền).
- **Huayan Buddhism** (華嚴宗): Systematized by Fazang (643–712 CE), Huayan presented reality as a vast jeweled net (*Indra's net*) in which each phenomenon reflects every other—a vision of total interdependence that resonated with Confucian cosmology and influenced Neo-Confucian metaphysics.
- **Tiantai Buddhism** (天台宗): Founded by Zhiyi (538–597 CE) on Mount Tiantai, this school organized all Buddhist teachings into a hierarchical system ranking the *Lotus Sutra* as highest. Tiantai's emphasis on textual interpretation influenced Buddhist scholarship for centuries.

### The Dunhuang Cave Libraries

The **Mogao Caves** (莫高窟) at Dunhuang, a Silk Road oasis in present-day Gansu Province, constitute the greatest archive of medieval Chinese Buddhist art and manuscript culture. Carved between the 4th and 14th centuries, the 492 surviving caves contain over 45,000 square meters of murals and thousands of sculptures spanning multiple dynasties. In 1900, a Taoist monk discovered a sealed chamber (Cave 17) containing approximately 50,000 manuscripts dating primarily from the 5th through 10th centuries—including the world's oldest printed book, a woodblock-printed *Diamond Sutra* dated 868 CE, now in the British Library. The Dunhuang manuscripts illuminate Buddhist practice, Silk Road commerce, multilingual communities, and the daily life of medieval China.

## Synthesis with Confucianism and Taoism

Rather than replacing Confucianism or Taoism, Buddhism synthesized with both traditions, creating a cosmopolitan spiritual ecosystem that the Ming-dynasty philosopher Lin Zhao'en (1517–1598) called the "Three Teachings in One" (三教合一). Confucianism provided ethics, governance, and social structure; Taoism offered naturalism, mysticism, and harmony with the cosmos; Buddhism added metaphysics, psychological depth, and paths to transcendence. Educated Chinese often drew from all three traditions depending on context—Confucian in public service, Taoist in retreat, Buddhist in meditation and devotion to the afterlife.

Buddhist concepts of *sunyata* (emptiness) and *anicca* (impermanence) enriched Taoist naturalism. Chan Buddhism's emphasis on direct experience and spontaneity aligned closely with Taoist *wu wei* (non-action). Confucian virtue and filial piety found Buddhist parallels in the Bodhisattva ideal of compassionate service to all beings. The Buddhist concept of *bardo* (intermediate state between death and rebirth) enriched Chinese ancestor veneration rituals.

**Neo-Confucianism**, systematized during the Song Dynasty (960–1279 CE) by Zhu Xi (1130–1200 CE), explicitly incorporated Buddhist psychology, epistemology, and metaphysics into Confucian thought—while often downplaying this Buddhist debt. The Neo-Confucian concept of *li* (principle) owes much to Huayan cosmology.

## Decline, Persecution, and Persistence

The **Huichang Persecution** of 845 CE under Emperor Wuzong proved the most devastating anti-Buddhist campaign in Chinese history. Over four years, approximately 4,600 major monasteries and 40,000 smaller temples were destroyed, around 260,500 monks and nuns were laicized and forced to return to secular life, and vast monastic landholdings were confiscated. The persecution, motivated partly by Confucian criticism of "foreign" religion and partly by fiscal desire to reclaim tax-exempt monastic lands, severely damaged Buddhist institutional infrastructure. Wuzong died the following year (846 CE), and his successor Emperor Xuanzong immediately reversed the proscription, but the damage was lasting. Several schools—including Huayan, Yogacara, and Tiantai—never fully recovered their pre-Tang scholarly vitality.

Chan Buddhism and Pure Land Buddhism survived the Huichang Persecution more intact, partly because they depended less on large monasteries and institutional libraries. These two traditions dominated Chinese Buddhism from the Song Dynasty onward, often blending into a syncretic "Chan-Pure Land" practice combining meditation with *nianfo* recitation.

During the **Ming Dynasty** (1368–1644 CE), Buddhism remained socially visible but politically secondary to Confucianism. Emperor Taizu (r. 1368–1398 CE) initially used Buddhist and Taoist religious legitimacy but later subordinated religious institutions to state supervision, establishing a Bureau of Buddhist Registry that registered and regulated monks. Despite restrictions, Ming Buddhism produced important thinkers including Yunqi Zhuhong (1535–1615 CE), who synthesized Chan and Pure Land while emphasizing lay ethics, and Hanshan Deqing (1546–1623 CE), who promoted a Buddhist revival integrating all three teachings.

The **Qing Dynasty** (1644–1912 CE) offered complex relations with Buddhism. The Manchu emperors patronized **Tibetan Buddhism** (Vajrayana) as a tool of political influence over Mongol and Tibetan populations; the Dalai Lama was received in Beijing, and the **Yonghe Temple** (雍和宮) in Beijing was converted into a major Tibetan Buddhist monastery in 1744 CE. Han Chinese Buddhism continued under state supervision but without the same imperial enthusiasm.

## Cultural and Philosophical Legacy

Buddhism's impact on Chinese civilization was immense and pervasive:

- **Philosophy**: Buddhist metaphysics, logic (including Dignaga's formal logic, introduced c. 5th century CE), and epistemology enriched Chinese thought. Neo-Confucianism's core concepts of principle (*li*) and mind (*xin*) are deeply indebted to Buddhist Huayan and Yogacara philosophy.
- **Art and Architecture**: Buddhist temples and pagodas became defining features of Chinese landscapes. Rock-cut cave complexes at **Yungang** (云岡石窟, 5th century CE), **Longmen** (龍門石窟, 5th–8th century CE), and **Dunhuang** (敦煌莫高窟, 4th–14th century CE) contain China's greatest pre-modern artistic achievements. The pagoda form—adapted from the Indian stupa—became an iconic Chinese architectural element.
- **Literature**: Buddhist themes pervade Chinese poetry, fiction, and drama. The 16th-century novel *Journey to the West* (西遊記), based on Xuanzang's pilgrimage, is one of China's four great classical novels. Tang poetry by Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, and Han Shan is suffused with Buddhist aesthetics.
- **Printing Technology**: Buddhism was the primary driver of early printing in China. The need to reproduce sutras in vast quantities motivated woodblock printing; the world's oldest printed book (the *Diamond Sutra*, 868 CE) is a Buddhist text.
- **Medicine**: Buddhist monasteries transmitted Indian Ayurvedic medicine to China, enriching the Chinese medical corpus. Monastic pharmacopeias documented hundreds of medicinal plants.
- **Calendar and Astronomy**: Buddhist monks from India brought astronomical knowledge that influenced Tang calendar reform.

## Modern Period

During the 20th century, Buddhism faced unprecedented challenges from secularization, Republican-era anti-superstition campaigns, and Communist anti-religious policies. The 1949 establishment of the People's Republic of China initially limited Buddhist institutions sharply; the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) destroyed thousands of temples and forced monks and nuns to disrobe. However, the post-1978 reform era saw partial recovery: the Chinese Buddhist Association (founded 1953) resumed activities, monasteries were rebuilt and reopened, and Buddhist tourism and scholarship revived.

Today, China hosts an estimated 185–244 million Buddhist practitioners (various surveys, 2010s), though many combine Buddhist practice with other traditions. Major pilgrimage sites including **Mount Wutai** (五台山), **Mount Emei** (峨眉山), **Mount Jiuhua** (九華山), and **Mount Putuo** (普陀山)—traditionally associated with the four great Bodhisattvas—attract millions of visitors annually. Buddhism remains a living presence in Chinese culture, art, and daily life.
