# Filial Piety (Xiao)

**Category**: Culture & Society
**Key figures**: Confucius, Mencius, Zhu Xi, the Twenty-Four Paragons of Filial Piety

## Summary

Filial piety (孝, *xiao*) is the cardinal Confucian virtue of respect, gratitude, care, and duty toward parents and ancestors. More than a personal moral quality, *xiao* became the foundation of social hierarchy, family structure, governance, and ethics in Chinese civilization for over two thousand years. Confucius taught that filial piety was the root of virtue and the basis of civilized order; without *xiao*, neither social harmony nor political legitimacy could exist. The concept extends beyond obedience to encompass deep reverence for parental authority, support in old age and death, proper mourning rituals, ancestor veneration, and the perpetuation of family lineage through male progeny—making it not merely an individual virtue but a systemic principle organizing Chinese society.

In the *Analects*, Confucius declares that filial piety and respect for elders are the root of moral excellence (*ren*), and that a truly filial son would not disobey his father. Mencius elaborated the concept, arguing that filial piety reflects the natural human emotions of love and gratitude toward parents, and that rulers who treat their subjects as children show the filial virtue extended to governance. The *Classic of Filial Piety* (*Xiaojing*), which reached its canonical form under the Han Dynasty, prescribed filial conduct across social ranks—from the emperor down to commoners—and became a core text of the Confucian curriculum studied for the imperial examinations over the centuries that followed. During the imperial periods, especially the Tang and Song dynasties, filial piety was enforced through law; sons who failed to support elderly parents could face punishment, and those who struck parents faced severe penalties including death.

## Origins and Classical Sources

The ideograph for *xiao* (孝) is itself instructive: it depicts an old man supported by a young person below—a visual emblem of the generational compact. The concept appears in the earliest Chinese texts. The *Book of Documents* (*Shujing*), traditionally attributed to the sage-kings of high antiquity, praises rulers who honor their parents as the supreme exemplars of virtue. The *Book of Songs* (*Shijing*, c. 1000–600 BCE) contains odes lamenting a son's failure to care for his parents as among the gravest offenses.

Confucius (551–479 BCE) placed *xiao* at the core of his moral philosophy. In the *Analects* (1.2), his disciple You Zi declares: "A man of filial piety and fraternal submission will rarely be found contending against his superiors." Confucius himself described the highest form of filial conduct not as blind obedience but as *gentle remonstrance*—a son should discreetly counsel a parent away from error, continuing to serve even if unheeded, without public reproach. This nuanced position allowed Confucianism to hold filial piety compatible with moral autonomy.

Mencius (372–289 BCE) ranked *xiao* among the four moral sprouts inherent in human nature, alongside *ren* (benevolence), *yi* (righteousness), and *li* (ritual propriety). He drew the famous parallel between family love and kingly governance: "The root of the empire is the state; the root of the state is the family" (*Mencius*, 4A.5). The filial household thus became the political microcosm.

## The Classic of Filial Piety (*Xiaojing*)

The *Classic of Filial Piety* (*Xiaojing*), probably compiled by Confucius's disciple Zengzi's school in the late fourth or early third century BCE and canonized during the Han, became the single most widely memorized text in the Chinese literary tradition. Written in a dialogue form between Confucius and Zengzi, it systematizes filial obligations according to social rank:

- **The Son of Heaven (emperor):** maintains the realm, honors the cosmic order, and extends filial reverence to all under heaven.
- **Feudal lords:** govern their territories as a father governs his household.
- **High ministers and officials:** serve without moral compromise, lest they disgrace their family.
- **Ordinary gentlemen:** preserve their family reputation through exemplary conduct.
- **Commoners:** work hard, support parents in old age, and avoid harm to the body given by their parents—for the body is held in trust.

The *Xiaojing*'s famous dictum—"Our bodies, every hair and bit of skin, are received from our parents; we dare not injure or scar them. This is the beginning of filial piety"—embedded *xiao* in the very concept of bodily integrity. Tattooing or disfigurement could be impious violations; suicide in the ordinary sense contradicted filial duty.

Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (r. 712–756) personally wrote a commentary on the *Xiaojing* and ordered it inscribed in stone at the imperial academy, signaling its permanent canonical status.

## Imperial Institutionalization

During the Han Dynasty (206 BCE – 220 CE), *xiao* was institutionalized at the highest levels of state. Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE) established Confucianism as the official state ideology, and the imperial examination system—though not fully bureaucratized until the Sui and Tang dynasties—began incorporating the *Xiaojing* as a required text. Han emperors used posthumous temple names incorporating *xiao* (e.g., Emperor Xiaohui, Emperor Xiaowu) to signal their filial virtue.

Tang legal codes (the *Tang lü shuyi*, 653 CE) enumerated "Ten Abominations" (*shi'e*), the most serious crimes under Chinese law. Among these, three directly concerned filial piety:

- **Unfilial conduct** (*buxiao*): failing to support parents financially, filing false accusations against relatives, or failing to observe mourning properly.
- **Discord** (*bumu*): harming members of one's own clan.
- **Lack of righteousness** (*buyi*): killing one's official superior (analogous to filial subordination).

Sons who struck parents faced beheading; those who killed parents faced the most agonizing forms of execution—*lingchi* (death by a thousand cuts) was reserved partly for this crime. Magistrates throughout the imperial period were expected to hear cases of unfilial conduct and to publicize exemplary cases of filial devotion.

The Qing Dynasty's *Sacred Edict* (*Shengyu guangxun*), originally issued by the Kangxi Emperor in 1670 and expanded by the Yongzheng Emperor in 1724, directed local officials to read out sixteen moral maxims—the first two explicitly commanding filial piety and respect for kin—to commoner audiences twice monthly in village assemblies. The Qing state thus made *xiao* a matter of ongoing public ritual performance.

## Mourning Rituals

The mourning system (*sang*) was one of the most elaborate institutional expressions of filial piety. The *Rites of Zhou* (*Zhouli*) and the *Book of Rites* (*Liji*) prescribed mourning duration and dress according to one's relationship to the deceased:

- **Parents:** Three years (in practice, 27 months, counted by the traditional Chinese method). During this period, an official was expected to resign his post and return home to observe full mourning—wearing rough hempen garments, abstaining from meat, wine, and music, sleeping on a mat with a clod of earth as a pillow.
- **Grandparents, siblings, and spouse:** One year of mourning.
- **More distant relatives:** Successively reduced periods down to three months.

Officials who failed to observe proper mourning for parents were subject to dismissal and legal sanction. The Song and Ming dynasties were particularly strict: officials who hid a parent's death to avoid leaving their posts were severely censured. Emperor Xuanzong of Tang dismissed two senior ministers for playing music during their mourning period—a public statement that imperial governance itself was subordinate to filial obligation.

Ancestor veneration—maintaining the family altar, offering seasonal sacrifices of food, incense, and paper money, and consulting ancestors before important decisions—extended filial duty beyond death indefinitely. The gravest threat a man could face was dying without male heirs, leaving ancestral rites to lapse.

## The Twenty-Four Paragons of Filial Piety

The *Twenty-Four Paragons of Filial Piety* (*Ershisi xiao*), compiled in the Yuan Dynasty (c. 1260–1368) by the scholar Guo Jujing, became the most widely circulated moral instruction text in late imperial China, appearing in primers, illustrated woodblock prints, painted screens, porcelain decorations, and theatrical performances. Each story features an exemplary son (or, in a few cases, daughter or daughter-in-law) performing an extraordinary act to honor parents. Selected examples:

- **Shun the Great** (legendary sage-king): Despite abuse by his father and stepmother, Shun continued to serve them with reverence, prompting Heaven to send elephants to plow his fields. His virtue earned him the succession to the throne of the mythic Emperor Yao.
- **Cao E** (Han Dynasty): At age 14, threw herself into the Cao River to retrieve her drowned father's body, becoming a cult figure of filial devotion.
- **Wang Xiang** (Wei period, c. 185–269 CE): Lay bare-chested on frozen ice in winter to melt it with his body heat, catching the fresh fish his ailing stepmother craved.
- **Wu Meng** (Jin Dynasty, c. 265–420 CE): Being too poor to afford mosquito netting, he slept naked beside his father each night so the mosquitoes would bite him rather than disturb his father's rest.
- **Lao Laizi** (Spring and Autumn period, traditional dating): Though 70 years old, dressed in children's colorful clothes and frolicked before his aged parents to cheer them, pretending he was still a child to ease their sense of mortality.

Modern readers frequently find some of these stories extreme to the point of harmful, and they were criticized within the tradition itself. The writer Lu Xun, in a 1926 essay on the *Twenty-Four Paragons of Filial Piety* (collected in *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk*), subjected these tales to sharp irony, arguing that an ethic requiring self-destruction to honor parents could not be the basis for a healthy modern society.

## Neo-Confucian Systematization

Zhu Xi (1130–1200), the foremost philosopher of Neo-Confucianism, embedded filial piety within a comprehensive metaphysical framework. For Zhu Xi, the natural love of a child for its parent was the phenomenal expression of the universal moral principle (*li*) inherent in all things. The family, organized by *xiao*, was the training ground for the moral cultivation (*gewu zhizhi*) that produced virtuous officials and eventually wise rulers. The *Great Learning* (*Daxue*)—one of the Four Books Zhu Xi designated as the core Confucian canon—explicitly linked moral self-cultivation, household regulation, state governance, and universal peace in a hierarchical chain where filial piety in the household was the indispensable first step.

Wang Yangming (1472–1529) introduced a more internalized reading: filial piety was not primarily about external ritual performance but about a direct moral intuition (*liangzhi*, innate knowledge) that every person can access. His interpretation made *xiao* more portable and independent of elaborate ritual apparatus, though he never denied its fundamental importance.

## Significance

Filial piety stands as perhaps the most pervasive and enduring value in Chinese civilization. It shaped family law, inheritance customs, mourning practices, and the emperor's claim to legitimacy—dynasties that failed to honor *xiao* were deemed to have lost the Mandate of Heaven. Throughout Chinese history, the *xiao* principle reinforced patrilineal succession, elder authority, and respect for the deceased, creating social cohesion across generations. The virtue appeared in historical narratives and moral exempla: the *Twenty-Four Paragons of Filial Piety* became a literary and educational touchstone, with stories of sons who sacrificed themselves or endured extreme hardship to honor parents, inspiring imitation and moral cultivation.

In the modern era, the Communist Party initially attacked filial piety as a feudal relic during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), encouraging youth to challenge parental authority in the name of revolutionary fervor. Red Guards publicly humiliated parents; children were encouraged to denounce "class-enemy" relatives. Yet the virtue never fully disappeared from Chinese consciousness; contemporary Chinese society, despite decades of communist rule and rapid urbanization, still emphasizes filial duty, elder care, and ancestor veneration. The People's Republic of China passed a revised Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly in 2013, which legally requires adult children to visit aging parents "often"—a striking statutory reinstatement of *xiao*. The concept thus bridges ancient imperial China and the modern People's Republic, revealing how deeply *xiao* is woven into Chinese identity and social practice.
