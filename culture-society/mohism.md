# Mohism

**Mohism** (*mojia*, 墨家, "the school of Mo"), founded by Mozi (墨翟, also known as Mo Di, c. 470–391 BCE) in the Warring States period, was one of the most significant Chinese philosophical schools and the primary intellectual rival to Confucianism for over two centuries. Distinguished by its doctrine of *jian'ai* (兼愛, "impartial care" or "universal love"), its consequentialist ethics, its pioneering logical theory, its proto-scientific inquiries into optics and mechanics, and its highly disciplined organizational structure, Mohism offered a radically different vision of the good society from that of the Confucians. Though ultimately suppressed and eclipsed when the Han Dynasty adopted Confucianism as state ideology after 136 BCE, Mohism remains one of the great unrealized alternatives in the history of civilization — a system that, had historical circumstances differed, might have shaped Chinese and world philosophy along profoundly different lines.

## Mozi: Biography and Context

Mozi was born in the small state of Song (in modern Shandong/Henan), probably around 470 BCE, into what appears from the textual evidence to have been an artisan or craftsman background. Some scholars, noting his practical engineering knowledge and his movement's stress on manual labor and anti-aristocratic values, suggest he may have been trained as a carpenter or woodworker — a tradition that contrasts pointedly with Confucius's aristocratic scholarly origins. Whether or not this is accurate, Mozi clearly came from a different social stratum than the Confucian sages, and his philosophy reflects that difference.

Mozi's active period (c. 440–390 BCE) coincided with intensifying interstate warfare as the great states of Qi, Chu, Wei, Han, Zhao, Yan, and Qin competed with increasing ruthlessness for territorial dominance. The old feudal order of the Zhou Dynasty — with its elaborate ceremonial culture, hereditary aristocracy, and ritual warfare — was disintegrating. This context of civilian suffering, military devastation, and political instability became the crucible of Mohist thought. Unlike Confucius, who looked back to the early Zhou as a golden age to be restored through ritual and moral exemplarity, Mozi addressed the immediate, practical problems of his time with blunt consequentialist urgency.

The *Mozi* text that survives comprises 53 chapters (of an original 71), organized into groups discussing different aspects of Mohist doctrine. The text represents multiple generations of Mohist authorship: the earlier chapters contain Mozi's own sayings and arguments; the later "Mohist Canons" (*mojing*, 墨經) — chapters 40–45 — appear to reflect later, more technically sophisticated Mohist thinkers of the third century BCE.

## Core Doctrines: The Ten Theses

Mohist doctrine is traditionally organized around ten central positions, each typically presented twice in the *Mozi* (in shorter and longer versions):

**1. Impartial Care** (*jian'ai*, 兼愛) — The central principle that individuals and states should care for the welfare of all people, not merely their own kin or interests. Mozi's argument was not that familial love is wrong but that it must be extended through cultivation to encompass all people equitably. He posed this in practical terms: if everyone practiced mutual care — each person regarding others' families as their own — then warfare, theft, and exploitation would cease, because no one would harm those they genuinely cared for. Confucian critics, especially Mencius (372–289 BCE), accused Mohism of abolishing natural family bonds in favor of an abstract universal caring — a misreading that the Mohist texts explicitly address.

**2. Against Aggressive Warfare** (*fei gong*, 非攻) — Mohists developed an extensive consequentialist critique of offensive warfare. Military aggression consumes vast resources, kills soldiers and civilians, destroys crops and cities, and produces no net benefit to the human population as a whole — even the "winner" suffers enormous costs. Mozi distinguished sharply between aggressive war (which he condemned) and defensive war (which he actively organized and supported). The famous story of Mozi's confrontation with the master engineer Gongshu Pan (公輸盤), also known as Lu Ban (魯班), encapsulates this position.

**3. Elevating the Worthy** (*shang xian*, 尚賢) — Meritocratic appointment of the most capable and virtuous individuals to positions of authority, regardless of birth or status. This was a direct challenge to the hereditary aristocracy of the Zhou feudal order. Mohists argued that social disorder arises when the incompetent govern; the remedy is to identify and elevate those with genuine ability and virtue, regardless of family background.

**4. Exalting Unity** (*shang tong*, 尚同) — Political authority requires unity of standards and commands. Mozi advocated a hierarchical political structure in which subordinates conform their standards of right and wrong to those of their superiors, ultimately conforming to the will of Heaven (*tian*). This position, which might seem authoritarian, was intended to prevent the anarchic warfare that arises from incompatible value systems in different states.

**5. Economy of Expenditure** (*jie yong*, 節用) — Resources should be deployed only in ways that benefit the people. Lavish court expenditure, elaborate rituals, and extravagant gifts consume resources without producing human welfare. Mohist communities themselves practiced notable austerity.

**6. Frugality in Funerals** (*jie zang*, 節葬) — Elaborate burial practices, including the burial of valuable goods and the sacrifice of slaves or horses, waste resources that could benefit the living. Mozi advocated simple burials.

**7. Condemnation of Music** (*fei yue*, 非樂) — Not a condemnation of music per se, but of the elaborate, expensive musical performances maintained by aristocratic courts. Resources spent on court music are resources not spent feeding the hungry or defending the poor.

**8. Against Fatalism** (*fei ming*, 非命) — The doctrine that human outcomes are pre-determined (*ming*) and that effort cannot change them is dangerous, because it counsels passivity in the face of preventable suffering. Mohists insisted that effort, organization, and good policy can improve human conditions.

**9. Will of Heaven** (*tian zhi*, 天志) — The cosmic principle of Heaven's will grounds moral obligation. Heaven rewards those who promote mutual benefit and care and punishes those who pursue selfish interests. This appeal to a cosmic moral order gave Mohism a quasi-religious dimension absent from early Confucian thought.

**10. On Ghosts and Spirits** (*ming gui*, 明鬼) — Spirits and ghosts exist and can reward virtue and punish wickedness. Mozi argued that belief in active spiritual agents reinforces moral behavior.

## The Gongshu Pan Confrontation: Mohist Defense in Practice

The most famous episode in Mozi's biography illustrates the school's distinctive combination of ethical argument and practical engineering. Around 440–430 BCE, the master craftsman Gongshu Pan (Lu Ban), working for the powerful state of Chu, invented a new siege weapon — variously described as a scaling device or catapult — and offered it to the Chu king for use in an attack on the small state of Song, Mozi's homeland. Mozi, hearing of this, traveled for ten days on foot from Qi to Chu's capital (Ying, near modern Jiangling, Hubei) to intervene.

In a famous debate before the Chu king, Mozi and Gongshu Pan engaged in a mock siege. Gongshu Pan demonstrated nine different methods of attack with a model of the siege weapon; Mozi countered each of the nine attacks with a defense, demonstrating nine separate defensive counters using pieces from his belt and sash as stand-ins for fortifications. Gongshu Pan, his offensive arsenal exhausted, declared that he still had one method that Mozi had not countered — presumably, killing Mozi himself. Mozi replied that his three hundred disciples were already at Song's walls with his defensive techniques, awaiting any attack, and that killing Mozi would not extinguish the knowledge.

The king abandoned the attack. The episode became the paradigmatic story of Mohist moral suasion combined with technical competence: the philosopher who could not only argue that aggressive war was wrong but also practically prevent it.

## Mohist Logic and the Canons (*Mojing*)

The Mohist Canons, composed by later Mohists of the third century BCE, are among the most remarkable intellectual documents of ancient China. They represent a sustained attempt to systematize reasoning, language, and the natural world in ways that have no close parallel in contemporaneous Chinese thought.

**Logical theory** — Mohist logicians distinguished between different types of statements and their validity. They analyzed the conditions under which arguments succeed or fail, developed criteria for identifying analogical reasoning (*tui*, "extending"), and established that statements must be tested against observable reality and the standard of human benefit. The three-question test for any doctrine asks: Does it accord with past sages' practice? Does it accord with the perceptions of the common people? Does it produce benefit when put into practice? This triple test — historical, empirical, consequentialist — represents a sophisticated epistemological position.

**Optics** — The Mohist Canons contain surprisingly detailed descriptions of optical phenomena, including the rectilinear propagation of light, the formation of shadows, and the functioning of a pinhole camera (*xiaokong chenxiang*). One passage in the Canons describes how light passing through a small aperture inverts an image — a description of what is now known as the camera obscura effect — with geometric precision that implies experimental investigation.

**Mechanics and geometry** — The Canons contain discussions of levers, the concept of a center of gravity, the conditions for equilibrium, and geometric definitions of points, lines, and planes. These proto-scientific inquiries, pursued for practical purposes (defensive engineering, construction), anticipate formal scientific method in ways unusual for the period.

**Language and paradox** — The Mohist logicians engaged with the paradoxes of the School of Names (*mingjia*), including the famous paradox attributed to Gongsun Long (c. 325–250 BCE): "A white horse is not a horse." The Mohists developed analyses of how names refer, how similarities and differences are established, and how language can mislead — analyses that remain of interest to philosophers of language today.

## Mohist Organization and Community

One of Mohism's most distinctive features was its organizational structure. Mohists were not merely philosophers but a disciplined community united by a common mission. Each cell had a leader called the *juzi* (鉅子, "giant" or "great one"), and the movement maintained a hierarchy extending across the different Chinese states. Members were expected to subordinate personal interests to the collective good and to follow the decisions of their *juzi* — including, reportedly, to the point of accepting death in defense of a state that the Mohist community had committed to protect.

This quasi-military organizational coherence gave Mohism an effectiveness that more loosely organized philosophical schools lacked. The Mohist network could mobilize engineers, fighters, and debaters across state lines, offering their defensive services to small states threatened by aggressive neighbors. Mohist cells reportedly committed to the defense of states such as Yangcheng (陽城) even at the cost of their members' lives, a discipline that reflected the logistical consciousness of a paramilitary organization.

This same organizational coherence, however, made Mohism appear threatening to rulers seeking centralized authority. The movement's capacity to operate independently across state boundaries, to offer armed resistance to aggression, and to maintain internal discipline outside of state control made it a potential challenge to any centralizing power.

## Mohism in the Warring States Intellectual Context

During the fourth and third centuries BCE, Mohism was one of the dominant intellectual forces in Chinese thought — on a par with Confucianism in prestige and arguably more sophisticated in its logical apparatus. The evidence for this is paradoxical: the most powerful testimony to Mohism's prominence is the extensive critical attention paid to it by its rivals.

Mencius (孟子, 372–289 BCE), the second great Confucian sage, regarded Mohism as the primary threat to Confucian values and devoted significant portions of his text to criticizing *jian'ai*. His famous charge — that Mozi's doctrine of love without gradations (*ai wu cha deng*, 愛無差等) amounted to "being without a father" (*wu fu*, 無父), denying the graded obligations owed first to one's own parents — is the most memorable Confucian critique of Mohist universalism. Zhuangzi (莊子, c. 369–286 BCE), the great Daoist thinker, also engaged critically with Mohism, acknowledging its moral earnestness while finding its asceticism joyless and its universalism naively optimistic.

Xunzi (荀子, c. 313–238 BCE), the systematic Confucian philosopher, wrote two chapters specifically criticizing Mohist positions, further evidence that the school could not be ignored.

## Decline and Suppression

Mohism's influence began to wane during the late Warring States period for multiple reasons. Legalism, which adopted Mohist ideas about meritocratic governance while abandoning Mohist ethics and universalism, gained influence among rulers who found its harder-edged political realism more useful in interstate competition. The Confucian emphasis on filial piety proved more emotionally compelling to many educated elites and more compatible with the hereditary aristocracy that still commanded social prestige.

The *fen shu* (焚書, "burning of books") ordered by Qin Shi Huang in 213 BCE targeted texts deemed subversive to the unified Qin state, and Mohist works were among those affected, though surviving copies were preserved in private collections and, later, in Buddhist monasteries. When the Han Dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) formally adopted Confucianism as state ideology under Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE) and the philosopher Dong Zhongshu (c. 179–104 BCE), the institutional and intellectual resources of the state were channeled into Confucian scholarship. Mohism as a living school — with its organizational structure, its communities of practitioners, and its network of defenders — did not survive the early Han. The *Mozi* text was preserved but not actively studied.

## Legacy and Rediscovery

The *Mozi* survived the dynasties — listed in imperial bibliographies, occasionally cited by scholars — but was not treated as a living philosophical resource until the twentieth century. The systematic rediscovery of Mohism began with Qing Dynasty philologists who worked on the damaged and difficult *mojing* chapters; the scholar Sun Yirang (孫詒讓, 1848–1908) produced the definitive classical Chinese edition of the *Mozi* in 1894.

In the twentieth century, renewed engagement with Western philosophy — particularly utilitarian ethics and formal logic — created new frameworks for appreciating Mohist thought. Chinese intellectuals of the May Fourth Movement (1919) and their successors found in Mohism a distinctly Chinese tradition of universalism, empirical inquiry, and social concern that could be engaged with modern Western ideas. The British scholar A.C. Graham's landmark study *Later Mohist Logic, Ethics and Science* (1978) made the Mohist Canons accessible to Western readers and established the school's proto-scientific achievements as historically significant.

Contemporary scholarly interest has led to new translations, philosophical engagements, and reassessments of Mohism's place in the history of philosophy. The school is now recognized as one of the great unrealized alternatives in Chinese thought — a school that, had the Han adopted Mohism rather than Confucianism as state ideology, might have produced a civilization with a stronger emphasis on universalism, meritocracy, empirical inquiry, and the philosophical grounding of social welfare.

The Mohist vision — a meritocratic society governed by the principle of mutual benefit, defended against aggression by technically sophisticated and morally committed communities — continues to resonate with those seeking resources within the Chinese philosophical tradition for addressing questions of justice, ethics, and global care.
