# Printing and Woodblock Printing

Printing technology emerged in China as one of humanity's most transformative inventions, fundamentally reshaping communication, knowledge dissemination, and cultural production. Chinese innovation in woodblock printing and later movable-type printing predated European printing by centuries, enabling mass reproduction of texts and establishing patterns of literacy and textual authority that defined Chinese civilization through the modern era.

## Preconditions: Paper and Ink

Woodblock printing presupposed the prior Chinese invention of paper. Cai Lun, a court official of the Han Emperor He (r. 88–105 CE), is traditionally credited with refining papermaking around 105 CE, though archaeological evidence from the Western Han period indicates cruder paper was made as early as the 2nd century BCE. By the Tang Dynasty, paper had completely displaced bamboo strips, silk, and wooden tablets as the primary writing medium in China, and the paper industry was producing sheets of consistent quality suitable for printed reproduction.

The inks used in woodblock printing were water-based, applied with brushes or daubers to the raised carved surfaces. Black carbon-based inks, developed from lampblack or soot, provided sharp contrast on paper. Later color printing required separate inks for each color, applied in successive impressions using multiple precisely registered blocks.

## Origins and Early Development (7th–10th Century)

Woodblock printing evolved gradually from earlier Chinese technologies. The technique of stamping ink-sealed seals onto materials had existed since antiquity, but organized woodblock printing as a reproducible technology emerged during the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE). The earliest surviving printed texts with definitive dates are Buddhist documents. A printed dharani scroll dated to approximately 704–751 CE discovered in Korea (the *Mugu jeonggwang daedarani gyeong*) is among the world's earliest known surviving printed texts, reflecting the early transmission of printing technology from Tang China.

The most famous early printed work is the *Diamond Sutra* (金剛般若波羅蜜經), a complete scroll measuring approximately 5.18 meters in length, bearing a colophon stating it was "reverently made for universal free distribution by Wang Jie on behalf of his two parents on the 13th of the 4th moon of the 9th year of Xiantong [868 CE]." Discovered in the Dunhuang cave library (sealed around 1000 CE and reopened in 1900), it now resides in the British Library. These early prints served primarily religious purposes—producing multiple copies of Buddhist sutras and protective spells (*dharani*) for distribution to temples and believers. The logic was explicitly meritorious: copying a sutra generated spiritual merit, and mechanical printing allowed that merit to be generated at vastly greater scale.

The technology involved carving characters or images in reverse on a smoothed block of hardwood (typically jujube, pear, or catalpa), inking the raised surfaces, and pressing paper or silk onto the block. Early blocks showed uneven inking and paper inconsistencies, but craftsmen rapidly developed standardized techniques. Buddhist monasteries, with their networks, scriptoria traditions, and need for large textual editions, became the primary institutional sponsors of printing's early development.

## Expansion and the Song Golden Age (960–1279)

Woodblock printing expanded dramatically during the Song Dynasty, when the technology moved decisively beyond religious texts into secular publishing. The Song court sponsored monumental official publications, most notably a complete printed edition of the Confucian canon—the *Jiu Jing* (Nine Classics), carved and printed beginning in 953 CE under the Later Zhou and completed under the Song. This project, producing standardized texts of the canonical classics, had profound intellectual consequences: for the first time, scholars across the empire could work from identical texts, reducing the variant readings that had plagued manuscript transmission for centuries.

The Song period saw the emergence of a vibrant commercial printing industry centered in several cities. Jianyang (建陽) in Fujian Province became China's most prolific commercial publishing center from the 11th century onward, producing inexpensive editions of classics, histories, almanacs, and examination aids for the expanding market of examination candidates. Hangzhou, the Southern Song capital, supported a luxury publishing trade for the court and educated elite. Chengdu in Sichuan was particularly important for printing paper currency, which the Song government issued beginning around 1024 CE—the world's first government-issued paper money, itself a product of printing technology.

Major scholarly compilations produced through Song printing include the *Cefu Yuangui* (冊府元龜, 1013 CE), an encyclopedic collection of over 9 million characters drawn from historical sources, and numerous printed editions of medical texts. The Song imperial medical bureau sponsored standardized printed editions of classical medical works including the *Bencao Yanyi* (Materia Medica) and the *Shang Han Lun* (Treatise on Cold Damage Disorders), distributing standardized pharmaceutical and diagnostic texts across the empire. This had real consequences for medical practice: print standardization reduced variant readings of crucial drug formulas.

During this period, Chinese printers also developed and refined multi-color printing techniques. The *Zhushi Huitu* (Illustrated Reference Text with Commentary) and similar illustrated works used multiple carved blocks—one per color—printed in precise registration to create sophisticated multi-chromatic images. This innovation, requiring exact alignment between successive printings, represented a significant technical achievement.

## Movable Type and Further Innovation (11th–14th Century)

While woodblock printing dominated, Chinese innovators also experimented with movable type—individual characters that could be rearranged for different texts, allowing faster composition for long texts without carving entirely new blocks. The earliest evidence of movable-type printing in China dates to circa 1040 CE, when Bi Sheng (畢昇), described in Shen Kuo's *Dream Pool Essays* (1088 CE) as a commoner craftsman, developed a system using baked clay characters set in an iron frame sealed with pine resin. This innovation predated Johannes Gutenberg's metal movable type by approximately four centuries.

Despite its theoretical advantages, clay movable type never fully superseded woodblock printing in China. The primary challenge was the sheer number of Chinese characters required: an educated reader required familiarity with 5,000–10,000 characters, and a comprehensive printing font needed tens of thousands of individual pieces to handle all characters and their variants. Managing such a font, retrieving specific characters, and ensuring even inking proved technically demanding. Woodblock printing, despite requiring more upfront carving labor, remained more economical for individual texts and allowed the integration of illustrations and calligraphic styles far more easily than type.

Wang Zhen (王禎), a Yuan Dynasty agricultural official, made the next major advance in movable type around 1298 CE. Wang developed a system of wooden (rather than clay) movable type and, crucially, invented a revolving typecase—a large rotating circular table allowing the typesetter to access characters without moving from a single position. Wang applied his system to print his own *Nong Shu* (農書, Treatise on Agriculture) in 1313 CE. In the preface he described setting 60,000 wooden type pieces to print the work in less than a month. Wang's system remained exceptional rather than typical; Chinese printers generally returned to woodblocks for most work.

Korean printers in the Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392 CE) pushed movable type further. Goryeo metal type—cast in bronze rather than carved in wood or clay—predates Gutenberg by at least two centuries: surviving metal type pieces and documentary evidence suggest Korean metal printing by the early 13th century, with the *Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun* (1234 CE) traditionally cited as the world's first text printed with metal movable type, though the earliest surviving Korean metal-type books date to 1377 CE.

## Major Buddhist Canon Projects

Among the most ambitious printing projects in Chinese history were the successive editions of the Buddhist Tripitaka (大藏經, *Dazangjing*)—the complete corpus of Buddhist scriptures translated into Chinese. The first printed Tripitaka, the *Kaibao Canon* (開寶藏), was carved and printed at Chengdu between 971 and 983 CE under Song Taizu, comprising approximately 1,076 texts in 5,048 volumes across 130,000 carved woodblocks. Subsequent editions multiplied: the *Qisha Canon* (磧砂藏, 1231–1322 CE) involved over 6,000 volumes; the famous Korean Goryeo *Tripitaka Koreana* (고려대장경), carved between 1236 and 1251 CE as a votive act to invoke divine protection against the Mongol invasions, used approximately 81,340 woodblocks and survives intact at Haeinsa temple. These projects mobilized the labor of thousands of craftsmen over decades and remain among the most extraordinary feats of pre-modern information reproduction.

## The Ming Florescence (14th–17th Century)

The Ming Dynasty became the zenith of Chinese woodblock printing. Ming printers achieved unprecedented technical sophistication, producing illustrated books, art albums, and luxury editions representing the height of Chinese craftsmanship. The Ming intellectual elite—scholar-officials, merchants, and literati—developed a robust market for printed books, creating both demand and capital for larger, more ambitious publications.

The most celebrated printed work of the early Ming was the *Yongle Encyclopedia* (永樂大典, 1408), commissioned by the Yongle Emperor. Compiled by over 2,000 scholars and running to approximately 22,877 volumes (11,095 volumes in the final manuscript version), it represented an attempt to compile all Chinese knowledge. Ironically, its sheer scale precluded printing—only three hand-copied editions were made, and the majority of the work was lost during the Qing period and foreign invasions. Only about 800 volumes survive today.

For commercial and wider distribution, the Ming printing industry produced vernacular fiction on an unprecedented scale. The *Journey to the West* (西遊記, attributed to Wu Cheng'en, published c. 1592), the *Water Margin* (水滸傳), and the *Plum in the Golden Vase* (金瓶梅, circulated in manuscript from the 1590s and printed in 1618) gained mass circulation through Ming commercial publishers, particularly in Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing, and Jianyang. The Beijing area, Suzhou's Lüliang Bridge district, and Nanjing's Sanshan Street became famous publishing quarters with concentrations of print shops and booksellers.

Ming printers innovated significantly in color printing. The *Chengshi Moyuan* (程氏墨苑, 1606), a study of ink cakes accompanied by elaborate color illustrations, demonstrated the technical capacity for up to five or more colors printed in precise registration. The *Shizhu Zhai Jian Pu* (Ten Bamboo Studio Letter Paper Manual, 1644) and *Shi Zhu Zhai Shu Hua Pu* (Ten Bamboo Studio Manual of Painting and Calligraphy, first published 1619–1633 CE) by Hu Zhengyan became the peak expressions of Ming polychrome printing, combining color printing and embossed relief printing (*gonghua*) to simulate the texture and tonal subtlety of brush painting.

The most influential Ming printing publication for East Asian art was the *Jieziyuan Huapu* (芥子園畫譜, Mustard Seed Garden Manual of Painting, 1679–1701 CE), a multi-volume woodblock-printed color manual of painting techniques and exemplary compositions. Produced in three series by publisher Shen Xinyou and continued by his descendants, it became the standard self-teaching guide for literati painters in China, Japan, and Korea for the next two centuries, its woodblock color plates serving both as aesthetic exemplars and technical instruction.

## Woodblock Art and the Print Tradition

Parallel to textual printing, Chinese woodblock artists developed a sophisticated tradition of artistic prints that were aesthetic works in their own right, not merely reproductive copies of paintings. The literati (*wenren*) tradition of the Ming and Qing prized the combination of calligraphy, painting, and printing into integrated objects. Artists of the Suzhou school created woodblock illustrations for luxury editions that were collected and admired independently of the texts they accompanied.

By the late Ming and Qing, woodblock prints of popular religion (door gods, kitchen gods, and New Year icons) served a mass market stretching from palace to peasant household. The Taohuawu quarter of Suzhou and the Yangliuqing area near Tianjin became the most celebrated production centers for these nianhua (年畫, New Year prints), each developing distinctive regional styles. Yangliuqing prints, combining woodblock and hand-coloring, could sell in editions of tens of thousands, making them among the world's first truly mass-market color images.

## Impact on East Asia and the World

Chinese printing technology spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, where local printers adapted it to their own writing systems and cultural needs. In Japan, Buddhist monks brought printing technology during the Nara period (710–794 CE), and the Japanese court sponsored early Buddhist printing projects. Japanese woodblock printing eventually developed into the celebrated *ukiyo-e* (浮世絵, "pictures of the floating world") tradition of the Edo period (1603–1868), producing the multicolor works of Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige, and others that would later electrify European artists including Van Gogh and Monet (who assembled a collection of over 400 Japanese prints).

Korean printers, working within the Confucian culture that valued textual transmission, produced both translations of Chinese classics and indigenous Korean literary and religious texts. The Goryeo Dynasty's *Tripitaka Koreana* stands as the most ambitious Korean printing achievement. Vietnamese printing, centered on Chinese-character texts and later on the *Chữ Nôm* demotic script, similarly adapted Chinese techniques to local needs.

Knowledge of Chinese printing technology eventually reached the Islamic world and Europe. European development of printing proceeded independently—Gutenberg's metal movable type (c. 1450 CE) represented a different technological solution optimized for the Roman alphabet's 26 letters—but when Europeans encountered Chinese printing through Jesuit missionaries and colonial contact in the 16th–17th centuries, they recognized its sophistication. The Jesuit Matteo Ricci, who arrived in China in 1583, wrote admiringly of the scope and low cost of Chinese book production compared to Europe.

## Modern Transformation and Decline

During the late Qing and Republican periods, Western printing technology—featuring metal type, mechanical flatbed and rotary presses, photographic reproduction (half-tone processes), and eventually offset lithography—gradually displaced traditional Chinese woodblock printing. The Self-Strengthening Movement of the 1860s–1890s had already introduced Western printing presses for newspapers and foreign-language publications in Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Hong Kong. The American Presbyterian Mission Press in Shanghai (founded 1844) and the Commercial Press (商務印書館, founded Shanghai 1897) combined Western printing technology with Chinese content, producing modern textbooks, dictionaries, and periodicals in formats incompatible with woodblock production.

By the 1920s and 1930s, the major Chinese publishing houses—Commercial Press, Zhonghua Book Company, World Book Company—operated entirely on Western-style mechanical presses. The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) and the subsequent civil war disrupted publishing, but the establishment of the People's Republic in 1949 and the nationalization of publishing accelerated the shift to industrial printing, which was far more efficient for the high-volume propaganda and educational materials the new state required.

Woodblock printing survived as a craft tradition and folk art, particularly in regions like Taohuawu (Suzhou) for New Year prints, Yangliuqing (Tianjin), and Weifang (Shandong) for kite imagery and decorative arts. The late 20th-century revival of traditional culture, accelerated by cultural tourism policies and UNESCO heritage designations, brought new attention to woodblock craft. Several institutions, including the Rongbaozhai studio in Beijing (founded 1672, revived in the Republican period), maintained and revived high-quality woodblock reproduction, producing museum-quality facsimiles of famous paintings using hundreds of individually carved and registered color blocks.

## Cultural Significance

The invention and refinement of printing technology transformed Chinese civilization in multiple ways. It reduced the cost of textual reproduction by orders of magnitude, enabling mass literacy by making books affordable to a far wider population than manuscript culture could support. The Song-era expansion of the civil examination system was inseparable from printing: without printed editions of the classics and commentaries, the examination's requirements could only have been met by the wealthy elite with access to private copyists.

Printing standardized texts, allowing the dissemination of orthodox editions of classics, official legal codes, and pharmaceutical formularies. It facilitated scholarship by enabling scholars to compare multiple editions and build encyclopedic compendia of existing knowledge. It enabled the mass circulation of literature—vernacular fiction, drama, and poetry—far beyond the scholar-official class to merchants, artisans, and educated women.

Printing also had profound political implications. Control over what was printed allowed governments to standardize law, project ideological uniformity, and distribute propaganda. The imperial state's sponsorship of massive official compilations (*leishu*, encyclopedias) asserted centralized textual authority. Conversely, commercial printing allowed merchants, private scholars, and religious communities to produce texts beyond state control, creating spaces for heterodox ideas, commercial fiction, and popular religion that state orthodoxy disapproved of but could not entirely suppress.

The cultural synthesis achieved by Ming and Qing printers—combining calligraphy, painting, poetry, and printing into integrated aesthetic objects, from luxury editions of collected works to polychrome painting manuals—represented a distinctively Chinese approach to knowledge and beauty in which material form and intellectual content were inseparable values.
