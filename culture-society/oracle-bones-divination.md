# Oracle Bones and Divination

## Summary

Oracle bones (*jiaguwen*, literally "shell and bone script") represent the earliest known form of Chinese writing, dating from the Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE), discovered systematically only in the late nineteenth century near Anyang, in the Yellow River valley. These objects—animal bones (especially ox scapulae) and tortoise shells—were inscribed with divination questions, heated until they cracked, and the resulting fracture patterns were interpreted by Shang diviners to answer queries from the king about weather, warfare, harvests, and the will of ancestors and spirits. More than 150,000 oracle bone fragments have been recovered from the Shang capital site at Yinxu (near modern Anyang, Henan Province), representing a corpus of tens of thousands of individual divination events spanning roughly 270 years. The practice embodies the cosmological framework of early China: a universe permeated by spirit forces, responsive to human ritual, and knowable through specialized interpretive practices. The oracle-bone script, comprising approximately 4,500 distinct graphs (of which roughly 1,500 have been reliably deciphered), evolved from pictographic and ideographic symbols into increasingly complex forms, becoming the direct ancestor of all subsequent Chinese writing systems. The discovery of oracle bones, first recognized as ancient writing in 1899, transformed understanding of ancient China, confirming the historical reality of the Shang Dynasty and providing direct textual evidence of pre-imperial Chinese civilization.

## Divination Practice and Cosmology

Oracle-bone divination (*buci* divination) was a state ritual performed in the Shang court to divine the will of ancestors, spirits, and cosmic forces. The Shang king, serving as intermediary between the human and spirit worlds, commissioned specialist diviners (*bu zhu*) to pose questions to the ancestors and spirits on behalf of the king. The divination process involved a series of precisely formalized steps: the diviner first prepared the bone by smoothing its surface and carving rows of oval hollows (*zuan*) into the back. The question was then inscribed, typically in a formulaic structure consisting of a preface (*xunci*), the charge (*mingci*), and sometimes a post-inscription noting the outcome (*zhan*). The diviner applied a heated bronze point to the hollows; the application of heat caused characteristic T-shaped cracks (*zhu*) to appear on the front surface, and the shape, direction, and configuration of these cracks were read as the answer—favorable or unfavorable, auspicious or inauspicious.

### Categories of Divination Inquiry

The questions recorded on oracle bones reveal the full range of royal concern in the Shang court. Major categories include:

- **Military affairs**: "Will the campaign against Qiang succeed?" or "Which day should we attack?" The military campaigns were often directed against the Qiang (a western pastoral people), the Fang (eastern groups), and other neighbors. Entire sequences of oracle bones track the preparations, execution, and outcomes of specific military expeditions.
- **Agricultural and climatic concerns**: "Will it rain this season?" "Will the millet harvest be good?" Rainfall predictions were of enormous practical importance in a bronze-age agricultural state; the king's ability to predict or influence the weather was central to his legitimacy.
- **Ritual sacrifice**: "Should we offer ten sheep?" or "Which ancestor should receive the sacrifice?" The Shang practiced elaborate ancestral sacrifice, including human sacrifice; oracle bones record the numbers and types of victims deemed appropriate for specific ancestral spirits.
- **Royal health and succession**: "Will the king recover from illness?" "Will the queen bear a son?" Personal and dynastic concerns occupied considerable oracle-bone inquiry, revealing anxieties about royal succession and health that defined Shang politics.
- **Hunting expeditions**: Royal hunts were ritually significant events, and the oracle bones asked which day to hunt and what game would be found. Successful hunts provided sacrificial animals and demonstrated the king's ritual efficacy.
- **Astronomical and calendrical**: "Is the eclipse an omen?" "Should we observe a sacrifice on the next *jiazi* day?" (The Shang used a sixty-day cycle for recording dates.)

This variety reveals that oracle-bone divination was not confined to extraordinary crises but was a systematic administrative technology woven into the routine governance of the Shang court. Major decisions, even routine ones, were framed as divine consultations.

### Cosmological Framework

The practice reflects a worldview in which the distinction between the natural and supernatural was not sharply drawn. The universe was believed to be populated by spiritual forces—the ancestors of the royal house, local earth deities (*she*), celestial spirits, river gods, and demonic forces whose influence was constant and whose intentions could be discovered through appropriate ritual. The Shang recognized a supreme deity, *Shangdi* (the High Lord), and a complex hierarchy of ancestral spirits corresponding to the royal genealogy; each deceased Shang king became an ancestor spirit who could intercede with *Shangdi* on the living king's behalf or, if neglected, cause disaster. This theocratic cosmology gave the king both religious authority and political legitimacy: by mediating between the human and ancestral realms through divination, the king demonstrated his unique cosmic role.

The materials themselves held cosmological significance. Tortoise shells (*jiaze*)—especially the plastrons (ventral shells) of river tortoises—and ox scapulae were favored because they were believed to serve as conduits to the spirit world. Turtles in particular were associated with longevity, cosmic stability, and aquatic underworld forces. The bones came from ritually sacrificed animals, creating a link between the human and animal realms. The application of heat and the resulting cracks were understood as the medium through which spirits communicated with the living.

## Script and Writing System

The oracle-bone inscriptions constitute the earliest reliably attested Chinese writing system. Scholars have identified approximately 4,500 distinct graphs in the entire oracle-bone corpus, of which roughly 1,500 have been reliably deciphered and understood. Many of the remaining graphs remain contested or unidentified, partly because they represent specialist ritual vocabulary no longer used in later texts, and partly because the pictographic origins of some characters have been obscured by stylistic variation across different scribal traditions.

### Pictographic and Logographic Structure

Early oracle-bone characters are highly pictorial: the character for "sun" (*ri*) appears as a circle with a central dot; the character for "tree" (*mu*) shows a trunk with roots and branches; the character for "mountain" (*shan*) depicts three peaks; the character for "fish" (*yu*) shows a fish in profile with tail fin and scales. These pictographs were supplemented by ideographic combinations—for example, the character for "forest" (*lin*) showed two tree characters side by side, and "bright" (*ming*) combined sun and moon.

The oracle-bone script also shows phonetic compounds—characters in which one element suggests the pronunciation and another the semantic category. This principle of phonosemantic composition, already visible in oracle-bone writing, became the dominant word-formation strategy in later Chinese writing, where over 80 percent of characters follow phonosemantic structure.

Writing direction in oracle bones was highly flexible but tended toward vertical columns read from top to bottom, and columns ordered from right to left—conventions that persisted in Chinese writing until the twentieth century. On individual bones, text might run in multiple directions depending on the orientation of the cracks and the arrangement of hollows.

### Scribal Traditions and Diviner Groups

Modern oracle-bone scholarship has identified multiple distinct scribal "groups" or hands among Shang inscriptions, each associated with a particular reign or divinatory tradition. Under the reign of Wu Ding (r. c. 1250–1192 BCE)—the most prolific period of oracle-bone activity—scholars identify the "Bin Group" (*Bin zu*), the "Chu Group" (*Chu zu*), and others, each showing distinct calligraphic conventions, formulaic preferences, and subject matter. The Bin Group inscriptions are the most numerous and include the famous "war divinations" tracking campaigns against the Fang peoples. Different groups may have served distinct functions within the Shang divination apparatus.

### Evolution into Later Scripts

The oracle-bone script underwent gradual transformation through subsequent dynasties. The Shang script evolved into the bronze-script characters (*jinwen*) cast into Zhou Dynasty ritual bronze vessels (c. 1046–256 BCE), which became increasingly standardized. By the Qin Dynasty (221–206 BCE), the script had been officially unified into Small Seal Script (*xiaozhuan*) as part of the Qin First Emperor's administrative standardization. Further simplification produced the clerical script (*lishu*) of the Han Dynasty, ancestor of modern Chinese characters. Every step of this two-millennium evolution traces directly to oracle-bone writing, making Shang inscriptions not merely historical documents but the source of a living writing tradition.

## Archaeological Discovery and Scholarship

Oracle bones lay buried and forgotten for millennia until the late nineteenth century. The site of Yinxu, the last Shang capital near modern Anyang in Henan Province, had been known in local folklore as a place where "dragon bones" emerged from the earth—fragments of ancient animal bones sold by pharmacists as medicinal material. For years before scholarly notice, villagers and bone traders sold these inscribed bones to pharmacies, where they were ground into powder. An unknown quantity of inscribed oracle bones was destroyed in this way before scholars recognized their significance.

### Wang Yirong and the First Recognition (1899)

In 1899, the Qing court official Wang Yirong (1845–1900), a distinguished scholar of ancient Chinese inscriptions, noticed unusual markings on bones purchased for medicinal use and recognized them as ancient script. Wang Yirong's recognition—that these were genuine antique inscriptions, not fossilized reptile bones—is the conventional starting point of oracle-bone scholarship. Wang Yirong began collecting the bones; after his death during the Boxer Rebellion in 1900, his collection passed to the scholar Liu E (1857–1909), who published the first systematic collection, *Tieyun Cang Gui* (1903), reproducing 1,058 oracle-bone inscriptions and making them available to the scholarly world.

Sun Yirang (1848–1908) produced the first comprehensive scholarly analysis, *Qiwen Juli* (1904), identifying 485 graphs and laying the foundation for systematic oracle-bone decipherment. Wang Guowei (1877–1927), one of China's greatest philologists, made a decisive contribution by correlating oracle-bone king names with the Shang royal genealogy recorded in the *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian), confirming that the bones belonged to the historical Shang royal court and not to some earlier or unidentified dynasty.

### Yinxu Excavations (1928–1937)

Systematic archaeological excavation began in 1928 under Li Ji (1896–1979), a Harvard-trained archaeologist working for the Academia Sinica. Between 1928 and 1937, Li Ji and his colleagues conducted fifteen excavation seasons at Yinxu, uncovering palace and temple foundations, royal tombs, bronze foundries, workshops, and—most dramatically—enormous pits packed with oracle bones. The Yinxu site covers approximately 24 square kilometers along the Huanhe River.

The most spectacular single find was Pit H127, excavated in 1936, which contained 17,096 oracle-bone pieces in a single compressed cache—the largest single deposit ever found. The pit appears to represent a systematic archival deposit of divination records, demonstrating that the Shang court maintained organized storage of these documents. In total, more than 150,000 oracle-bone pieces have been recovered from Yinxu, with additional fragments scattered in private collections and foreign museums acquired through the antique trade before systematic archaeology began.

### Fu Hao's Tomb (1976)

In 1976, archaeologists excavating at Yinxu discovered the intact tomb of Fu Hao (died c. 1200 BCE), a consort of King Wu Ding and, unusually for the Shang period, a military commander who led armies against the Fang peoples. Fu Hao's tomb—the only unrobbed royal Shang tomb ever excavated—contained 1,928 bronze objects, 755 jade pieces, 6,880 cowrie shells (used as currency), and numerous oracle-bone inscriptions referring to her. The tomb contents confirmed oracle-bone references to Fu Hao as a real historical figure, demonstrated the exceptional role of elite women in Shang military affairs, and provided one of the richest assemblages of Shang material culture ever recovered.

### UNESCO Recognition

The oracle-bone collection held in China's National Museum was inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 2017, recognizing oracle bones as among humanity's most significant documentary heritage and one of the foundational records of East Asian civilization.

## Relationship to Later Divination Traditions

While oracle-bone divination was specific to the Shang Dynasty, the cosmological assumptions underlying it persisted through subsequent Chinese history and influenced later divination systems.

The Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BCE) retained divination as a royal prerogative but supplemented pyromantic crack-reading with milfoil-stalk divination using the *I Ching* (Book of Changes, *Yijing*), which generated hexagrams—patterns of six solid or broken lines—through casting fifty yarrow stalks. The *I Ching* tradition, compiled and elaborated over centuries, retained the core Shang assumption that patterned natural events encode meaningful cosmic information accessible to skilled interpreters, but replaced the physical medium of cracked bones with abstract symbolic notation. The *I Ching* achieved its canonical form during the Han Dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) and became one of the Five Classics of the Confucian canon, studied by scholars for two millennia.

Daoist, Buddhist, and folk Chinese traditions maintained a vast spectrum of divination practices: geomancy (*feng shui*), palmistry (*xiangfa*), physiognomy (*xiang mian*), and astrological interpretation. The *Tong Shu* (Almanac), consulted annually by millions of Chinese families to identify auspicious and inauspicious days for major activities, represents a direct descendent of oracle-bone calendar divination. The assumption underlying all these practices—that the cosmos is ordered by recurring patterns and that trained interpreters can read those patterns to guide human action—derives from the same epistemological framework visible in Shang oracle-bone divination.

## Significance for Chinese Civilization

Oracle bones represent a watershed in human history: the moment when systematic writing emerged in East Asia and connected abstract symbols to human meaning. The script evolved into the classical Chinese writing system that unified the vast Chinese cultural sphere across linguistic variations—the same characters readable by speakers of Cantonese, Shanghainese, Hokkien, Mandarin, and other mutually unintelligible languages, creating what scholars call a "logographic" civilization bound together by written rather than spoken language.

The oracle-bone corpus provides the first direct textual evidence of early Chinese political organization, ritual practice, warfare, and cosmology. It confirms the existence of a Shang state with a sophisticated theocratic bureaucracy, organized military, and hereditary monarchy that communicated with ancestors and spirits through formalized ritual. The bones also preserve the names of thirty-one Shang kings, corroborating the dynastic genealogy preserved in *Shiji* and demonstrating that Sima Qian's second-century BCE historical record incorporated genuine archival knowledge.

Beyond their historical significance, oracle bones embody a distinctly Chinese approach to knowledge: the conviction that reality is ordered, patterned, and intelligible to those with proper training and ritual access. This epistemological stance—that meaning is immanent in patterns, and that the skilled interpreter can read reality through symbols—became foundational to Chinese philosophy, aesthetics, and practice. It informed the *I Ching*, calligraphy's expressive symbolism, landscape painting's cosmic geometry, and the broader Chinese conviction that the natural world is a text available for reading by the wisely educated. In this sense, oracle bones are not merely historical artifacts but the original documents of a cognitive and aesthetic tradition still active in Chinese culture.
