# Special Economic Zones

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: Deng Xiaoping, Jiang Zemin, Xi Jinping  
**Period**: 1979–present

## Summary

China's Special Economic Zones (SEZs) were designated areas established beginning in 1979 as experimental laboratories for market-oriented economic practices during the early stages of Deng Xiaoping's Reform and Opening-up program. The first four SEZs—Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen—were created in Guangdong and Fujian provinces, strategically positioned to attract overseas Chinese investment and enable the importation of foreign technology and management practices under a more flexible regulatory regime than the rest of mainland China. Within these zones, enterprises were permitted to engage in foreign trade, joint ventures with foreign companies, and market-based pricing—practices forbidden elsewhere in the centrally planned economy. The experiment proved extraordinarily successful: Shenzhen, a fishing village of approximately 30,000 people in 1979, transformed into a modern city of over 10 million within three decades, becoming a symbol of China's economic dynamism and proving that capitalist-style market mechanisms could coexist with Communist Party political control.

## Establishment and Early Design (1979–1984)

The legal framework for SEZs was authorized by the National People's Congress on July 1, 1979. The Guangdong and Fujian provincial governments were granted special authority to offer preferential terms to foreign investors, including reduced taxes, streamlined customs procedures, and flexible labor contracts. On August 26, 1980, Guangdong Province formally promulgated the regulations establishing the four original zones.

The selection of locations was deliberate: Shenzhen and Zhuhai in Guangdong province sat adjacent to Hong Kong and Macau respectively, enabling easy flow of capital, people, and goods from two of Asia's most vibrant capitalist economies. Xiamen in Fujian faced Taiwan, providing a channel for investment from the substantial Taiwanese and overseas Chinese business community. Shantou, also in Guangdong, had historic ties to the large Teochew-speaking diaspora in Southeast Asia.

Each zone received designated territory—Shenzhen initially encompassed 327.5 square kilometers—fenced off from the surrounding country, with the border strictly controlled. Foreign exchange earnings could be retained and reinvested within the zones rather than surrendered to the central bank. Corporate income tax rates were set at 15 percent, compared with the 55 percent standard rate applied elsewhere.

Within two years of opening, Hong Kong investors had financed more than 1,000 enterprises in the Shenzhen SEZ alone. By 1984, total utilized foreign investment in Shenzhen had surpassed US$200 million, and the zone's export revenues had grown from near-zero to over US$100 million annually.

## Expansion to 14 Coastal Cities (1984)

Emboldened by the SEZs' success, the State Council in May 1984 approved the opening of 14 additional coastal cities to foreign investment on preferential terms. These included major ports and industrial centers: Dalian, Qinhuangdao, Tianjin, Yantai, Qingdao, Lianyungang, Nantong, Shanghai, Ningbo, Wenzhou, Fuzhou, Guangzhou, Zhanjiang, and Beihai. While not designated as full SEZs, these cities received authority to establish Economic and Technical Development Zones (ETDZs) offering tax incentives and streamlined approvals.

The same year, Hainan Island began receiving special investment policies, which culminated in April 1988 when Hainan was elevated to provincial status and designated the largest SEZ in China, covering its entire 33,900 square kilometers. Hainan's SEZ status was intended to transform it into an international tourism destination and offshore financial center, though its early years were marked by speculative excess including the so-called "Hainan auto scandal" of 1984–1985, in which local officials used import exemptions to purchase and resell 89,000 cars, 2.86 million television sets, and other goods on the mainland at enormous profit, exposing risks in the decentralized SEZ model.

## The Pudong New Area and Shanghai's Rise (1990–1993)

In April 1990, the State Council announced the development of the Pudong New Area on the eastern bank of the Huangpu River, across from Shanghai's historic Bund district. This decision was partly strategic: Shanghai had been excluded from the first wave of SEZ designations in 1980, a sore point for the city's leadership. Pudong received policies comparable to the original SEZs—including the first bonded free-trade port zone in China—while the central government invested heavily in infrastructure, including the Yangpu and Nanpu suspension bridges (opened 1991 and 1993) connecting Pudong to Puxi.

Pudong's Lujiazui financial district was designated in 1990 as the location for foreign financial institutions, initially restricted from operating elsewhere in China. By 1993, Pudong's GDP had reached 25 billion yuan (roughly US$4.3 billion), more than doubling from 1990 levels. The Oriental Pearl Tower, completed in 1994 at a height of 468 meters, became an internationally recognized symbol of Shanghai's transformation.

## Deng's Southern Tour and Ideological Consolidation (1992)

Following the political retrenchment after June 4, 1989, SEZ policies faced criticism from ideological conservatives who argued that market-oriented reforms were leading to "peaceful evolution" toward capitalism. In January–February 1992, Deng Xiaoping, then aged 87 and formally retired, undertook a celebrated tour of Guangdong province—visiting Shenzhen, Zhuhai, and other southern cities—in which he made forceful speeches defending the market economy and calling for bolder reform.

Deng declared that "productivity is the only criterion of truth" and warned that dogmatic socialism that failed to deliver prosperity was as dangerous as capitalism. His talks, initially suppressed by conservative media and first circulated through internal channels, were published in March 1992 and restarted the reform momentum that had stalled since 1989. The tour cemented Shenzhen's identity as the symbolic capital of Chinese reform: its skyline, its per capita GDP (which had surpassed Hong Kong's growth rate), and its population growth—from 30,000 in 1979 to roughly 2.3 million by 1992—became the evidence Deng marshaled to refute critics.

## Economic Impact and Scale

By 2000, China's five original SEZs together attracted over US$30 billion in cumulative utilized foreign direct investment. Shenzhen alone accounted for an annual GDP of approximately US$10 billion by 1995, and its economy grew at an average annual rate exceeding 25 percent throughout the 1980s. The manufacturing cluster centered on Shenzhen produced consumer electronics, textiles, toys, and later semiconductors and telecommunications equipment for global export.

The SEZs served as the primary channel through which multinational corporations from Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, South Korea, and the United States established Chinese manufacturing operations. Companies such as Foxconn (then Hon Hai Precision Industry), which began operations in Shenzhen in 1988, grew to employ hundreds of thousands of workers in the zone, assembling products for Apple, Sony, and other global brands. By the early 2000s, Shenzhen's port had become one of the world's largest container terminals, handling over 16 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) annually.

The SEZ model also generated massive internal migration. Between 1980 and 2000, an estimated 25–30 million rural workers migrated to Guangdong province's SEZs and their surrounding cities, representing one of the largest voluntary labor migrations in history. These workers—the so-called *dagongmei* (working sisters) and *dagongzai* (working brothers)—fueled both the zones' industrial output and a broader transformation of China's urbanization pattern.

## Later Development: Free Trade Zones and Beyond

In September 2013, the State Council established the China (Shanghai) Pilot Free Trade Zone (FTZ), covering 29 square kilometers in Pudong. Unlike earlier SEZs focused on manufacturing, the Shanghai FTZ targeted financial services liberalization, including experiments with convertibility of the renminbi capital account, cross-border lending, and relaxation of foreign ownership restrictions in banking and insurance.

By 2015, the FTZ model had been extended to three additional cities: Guangdong, Tianjin, and Fujian. By 2020, China had approved 21 pilot FTZs spanning coastal and interior provinces. The Hainan Free Trade Port, announced in June 2020, represented the most ambitious version: Hainan Island, population 9.2 million, was designated as a full free trade port with near-zero tariffs for all goods by 2025 and full capital account convertibility planned for 2035.

## Significance

The Special Economic Zones represent one of the most consequential institutional innovations in modern economic history. They served as both a policy tool and a proof-of-concept: by geographically limiting market reforms to small, controlled areas, China's leaders could test whether capitalist economic practices would destabilize the socialist system or, conversely, generate rapid growth and technological advancement. The overwhelming success of the SEZs—particularly Shenzhen's transformation—provided the empirical foundation for Deng's confidence in expanding market reforms nationally. The zones attracted tens of billions of dollars in foreign direct investment, created millions of manufacturing jobs, and became the manufacturing heartland that would make China "the world's factory" by the 2000s.

More broadly, the SEZ model demonstrated how state control and market mechanisms could coexist, a compromise that enabled China's economic rise without requiring the wholesale dismantling of Communist Party authority. The institutional success of the zones also influenced Deng's famous dictum "seek truth from facts" and his pragmatic approach to ideology: if a policy worked economically and did not threaten political control, it was acceptable regardless of theoretical purity. Shenzhen in particular became a template that other developing nations—from Vietnam's Doi Moi reforms to India's Special Economic Zones Act of 2005—studied and adapted.
