# State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs)

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: Deng Xiaoping, Jiang Zemin, Zhu Rongji, Premier Li Keqiang, Xi Jinping

## Summary

State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs; 国有企业, guoyou qiye) are corporations in which the Chinese state—typically through ministry holdings, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC), or provincial ownership—retains majority or controlling ownership. SOEs dominate China's strategic sectors: energy (coal, oil, hydroelectric), telecommunications, aviation, steel, chemicals, and banking. The SOE system originates in Mao Zedong's communist planned economy, where state ownership of all productive assets was ideological doctrine. However, SOEs have persisted and evolved through Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 reforms, market liberalization, and Xi Jinping's consolidation, proving far more resilient than Western observers expected. The paradox of Chinese economic reform is that while the state has permitted private enterprise and market mechanisms to flourish in consumer goods and services, it has retained iron control over SOEs in sectors deemed strategically vital. Since the 1990s, repeated reform initiatives—corporatization, partial privatization (allowing minority share sales), separation of ownership and management, and managerial compensation tied to profitability—have sought to make SOEs operate more like market enterprises while remaining state-controlled. Yet SOEs have proven chronically unprofitable relative to private firms, burden the state with debt, resist reform, and consume vast state resources (capital allocation, subsidies, tax breaks). Simultaneously, they provide employment, control key infrastructure, enforce party discipline, and enable state direction of the economy—functions the Communist Party deems irreplaceable. This tension between market efficiency and state control defines contemporary China's economic model.

## Historical Development

### Maoist Foundation (1949–1978)

The People's Republic's founding in 1949 inaugurated the wholesale nationalization of Chinese industry. By the First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957), modeled closely on Soviet industrial planning, virtually all significant industrial assets were state-owned. Private enterprises were progressively absorbed into state collectives through the 1950s, a process completed under the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962). By 1978, China had approximately 83,000 state-owned industrial enterprises, virtually all operating under central planning with no profit incentive. Factory managers were administrators, not executives; production targets were set by ministries in Beijing. The results were chronic inefficiency, poor product quality, and suppressed innovation—but also full employment and social stability through the *danwei* (work unit) system, by which SOEs provided workers housing, healthcare, schooling, and pensions.

### Early Reform Era (1978–1993)

Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 reforms initially touched SOEs cautiously. The 1984 "Decision on Economic System Reform" granted SOEs greater managerial autonomy—the right to retain profits above plan targets—while preserving state ownership. A "dual-track" pricing system allowed SOEs to sell above-quota output at market prices alongside below-quota output at fixed state prices. This created enormous arbitrage opportunities and fueled corruption, but also injected market incentives. Township and village enterprises (TVEs)—quasi-private collective firms—flourished in the 1980s as market competitors while SOEs remained protected. By the early 1990s, it was clear that SOEs were losing market share to private and foreign enterprises in consumer goods and were financially deteriorating.

### Corporatization and the "Grasp the Large, Release the Small" Era (1993–2003)

Premier Zhu Rongji's dramatic SOE reforms of the late 1990s mark the most consequential restructuring of the sector. The 1993 Company Law converted SOEs from administrative units into corporate legal entities. The 15th Party Congress (1997) endorsed the "grasp the large, release the small" (抓大放小, zhuā dà fàng xiǎo) strategy: major SOEs in strategic sectors would be retained and strengthened, while small and medium SOEs would be sold, merged, or liquidated. Between 1996 and 2003, approximately 30 million SOE workers were laid off (xiagang)—a social convulsion China had not experienced since the 1950s—and roughly half of China's 100,000+ small SOEs were privatized. The number of large central SOEs fell from over 100,000 to under 50,000. SASAC was established in 2003 to centralize oversight of the remaining large national SOEs.

### Consolidation and Global Expansion (2003–2012)

Under Hu Jintao and Wen Jiabao, the remaining large central SOEs (央企, yangqi) were consolidated into about 120 major conglomerates under SASAC supervision. A phenomenon critics termed "guojin mintui" (国进民退, "state advance, private retreat") was documented from roughly 2008 onward: following the Global Financial Crisis, the state directed massive stimulus spending through SOEs, which used their privileged access to capital and land to expand into sectors previously dominated by private firms. By 2012, the 120 central SOEs collectively held assets exceeding 33 trillion RMB and generated revenues accounting for roughly 10% of GDP—though their return on assets averaged roughly half that of private sector firms.

### Xi Jinping Era: Party Penetration and Selective Reform (2012–present)

Xi Jinping's consolidation of power brought new dynamics to SOE governance. The 2015 "Guidelines on Deepening SOE Reform" (Guo Fa [2015] No. 22) articulated a distinction between "commercial" SOEs (subject to market discipline and profitability targets) and "public interest" SOEs (prioritizing social functions over profit). These guidelines were widely interpreted as signaling a willingness to allow greater private participation in commercial SOEs via "mixed ownership" (混合所有制) investments. However, implementation remained uneven; private investors found it difficult to exercise meaningful governance rights in practice. More significantly, Xi's reforms emphasized embedding Communist Party committees more deeply in SOE governance—explicitly requiring that party committees be written into company charters and that they exercise oversight of major business decisions. This represented a reversal of Zhu Rongji-era efforts to separate party and management functions.

## Structure and Scale

### SASAC and Central SOEs

SASAC (国务院国有资产监督管理委员会, established 2003) supervises the central SOEs (yangqi) on behalf of the State Council. As of 2024, SASAC directly oversees approximately 98 central enterprise groups (reduced from 196 in 2003 through mergers). These include China's largest corporations by asset value:

- **Energy**: China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC/PetroChina), China Petroleum & Chemical Corporation (Sinopec), China National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC), State Power Investment Corporation, China Three Gorges Corporation
- **Telecommunications**: China Mobile, China Unicom, China Telecom
- **Transport**: Air China, China Eastern Airlines, China Southern Airlines, China State Railway Group
- **Finance**: Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC), China Construction Bank, Bank of China, Agricultural Bank of China—the four largest banks by assets in the world
- **Construction**: China Communications Construction, China Railway Construction Corporation, China State Construction Engineering

SASAC-supervised central SOEs collectively held total assets of approximately 81 trillion RMB (~$11 trillion) as of 2023 and employed over 6 million people directly.

### Provincial and Local SOEs

Beyond central SOEs, Chinese provinces and municipalities own additional state enterprises—provincial SOEs (地方国企) that are often as large as major central SOEs in their own right. Guangdong's state enterprises, for example, include major entities in petrochemicals, utilities, and real estate. Total SOE assets including provincial entities substantially exceed central SOE figures; estimates put total state-sector assets at 200+ trillion RMB.

### "Mixed Ownership" SOEs

A growing category involves SOEs that have sold minority stakes to private investors through partial IPOs or mixed-ownership reform. Entities like China Unicom (which attracted investments from Alibaba, Tencent, JD.com, and others in 2017 for about 78 billion RMB) represent this hybrid form—formally state-controlled but with private shareholders exercising limited market discipline.

## Performance and Efficiency Debates

SOE financial performance has been a persistent concern among economists. Studies consistently find that SOEs generate lower returns on assets, lower total factor productivity, and lower profitability than comparable private firms—often by margins of 50–80%. A widely cited 2015 analysis by economists at the People's Bank of China found that SOEs with roughly 40% of total industrial assets generated only 22% of industrial profits; private firms with 60% of assets generated 78% of profits.

However, defenders of SOEs note that profitability comparisons are misleading because SOEs disproportionately operate in capital-intensive, long-cycle industries (steel, energy, chemicals) rather than consumer goods where private firms excel. SOEs also bear social costs—maintaining employment, investing in infrastructure for under-served regions, and accepting below-market-rate pricing mandates—that private firms do not.

The concept of "soft budget constraints" (a term from Hungarian economist János Kornai, applied extensively to Chinese SOEs) captures the fundamental problem: because the state will ultimately bail out failing SOEs, they face no credible threat of bankruptcy and thus lack incentives for efficiency. The 2015 bankruptcy of several large steel SOEs was exceptional enough to make news precisely because SOE bankruptcies were so rare.

## SOEs and Foreign Policy

Chinese SOEs, particularly in construction, energy, and telecommunications, serve as primary instruments of Chinese foreign economic policy. Under the [Belt and Road Initiative](belt-and-road-initiative.md), SOEs including China Communications Construction, Power Construction Corporation of China (PowerChina), and China National Machinery Industry Corporation (Sinomach) have contracted infrastructure projects—ports, railways, roads, power plants—in over 100 countries. Critics note that BRI projects often feature Chinese state financing, Chinese SOE contractors, and Chinese labor, limiting benefits to host countries and creating debt dependencies.

In telecommunications, Huawei (technically a private company with unusual ownership structures) and ZTE (a state-controlled SOE) have become focal points of US-China technology rivalry, with Western governments restricting their participation in 5G networks over national security concerns related to their state connections.

## Significance

SOEs represent the structural foundation of China's distinctive "socialist market economy"—a system that combines capitalist market mechanisms with Communist Party ownership and control of commanding heights. **Economically**, SOEs control roughly 30% of national assets and employ tens of millions; their poor profitability and capital inefficiency drain state resources, yet their strategic role is deemed non-negotiable by party leadership. **Politically**, SOEs are instruments of state policy—they can be directed to invest in unprofitable regions for political stability, hire surplus workers to prevent unemployment-driven unrest, or fund party priorities. **Ideologically**, SOE retention allows the Communist Party to claim fidelity to socialism while adopting market mechanisms; without SOEs, the party would lose its claim to socialist character. **Strategically**, control of SOEs in energy, telecommunications, and defense-related sectors ensures the state can direct national resources in crises and resist Western economic pressure. **Globally**, Chinese SOEs are vehicles for state foreign policy, funding Belt and Road projects and establishing Chinese economic presence worldwide. **Domestically**, SOE reform has repeatedly fallen short; efforts to improve efficiency and corporate governance have been undermined by party interference, soft-budget constraints, and the imperative to prioritize employment and stability over profit.
