## Summary

Agriculture and land reform form the foundation of China's modern economic and political transformation. From the 1949 Communist victory through the Deng Xiaoping reforms of the 1980s, the CPC's control and redistribution of agricultural land were instruments of both revolutionary legitimacy and economic policy. Land reform redistributed holdings from landlords to peasants (1947–1952), creating a grateful rural constituency; collectivization (1953–1958) consolidated state control over production; the Great Leap Forward's people's communes (1958–1962) attempted to industrialize agriculture and collectivize consumption, leading to catastrophic famine; and the Cultural Revolution disrupted agricultural administration. Deng's 1980s household-responsibility system partially de-collectivized farming, raising productivity but leaving fundamental tenure insecurity. The agrarian economy remains central to China's development, yet rural-urban inequality, environmental degradation, the Hukou system's restrictions on migration, and the rise of agribusiness have reshaped farming's social and economic role.

## Traditional Agriculture and Landlord Tenure (Pre-1949)

Imperial China's agricultural system rested on landlord ownership and peasant tenancy. A small landlord class—estimated at roughly 4% of the rural population by the 1930s—controlled approximately 50% of cultivated land in some provinces. Peasants worked as tenants, sharecroppers, or landless laborers, often paying rents of 40–60% of annual harvests. Debt bondage compounded tenancy servitude: rural usury rates commonly ran at 36–60% annually, keeping poor peasants in permanent arrears. Tax farming, military conscription, and natural disasters (floods on the Huang He and Yangtze, droughts in Gansu and Shaanxi, locust plagues) periodically triggered peasant rebellions—the Nian Rebellion (1851–1868), the Boxer Uprising (1899–1900), and numerous local jacqueries punctuate the dynastic twilight.

By the late Qing and Republican era, land inequality intensified dramatically. The collapse of the Qing dynasty in 1912 transferred political power to regional warlords who sustained the landlord system as a revenue base. The Japanese invasion (1937–1945) devastated rural infrastructure in Yangtze valley provinces and forced millions of refugees into an already overcrowded countryside. The Chinese Civil War (1927–1949) brought further displacement, taxation, and confiscation. Rural land surveys in the 1930s, including John Lossing Buck's landmark study of Chinese farm economy, found that the richest tenth of rural households owned roughly half of cultivated land, while the poorest majority held only a small fraction.

The Communist Party, born in Shanghai's urban labor movement in 1921, gradually recognized that peasant grievances over land and landlord exploitation were its most powerful organizing force. Mao Zedong's February 1927 *Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan* documented the violent energy of peasant organizing and argued that the CPC should channel rather than restrain it. By the Jiangxi Soviet (1931–1934) and the Yan'an base areas (1935–1945), the CPC's promise of land redistribution became central to its legitimacy—a crucial source of recruits, porters, food, and popular support that the Nationalists never replicated. When the Long March (October 1934 – October 1935) ended in the loess hills of Shaanxi, Mao's exhausted army was surrounded by tens of millions of peasants who could be mobilized if given hope of owning their own land.

## Land Reform and Peasant Mobilization (1947–1952)

The CPC's first revolutionary act after winning the Civil War (1949) was systematic land reform. Land reform in Communist-controlled areas had actually begun as early as 1947 in Manchuria (Dongbei) and the North China Plain, governed by the **Outline Land Law** (土地法大纲) promulgated on October 10, 1947. After the People's Republic was founded on October 1, 1949, the **Land Reform Law** of June 28, 1950 formalized the national campaign, which proceeded province by province through 1952.

The redistribution process unfolded through village-level "struggle sessions" (*pidouhui*) in which poor peasants were assembled to publicly denounce landlords and rich peasants, confiscate their properties, and receive title deeds to redistributed land. These sessions, orchestrated by CPC work teams, were often violent: landlords and wealthier farmers were beaten, publicly humiliated, and frequently executed. Scholarly estimates of deaths attributable to land reform range widely—from 200,000 (official-adjacent estimates) to 2 million (Frank Dikötter's archival work). The violence was state-sanctioned and instrumental: it consolidated the poor peasantry's psychological and material stake in the new regime by making them complicit in its founding act.

By 1952, approximately **300 million rural poor** had received a total of **47 million hectares** (116 million acres) of redistributed land. An additional 45 million peasant households received draft animals, farm tools, grain, and housing. The average redistribution gave each household roughly 0.1–0.2 hectares—economically viable only for subsistence farming.

This land reform was both economically rational and politically astute. It lifted hundreds of millions out of feudal-like dependence, raised agricultural morale, and solved a land question that had plagued Chinese politics since the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864). However, it left landholdings tiny and fragmented—the average peasant plot of 0.1–0.2 hectares was insufficient for mechanized cultivation—and created a rural population with both newfound stakes in the Communist regime and limited economies of scale. These structural problems set the stage for the collectivization drive that followed almost immediately.

## Collectivization and the Agricultural Communes (1953–1961)

Rather than leave peasants as smallholders, the CPC moved quickly to collectivization, beginning in 1953 with mutual-aid teams and progressing to agricultural cooperatives (1954–1956) and finally to people's communes (1958). Collectivization aimed to:

1. **Increase grain requisitions** for urban feeding and Soviet-style industrialization.
2. **Maximize state control** over agricultural surplus and peasant labor.
3. **Mechanize and "modernize"** farming through centralized resource allocation.

The process paralleled Stalin's collectivization in the Soviet Union, and like Stalin's, it was achieved through coercion. Richer peasants (kulaks) resisted; the state applied pressure; by 1956, roughly 96% of peasant households were in producer cooperatives. Peasants lost control over planting decisions, crop disposition, and work schedules.

The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) accelerated collectivization into people's communes—vast administrative units (averaging 5,000–10,000 households) that merged agricultural production, light industry, defense, education, and even dining and childcare into a single collective. Communes were meant to rival factories in efficiency and size. They were also instruments of mass mobilization: peasants were diverted from farming to backyard steel furnaces, dam construction, and other crash projects as part of Mao's vision of instant industrialization. Simultaneously, inflated grain-production targets led local cadres to overstate harvests; the state's requisition system seized whatever grain was claimed as surplus, even as crops failed due to droughts, floods, and poor cultivation.

## The Great Famine and Agricultural Catastrophe (1959–1962)

The result was history's deadliest famine. Estimated deaths range from 15 to 45 million (most scholars suggest 30 million), concentrated in 1959–1962. The famine was partly meteorological—several provinces suffered droughts and floods—but primarily man-made: state requisitions of grain that left rural populations starving; the diversion of peasants from farming to industrial projects; the hoarding of grain by local cadres to meet inflated targets; and the suppression of information about shortages.

The famine exposed the fatal weakness of Mao's agrarian collectivism: it severed peasants' connection to their own labor's rewards, destroyed incentives for careful cultivation, and placed power over life-and-death decisions in the hands of cadres answerable to higher party echelons, not to the people they governed. The Lushan Conference (1959) purged critical voices (notably Peng Dehuai), preventing early course correction. It was not until 1961–1962 that communes were scaled back and peasants granted small private plots.

## Reformist Adjustment and Renewed Collectivism (1962–1966)

After the famine's nadir, Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping presided over a pragmatic retrenchment. Communes were reduced in size; peasants were allowed small private plots (0.05 hectares) for vegetable growing and pig raising; material incentives partially replaced ideological exhortation; and some market activity was tolerated. Agricultural production recovered, and grain stocks stabilized.

However, Mao resisted this pragmatism as a betrayal of socialist principles. His Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) re-radicalized agriculture, disrupting the commune system anew and generating chaos. Productivity stagnated; rural poverty deepened; and by the 1970s, agricultural yields per capita had barely grown since the 1950s, a failure that contrasted sharply with the Green Revolution's spread across Asia.

## The Deng Reforms and the Household-Responsibility System (1978–1984)

Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 reforms fundamentally restructured agricultural incentives. Their origins lay not in central planning but in spontaneous local experimentation: in late 1978, eighteen peasant households in **Xiaogang village, Fengyang county, Anhui province**, secretly signed a contract to divide commune land among themselves and take individual responsibility for production quotas. This illegal bottom-up reform yielded a dramatic harvest increase; local and then provincial officials quietly permitted it rather than enforcing collectivism. The significance of the reform lay in the central leadership's decision to recognize and legitimize a practice already spreading on the ground rather than suppress it.

The resulting **household-responsibility system** (包干到户, *baogan daohu*) distributed collective land to individual households in long-term-use contracts (initially 3–5 years, later extended to 15 years under the 1984 *Central Document No. 1*, and 30 years under 1993 legislation). Peasant households were assigned plots proportional to family size and given production quotas; after delivering the state's quota at fixed procurement prices, they could sell surplus grain at free-market prices and grow non-grain crops for sale. Land remained nominally collective—owned by the village—rather than privately owned, preserving the ideological framework of socialism while restoring material incentives.

The results were dramatic and rapid:

- **Grain output** rose from 305 million tonnes in 1978 to 407 million tonnes in 1984—a 34% increase in six years.
- **Rural per-capita income** nearly doubled in real terms from 1978 to 1985, from roughly 134 yuan to 398 yuan.
- **Agricultural diversification** accelerated: peasants shifted to vegetables, fruit, aquaculture, poultry, and pig-raising for market sale, breaking the grain monoculture of the commune era.
- **Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs)** emerged alongside the household system: collectively owned but market-oriented factories in rural areas employed millions and contributed to rapid industrialization of the countryside.

However, the household-responsibility system perpetuated structural insecurity. Land remained nominally collective, and village cadres periodically reallocated plots to adjust for new births and deaths or to equalize plot quality—creating a disincentive to invest in long-term soil improvement, terracing, or irrigation. Peasants could not freely buy, sell, mortgage, or lease land; this constrained agricultural capital accumulation. Moreover, the 1989–1991 post-Tiananmen retrenchment saw central authorities re-emphasize grain self-sufficiency, increase procurement quotas at fixed prices, and restrict rural market activity, squeezing peasant incomes just as urban residents benefited from rapid industrial wage growth. This divergence in rural-urban fortunes ignited a wave of anti-tax peasant protests through the 1990s and 2000s.

## Marketization, Rural-Urban Divide, and Agribusiness (1990s–Present)

The 1990s saw the rise of agricultural markets and agribusiness. Township and village enterprises (TVEs)—collectively owned but market-oriented factories—proliferated, employing tens of millions of rural workers. However, the Hukou system (the household-registration barrier) restricted rural migrants' access to urban services (education, healthcare, pensions), creating a "floating population" of over 200 million by the 2000s—rural-born workers in cities without urban residency rights. Land, meanwhile, was increasingly consolidated through long-term leases (though still not freely bought and sold) into larger farms operated by agribusiness firms, and smallholder peasants became wage laborers.

The 1990s also witnessed the tax burden on rural areas increase, as fiscal centralization shifted revenues to Beijing but left provincial and county governments dependent on local taxation. Excessive agricultural levies and arbitrary fees sparked scattered peasant unrest, including the 1993 Renshou county protests in Sichuan over road-construction charges. Beginning around 2000, the state phased out and by 2006 fully abolished the centuries-old agricultural tax, easing rural grievances while shifting county finances onto other revenue sources.

Deng's 1992 Southern Tour renewed market reforms; by the 2000s, a capitalist-style land market emerged in practice, though officially illegal. Large agricultural corporations purchased or leased land from village collectives; peasants were compensated but displaced. The environmental costs were severe: erosion, chemical runoff from industrial farming, aquifer depletion, and desertification transformed rural landscapes. The 2003 Sun Zhigang case and subsequent police-brutality incidents highlighted the Hukou system's injustices. Rural-urban inequality, measured by income ratios, widened dramatically from the 1990s through the 2010s.

## Contemporary Challenges and the Dual-Track System

Today, Chinese agriculture remains structurally bifurcated: a nominally collective system (villages hold land "for the people" under the 1998 Land Management Law and its 2004 and 2019 amendments) coexists with market mechanisms (long-term leases, agribusiness contracts, land transfer markets, and market-priced sales). Peasants' land rights remain contingent and insecure. Local governments can expropriate collective land for urban expansion, infrastructure, and industrial parks—paying compensation based on agricultural-use value (typically 6–30 times annual output), rather than the market value of converted land, a gap that has generated enormous wealth for municipal governments and developers while leaving displaced peasants with inadequate settlement.

Xi Jinping's **Rural Vitalization Strategy** (乡村振兴战略), enshrined in the 2018 State Council plan and elevated to a central policy priority in the 2021 "Rural Vitalization Promotion Law," aims to close the rural-urban income gap and reverse decades of agricultural neglect. Its components include:

- **Land consolidation**: Encouraging small-plot holders to transfer contracts to agribusiness operators and agricultural cooperatives, creating larger, more efficient farms. By 2022, the area under "appropriate-scale" farming exceeded 900 million *mu* (60 million hectares).
- **Digital agriculture**: Drone-based crop monitoring, precision fertilization, and satellite mapping of arable land. The Ministry of Agriculture launched a national "digital village" pilot program in 2019.
- **Rural e-commerce**: By 2022, rural online retail sales reached approximately 2.17 trillion yuan (≈ $300 billion USD), integrating small farmers into supply chains via platforms like JD Agri and Taobao.
- **Poverty alleviation**: The 2015–2020 targeted poverty-alleviation campaign lifted approximately 98.99 million rural residents above the national poverty line (2,300 yuan per year in 2011 prices), reducing the rural poverty rate from 10.2% (2012) to 0% (declared in 2021).

Environmental degradation remains severe. Industrial-scale farming has led to aquifer depletion in the North China Plain (water tables dropping 1–3 meters per year), soil contamination affecting 16.1% of all surveyed land and 19.4% of arable land (per the 2014 National Soil Pollution Survey), and tens of millions of hectares at risk of desertification. The 2018 Soil Pollution Prevention and Control Law (effective 2019) and subsequent regulations set thresholds for cadmium, arsenic, and mercury in agricultural soils, but enforcement remains inconsistent.

The Great Leap Forward famine and the subsequent rural-urban inequality remain sensitive political topics. The state acknowledges the famine's "hardships" but frames them as an unavoidable tragedy of rapid modernization and adverse weather, not as policy failure. Independent demographic research by Judith Banister (1987) and Jasper Becker (1997) placed excess deaths at 30 million or higher; this scholarship circulates among Chinese academics but is not officially endorsed. Deng's reforms are lionized, but their incompleteness—the inability to fully commercialize land, the persistence of Hukou stratification, and environmental damage from intensive farming—defines the agrarian challenges still confronting the Communist Party in its second century.
