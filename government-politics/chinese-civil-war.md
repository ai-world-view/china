# Chinese Civil War

## Summary

The Chinese Civil War (1927–1950, with major fighting 1945–1949) was the armed conflict between the Nationalist Party (Kuomintang / KMT) and the Communist Party of China (CPC) for control of mainland China. The conflict determined the political future of China: the Communist victory in 1949 established the People's Republic of China under Mao Zedong's leadership, while the defeated Nationalists retreated to Taiwan and maintained the Republic of China government there. The war's outcome reshaped global geopolitics, installed communism in the world's most populous nation, and shaped Cold War divisions for decades. Military and civilian deaths across the entire conflict (1927–1949) are estimated at between 1.2 and 6 million, with additional millions displaced or dying from war-induced famine and disease.

## Origins and Background (1912–1927)

The civil war grew from the collapse of the Chinese imperial system following the Xinhai Revolution of October 1911, which overthrew the Qing Dynasty. The Republic of China (ROC), founded by Sun Yat-sen, struggled from its inception: regional warlords commanded their own armies, the Beijing government controlled little territory, and Sun's Nationalist Party (Kuomintang, KMT) was politically marginalized. Sun reorganized the KMT in 1923 with Soviet Comintern assistance and established a military academy at Whampoa (near Guangzhou) in 1924, with Chiang Kai-shek as its commandant and Soviet advisors shaping its curriculum. The Chinese Communist Party, founded in Shanghai in July 1921 by Chen Duxiu, Li Dazhao, and others (with Mao Zedong among the early participants), initially allied with the KMT in a "United Front" brokered by the Soviets.

## First Phase: Open War (1927–1937)

### The Shanghai Massacre and Rupture (April 1927)

Sun Yat-sen died in March 1925, and Chiang Kai-shek emerged as the dominant KMT military commander. In 1926–1927, the KMT's Northern Expedition swept north from Guangzhou, unifying much of southern and central China against the warlords. As the KMT forces approached Shanghai, Chiang turned against his Communist partners. On April 12, 1927, KMT forces and affiliated criminal organizations (notably the Green Gang) launched simultaneous attacks on Communist party offices and labor unions in Shanghai, killing hundreds of workers and activists in what became known as the Shanghai Massacre (or "April 12 Incident"). Similar purges followed in other cities. The united front collapsed; the CPC went underground or fled to the countryside.

### Communist Rural Strategy and Encirclement Campaigns (1927–1934)

Following the failed urban uprisings of 1927 (including the Autumn Harvest Uprising led by Mao in Hunan), surviving Communist forces retreated to remote rural areas. Mao Zedong and General Zhu De established the Jinggangshan base area in the Jinggang Mountains on the Hunan-Jiangxi border in 1927–1928, developing the tactics of guerrilla warfare and rural mass mobilization that would define Communist military strategy for the next two decades. The Jiangxi Soviet (Chinese Soviet Republic) was proclaimed at Ruijin, Jiangxi, in November 1931, with Mao as Chairman.

Chiang Kai-shek launched five successive "encirclement and annihilation" campaigns against the Communist base areas. The first four (1930–1933) failed; Communist forces exploited their interior lines and knowledge of terrain to defeat numerically superior Nationalist forces. The fifth campaign (1933–1934), directed by German military advisor Hans von Seeckt, employed a methodical advance of blockhouses, roads, and strangling fortifications that the Communists could not break.

### The Long March (October 1934 – October 1935)

In October 1934, approximately 86,000 Communist fighters and party cadres broke out of the encircled Jiangxi Soviet and began a strategic retreat westward. The Long March covered roughly 9,600 kilometers (approximately 6,000 miles) of some of China's most difficult terrain: mountain ranges, swamps, high-altitude grasslands, and rivers. Pursuing Nationalist forces, hostile local populations in some areas, disease, cold, and starvation reduced the army to roughly 8,000 survivors by its end. The march traversed eleven provinces and crossed eighteen major mountain ranges and twenty-four rivers.

The Zunyi Conference of January 1935 (in Guizhou Province) was a pivotal moment: Mao Zedong asserted his authority over the military leadership, criticizing the Comintern-backed strategy that had led to defeat in Jiangxi and consolidating his position within the party. The Long March concluded in October 1935 when surviving forces reached Yan'an in Shaanxi Province, which became the new Communist base. Though a military defeat, the Long March became the founding myth of Chinese Communist identity—an epic of endurance, sacrifice, and revolutionary will that Mao and subsequent leaders repeatedly invoked.

## The Second United Front and the Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945)

### Japanese Invasion and Forced Cooperation

Japan's full-scale invasion of China began with the Marco Polo Bridge Incident of July 7, 1937, near Beijing. Facing national extinction, Chiang Kai-shek negotiated a second united front with the Communists (formalized September 1937), under which Communist forces nominally integrated into the National Revolutionary Army as the Eighth Route Army (in the north) and the New Fourth Army (in central China). In practice, each party conserved its military forces and built its political base while fighting Japan.

The Nationalists bore the brunt of conventional Japanese military pressure. The Battle of Shanghai (August–November 1937) cost the KMT some 200,000 casualties. Nanjing fell in December 1937; the subsequent Nanjing Massacre killed an estimated 80,000–200,000 civilians and prisoners of war (figures remain contested). Chiang's government retreated to Chongqing, in Sichuan Province, which served as the wartime capital.

### Diverging Fortunes

The eight-year war profoundly affected each party differently:

- **Nationalists**: Constant conventional warfare against a modernized Japanese military, combined with economic blockade, inflation (the KMT printed money to fund the war), and corrupt wartime administration, exhausted and discredited the KMT. US military aid began flowing after Pearl Harbor (December 1941), but relations between US General Joseph Stilwell and Chiang Kai-shek deteriorated badly over strategy and corruption.

- **Communists**: From Yan'an, the CPC expanded its base areas across north China through guerrilla operations and intensive political work. Land reform redistributed land from landlords to peasants, winning mass rural support. By 1945 the Communist armies numbered approximately 900,000 regular troops and commanded a population of roughly 90 million in their base areas. The disciplined, land-reforming Communist forces contrasted starkly in peasant perception with the often predatory Nationalist conscription and taxation systems.

### New Fourth Army Incident (January 1941)

Despite the united front, tensions erupted in January 1941 when Nationalist forces ambushed the New Fourth Army's headquarters column in Anhui Province, killing over 2,000 and capturing the Communist commander Ye Ting. The incident ended practical military cooperation between the two parties for the remainder of the war.

## The Final Phase: Communist Victory (1945–1949)

### Postwar Situation and the Marshall Mission

Japan's sudden surrender in August 1945 triggered immediate competition for control of Japanese-held territory, especially Manchuria, Japan's most industrialized Chinese possession. Soviet forces, entering the war against Japan in August 1945 per the Yalta Agreement, occupied Manchuria and facilitated the transfer of captured Japanese weapons—including artillery, tanks, and ammunition—to Communist forces, providing Mao's armies with modern heavy equipment for the first time.

US President Harry Truman dispatched General George C. Marshall to China in December 1945 to negotiate a ceasefire and coalition government. The Marshall Mission lasted through early 1947 but failed: neither side was willing to accept a genuinely shared power arrangement. Truman recalled Marshall in January 1947, acknowledging the failure.

### Military Campaigns (1946–1949)

The Nationalists initially held advantages: a larger army (approximately 4 million vs. Communist 1.3 million at war's end), control of most major cities, and continued US military assistance ($2 billion in aid, 1945–1949). Despite these advantages, KMT forces rapidly deteriorated due to overextension, poor logistics, and catastrophic hyperinflation that destroyed urban support. Communist forces, renamed the People's Liberation Army (PLA) in 1947, implemented mobile warfare doctrine and benefited from mass defections from demoralized Nationalist units.

Three decisive campaigns in late 1948 and early 1949 determined the outcome:

- **Liaoshen Campaign** (September–November 1948, Manchuria): PLA forces under Lin Biao encircled and destroyed approximately 470,000 Nationalist troops, securing all of Manchuria for the Communists.

- **Huai-Hai Campaign** (November 1948 – January 1949, east-central China): The largest battle of the civil war, involving approximately 600,000 PLA troops against 550,000 Nationalists. PLA forces under Chen Yi and Liu Bocheng annihilated multiple Nationalist armies; approximately 200,000 Nationalist troops were killed or wounded, and 327,000 surrendered. The victory opened the path to the Yangtze River and the south.

- **Pingjin Campaign** (November 1948 – January 1949, north China): Beijing and Tianjin fell with minimal resistance; general Fu Zuoyi negotiated a peaceful surrender of Beijing on January 31, 1949, preserving the ancient capital from bombardment.

### Fall of the Yangtze Line and Nationalist Collapse

On April 21, 1949, the PLA crossed the Yangtze River on a front of over 1,200 kilometers. Nanjing (the Nationalist capital) fell on April 23. Shanghai fell on May 25 after brief fighting. Canton (Guangzhou) fell on October 14; Chongqing on November 30; Chengdu in December. By the end of 1949 the Nationalist government had ceased to function on the mainland.

### Nationalist Retreat to Taiwan

In November–December 1949, Chiang Kai-shek oversaw the evacuation of approximately 1.2–2 million military personnel, government officials, and civilians to Taiwan. The Nationalists transported the bulk of the Central Bank of China's gold reserves (estimates of the amount vary widely), cultural artifacts from the Palace Museum (which formed the core of Taiwan's National Palace Museum collection), and the governing apparatus of the Republic of China. Chiang formally resumed the presidency of the ROC on Taiwan in March 1950.

## Proclamation of the People's Republic

On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the founding of the People's Republic of China from the Gate of Heavenly Peace (*Tiananmen*) in Beijing, declaring that "the Chinese people have stood up." The PLA briefly continued operations: Hainan Island was taken in April–May 1950, and Tibet was incorporated under military pressure in 1950–1951. The Republic of China on Taiwan retained a UN Security Council seat until 1971 and was recognized by the United States as the legitimate government of China until 1979.

## The Korean War's Impact (1950–1953)

The Korean War, which began in June 1950, fundamentally altered the cross-strait situation. President Truman dispatched the Seventh Fleet to the Taiwan Strait in June 1950, preventing a planned PLA amphibious assault on Taiwan. The PRC's entry into the Korean War in October 1950 with approximately 300,000 "People's Volunteers" solidified US-PRC hostility. US military and economic support for Taiwan stabilized the ROC government, postponing indefinitely the civil war's final military resolution.

## Causes of Communist Victory

Scholars have debated why the Communists won despite beginning from profound disadvantages. Key factors include:

- **Land reform and peasant mobilization**: CPC promises and implementation of land redistribution won massive rural support in a country where 80% of the population were peasants.
- **Military strategy**: Mao's emphasis on mobile warfare, guerrilla tactics, and the principle of concentrating forces to destroy enemies in detail proved consistently superior to Nationalist positional defense.
- **Political discipline and organization**: Communist party cadres maintained ideological coherence and popular work among civilian populations; KMT forces in contrast were riddled with corruption and poor morale.
- **Economic collapse**: KMT hyperinflation destroyed urban middle-class support. Chiang's government issued 60 trillion Chinese yuan notes between 1945 and 1949; by late 1948 inflation ran at over 1,000% monthly.
- **Soviet assistance**: Soviet transfer of Japanese weapons in Manchuria provided the PLA with heavy equipment critical for conventional operations.
- **US withdrawal of unconditional support**: Though the US continued aid, it was insufficient to offset KMT political and military failures; the "loss of China" became a bitter American political issue.

## Long-term Significance

The Chinese Civil War's outcome reshaped the 20th century. The People's Republic of China—home to roughly 25% of the world's population at its founding—became a nuclear power (1964), a permanent UN Security Council member (1971), and eventually the world's second-largest economy. The Nationalist government on Taiwan evolved from authoritarian KMT rule into a democratic state by the 1990s, creating a compelling contrast with the mainland's one-party system. The fundamental question of Taiwan's status—the unfinished business of the civil war—remains the most dangerous potential flashpoint in contemporary geopolitics, with Beijing insisting on eventual unification and Taiwan maintaining its de facto independence.

The war also demonstrated the power of rural revolutionary movements against urban conventional military establishments, a model studied by revolutionary movements globally from Vietnam to Africa to Latin America.
