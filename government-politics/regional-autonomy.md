# Regional Autonomy

Regional autonomy in China refers to the constitutional and administrative system through which ethnic minorities govern themselves in designated autonomous regions, autonomous prefectures, and autonomous counties. Established as a cornerstone of the People's Republic's governance model since 1949, the autonomy system embodies a theoretical commitment to minority rights within the framework of Communist Party control, though the tension between autonomy and centralized authority has defined the system's entire history.

## Ethnic Composition and Scope

China officially recognizes 56 *minzu* (民族, ethnic nationalities): the Han majority and 55 ethnic minorities. The minorities together number approximately 125 million people as of the 2020 census, roughly 8.9 percent of the total population, but they are concentrated in strategically vital borderland regions comprising well over half of China's territory. The five autonomous regions—Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, Ningxia, and Guangxi—cover approximately 64 percent of China's land area. Alongside the five autonomous regions, China recognizes 30 autonomous prefectures and 120 autonomous counties and banners (*qi*), for a total of over 155 autonomous administrative units at various levels.

The ten largest ethnic minorities recognized by the state are Zhuang (18 million), Hui (11.4 million), Manchu (10.4 million), Uyghur (10.1 million), Miao (9.4 million), Yi (8.7 million), Tujia (8 million), Tibetan (6.3 million), Mongol (6 million), and Dong (2.9 million)—all figures from the 2010 census, with the Uyghur population contested by later reports.

## Constitutional Foundation

The autonomy framework was written into the PRC's founding documents and elaborated in successive constitutions. Article 4 of the 1954 Constitution (the PRC's first) enshrined the principle of regional autonomy for ethnic minorities. Subsequent constitutions (1975, 1978, 1982, and amendments) have reaffirmed this principle, with the 1982 Constitution providing the most detailed specification of autonomy rights and duties. Articles 111–122 of the 1982 Constitution outline the structure of autonomous governance, the rights of autonomous legislatures to adapt national laws to local circumstances, the use of minority languages in governance and education, and the rights of autonomous units to manage local finances.

The most significant implementing legislation is the **Law on Regional National Autonomy** (民族区域自治法, LRNA), first passed by the National People's Congress on **September 1, 1984**, and substantially amended on February 28, 2001. The LRNA specifies in 74 articles the rights and duties of autonomous regions, including the right to enact autonomous regulations, use and develop minority languages, manage local economic affairs within the state plan, and retain a portion of local revenues. The 2001 amendment was partly a response to pressure from minority scholars and officials who argued the original law lacked enforcement mechanisms; however, critics noted the amendment still contained no penalties for central government violations of minority rights.

The system is organized in a three-tier hierarchy:

- **Autonomous Regions** (*zìzhìqū*, 自治区) — the largest and highest-level autonomous entities, with provincial-level status and theoretical self-governance over ethnic populations. Five autonomous regions were established: Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (1947, predating the PRC), Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region (October 1, 1955), Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (March 5, 1958), Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (October 25, 1958), and Tibet Autonomous Region (September 9, 1965).
- **Autonomous Prefectures** (*zìzhìzhōu*, 自治州) — sub-provincial administrative divisions within provinces or autonomous regions, typically encompassing ethnic minority populations. Examples include Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture in Jilin Province and Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture in Yunnan.
- **Autonomous Counties** (*zìzhìxiàn*, 自治县) — the lowest tier. Inner Mongolia also uses a unit called "autonomous banners" (*zìzhì qí*, 自治旗), a legacy of the Mongol administrative system.

## Governance Structure

Autonomous regions and prefectures possess nominal legislative and executive authority. Each autonomous entity elects a People's Congress (its local legislature) and establishes a local government (*People's Government*), theoretically empowered to enact regulations suited to the autonomous area's circumstances and minority cultures. The chairpersons of autonomous region governments and the heads of autonomous prefectures and counties are by law supposed to be citizens of the autonomous nationality—a formal concession to minority representation. Representation in local People's Congresses must also reflect the ethnic composition of the region.

However, the key operative distinction in Chinese governance is between the government apparatus and the Communist Party apparatus. While the formal head of government (e.g., the Chairman of the Tibet Autonomous Region government) has typically been Tibetan, the First Secretary of the Tibet Communist Party Committee—the actual locus of decision-making power—has been Han Chinese throughout most of the PRC's history. The same pattern applies in Xinjiang and other sensitive regions. Party committees operating in parallel with and above government structures ensure that autonomy remains largely formal.

The LRNA allows autonomous units to enact regulations (*zìzhì tiáolì*, 自治条例) and separate regulations (*dānxíng tiáolì*, 单行条例) adapting national laws to local conditions. In practice, few autonomous regulations have been enacted, and those that exist are carefully calibrated to avoid conflict with central policies. The approval process—autonomous regulations for ethnic regions require ratification by the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress—gives Beijing effective veto over minority legislation.

## Historical Practice and Evolution

### Soviet Model and Early Adaptation (1949–1956)

The Chinese autonomy model drew partly on the Soviet nationalities policy developed under Stalin—the system of union republics, autonomous oblasts, and autonomous okrugs that granted Soviet minorities nominal territorial identity. However, the Chinese Communist Party rejected the Soviet model's most significant feature: the right of union republics to secede from the union. Mao Zedong, from the late 1930s onward, moved away from earlier CCP statements supporting minority self-determination (including statements during the Long March that promised Tibetan and Uyghur independence) toward the position that all of China's historical territory must remain unified under the new state. The PRC's autonomy system was thus, from the beginning, designed to provide cultural recognition without political separability.

The Mongolian precedent was decisive. The Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region was proclaimed on May 1, 1947, two years before the PRC itself, as a way of incorporating Mongol-populated territories by offering Mongol leaders a degree of nominal self-rule. This early incorporation, managed by Mongolian Communist Ulanhu (*Wulanfu*, 乌兰夫), who served as First Secretary of the Inner Mongolia region from 1947 to 1966, became the template for subsequent autonomous regions.

### Early Period (1949–1966)

The early period of autonomy was marked by a dual strategy of political incorporation and cultural accommodation paired with economic and demographic integration. Han migration into autonomous regions, particularly Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, increased steadily during the 1950s through the settlement of demobilized soldiers (the **Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps**, or *Bingtuan*, 兵团, established 1954) and migration of Han civilians. The *Bingtuan* operated its own parallel administrative structure across Xinjiang, effectively establishing a Han-populated institutional network independent of the autonomous region government.

In Tibet, the 1951 **Seventeen-Point Agreement** (formally the "Agreement of the Central People's Government and the Local Government of Tibet on Measures for the Peaceful Liberation of Tibet") incorporated Tibet while notionally preserving the existing political system and the Dalai Lama's authority. This arrangement broke down with the 1959 Tibetan uprising, after which the Dalai Lama fled to India, the traditional government was abolished, and the Tibet Autonomous Region was formally constituted in 1965. The region's real administrative structures were then closely supervised by Beijing-appointed officials.

### Cultural Revolution and After (1966–1978)

During the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), the autonomy system functioned in name only. Red Guard campaigns targeting "the four olds" (old customs, old culture, old habits, old ideas) attacked minority religions, scripts, and cultural practices with particular force in Tibet, Inner Mongolia, and Xinjiang. In Inner Mongolia, the "Inner Mongolian People's Party" (IMPP) incident of 1968 led to a campaign of mass persecution in which tens of thousands of Mongolian cadres and intellectuals were accused of membership in a fabricated separatist organization; estimates of those killed range from 16,000 to over 100,000, with hundreds of thousands more tortured or imprisoned. Ulanhu, the region's founding leader, was purged in 1966.

The formal structures of autonomous governance were dismantled or subordinated to revolutionary committees. Minority-language education was suspended in many areas, and religious institutions (Buddhist monasteries in Tibet and Mongolian regions, mosques in Xinjiang and Ningxia) were closed, destroyed, or converted to secular uses. The period represented the nadir of the autonomy system's actually implemented protections.

### Reform Era (1978–2012)

The reform period under Deng Xiaoping brought nominal restoration of autonomy structures and selective relaxation of cultural restrictions. The rehabilitation of Ulanhu (1978) and others purged during the Cultural Revolution signaled a renewed commitment to minority representation. The 1982 Constitution provided the most robust constitutional basis for minority autonomy in PRC history, and the LRNA (1984) gave legislative form to these rights. Minority-language education was restored, religious institutions were allowed to reopen under state supervision, and autonomous regions gained somewhat more latitude in managing local economic affairs.

During the 1980s, autonomous regions gained authority over local natural resource management (within national plans), the right to retain a larger share of local tax revenues, and latitude in planning local economic development. This period saw significant economic growth in some autonomous regions, though critics argued that development policies prioritized Han migration and resource extraction over local minority welfare.

The 1989 events and their aftermath, combined with the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 and the sudden independence of the Soviet Central Asian republics (several with ethnic kin populations in Chinese Xinjiang), prompted Beijing to tighten security policies in minority regions. The Xinjiang separatist movements that were active during the 1990s—culminating in unrest in Urumqi and Yining (Ghulja) in 1997—led to "Strike Hard" (严打) crackdowns that prioritized security over cultural accommodation. These events established a pattern in which security concerns regularly overrode the nominal cultural protections of the autonomy framework.

### 21st-Century Tightening (2000–Present)

The most significant challenge to the autonomy system's cultural protections came in the early 21st century. The Tibetan protests of March 2008, which spread across Tibetan-populated areas of Sichuan, Qinghai, and Gansu as well as the Tibet Autonomous Region itself, and the **Urumqi riots of July 5, 2009**, in which at least 197 people died in clashes between Uyghur and Han residents, prompted Beijing to significantly intensify security measures.

The 2014 Kunming railway station knife attack (March 1, 2014), in which 29 people were killed by a group Beijing identified as Uyghur separatists, and the 2014 Urumqi car bomb attack (May 22, 2014) accelerated a shift toward comprehensive surveillance and population control in Xinjiang. The appointment of **Chen Quanguo** (陈全国) as Xinjiang Communist Party Secretary in August 2016 (he had previously served as Tibet Party Secretary from 2011 to 2016, where he implemented an extensive security grid) marked the beginning of an unprecedented expansion of the security state in Xinjiang.

From 2017 onward, the Xinjiang government established what it officially termed **Vocational Education and Training Centers** (职业技能教育培训中心)—facilities that international human rights organizations, foreign governments, and the United Nations human rights system have described as mass extrajudicial detention facilities. Estimates of the number detained range from several hundred thousand to over one million, primarily Uyghur Muslims but also members of the Kazakh and Kyrgyz communities. The facilities, combined with comprehensive biometric surveillance, restricted movement, mandatory Mandarin-language political education, and prohibitions on religious practice, represented an effective suspension of the autonomy system's cultural and religious protections in Xinjiang.

In Tibet, a wave of self-immolations—approximately 159 documented cases between 2008 and 2020—by monks, nuns, and laypeople protesting cultural and religious restrictions drew international attention to the deterioration of practical autonomy. Beijing responded with tightened controls on monasteries, restrictions on the Dalai Lama's image and teachings, and requirements for government-approved "patriotic education" in religious institutions.

In Inner Mongolia, a 2020 policy mandating reductions in Mongolian-language instruction in favor of Mandarin in three core subjects triggered rare public protests by Mongolian parents and students across the region—among the largest public demonstrations in Inner Mongolia in decades. Beijing maintained the policy but faced public acknowledgment of its unpopularity.

## The Autonomy-Control Tension

The fundamental contradiction of China's regional autonomy system has been the gap between constitutional text and institutional practice. Several mechanisms enforce central control:

- **Party Leadership Primacy** — Communist Party secretaries at all levels outrank elected executives; party committees make binding decisions that government organs implement. In sensitive regions, the Party Secretary has been Han Chinese while the nominal government head is from the local minority.
- **Personnel Control** — Beijing appoints or approves top officials in autonomous regions through the party nomenklatura system; the Xinjiang and Tibet party secretariats are staffed predominantly by Han officials appointed from outside.
- **Security Apparatus Autonomy** — Police, armed police (武警), and People's Liberation Army forces in autonomous regions operate under national command chains that bypass local autonomous governments.
- **Fiscal Dependence** — All five autonomous regions are net recipients of central fiscal transfers. Xinjiang receives some of the largest per-capita transfers in China; this fiscal dependence ensures that autonomous governments cannot sustain programs at odds with Beijing's priorities.
- **Institutional Subordination** — The National People's Congress must ratify autonomous regulations; the State Council can override autonomous decisions; and the party's central Organization Department controls senior cadre appointments.

## Minority Policy Debates

Within Chinese Communist discourse, the autonomy system is debated through competing frameworks. The mainstream official framework asserts "ethnic unity" (*minzu tuanjie*, 民族团结) and "common prosperity" (*gongtong fanrong*, 共同繁荣), presenting minority cultures as living traditions deserving protection within a unified Chinese state identity. This framework has been elaborated through successive party documents, most recently Xi Jinping's concept of "building a sense of community for the Chinese nation" (*zhonghua minzu gongtongti yishi*, 中华民族共同体意识), which emphasizes shared Chinese national identity across all *minzu*.

A significant intellectual debate emerged in the 2000s around proposals for a "second-generation ethnic policy" (*minzu zhengce di er dai*, 民族政策第二代), associated with scholars Ma Rong (Peking University) and Hu Angang (Tsinghua University). These scholars argued that the existing autonomy system entrenched ethnic divisions and contributed to separatism, and proposed shifting toward a "melting pot" model of cultural assimilation modeled partly on American immigration policy. The proposals were controversial and were not formally adopted as policy, but critics argued that Xi-era policies in Xinjiang effectively implemented assimilation through coercion.

Ethnic minority intellectuals, both inside China and in diaspora communities, have critiqued the autonomy system as providing nominal sovereignty without substantive power. Ilham Tohti, a Uyghur economics professor at Minzu University in Beijing, published detailed academic analyses of the gap between the LRNA's provisions and their implementation in Xinjiang; he was convicted of "separatism" in 2014 and sentenced to life imprisonment.

## Contemporary Role and Trajectory

In the 21st century, particularly under Xi Jinping (General Secretary since 2012), the autonomy system has been more tightly integrated into broader national policy frameworks. "Ecological civilization" conservation programs, "Belt and Road" infrastructure projects, poverty alleviation campaigns, and economic integration policies are applied uniformly across autonomous and non-autonomous regions, leaving little space for autonomous regional governments to diverge from national templates.

The "Strike Hard, Root Out" (*严打整治*) anti-terrorism campaigns in Xinjiang, the Living Buddha reincarnation approval system in Tibet (requiring state approval of reincarnations, including potentially the next Dalai Lama), and the Mongolian education language policy all illustrate the widening gap between the LRNA's text and the practical autonomy of minority regions.

International scrutiny of China's ethnic policies has grown substantially since the late 2010s. The United States, the European Parliament, the United Kingdom, Canada, and several other states have adopted resolutions or statements characterizing conditions in Xinjiang using terms including "genocide" or "crimes against humanity," charges Beijing strongly rejects as interference in internal affairs and misrepresentation of counter-terrorism measures. The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD) expressed concern in 2018 about conditions in Xinjiang, and a 2022 assessment by the UN Human Rights Office found that China's Xinjiang policies may constitute "international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity."

The fate of the regional autonomy system remains one of the most consequential questions in contemporary Chinese governance, with implications for social stability, international relations, China's stated commitment to constitutional governance, and the rights of approximately 125 million people.
