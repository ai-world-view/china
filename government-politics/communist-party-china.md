# The Communist Party of China

**Category**: Government & Politics  
**Founded**: July 1921, Shanghai (July 1 observed as the anniversary)  
**Key figures**: Mao Zedong, Deng Xiaoping, Jiang Zemin, Hu Jintao, Xi Jinping  
**Membership**: ~99.2 million (end of 2023)

## Summary

The Communist Party of China (CPC; Chinese: 中国共产党, *Zhōngguó Gòngchǎndǎng*) is the founding and ruling political party of the People's Republic of China (PRC). Established at its First National Congress, which convened in Shanghai in July 1921 (the party observes July 1 as its founding anniversary), the party emerged from intellectual and revolutionary currents responding to national humiliation, foreign imperialism, and internal weakness following the 1911 collapse of the Qing Dynasty. Under Mao Zedong's leadership, the CPC conducted a revolutionary struggle against the Republic of China government and Japanese occupation across the 1920s–1940s, ultimately establishing the PRC on October 1, 1949. The CPC has remained the sole governing party since, with a membership of approximately 99.2 million by the end of 2023 — the world's largest political party.

The party's organizational structure follows the Leninist principle of democratic centralism: decisions flow downward through a hierarchy from the National Congress (held every five years) to the Central Committee (currently ~205 full members) to the Politburo (24 members) to the Politburo Standing Committee (7 members), the apex of state power. Party committees parallel all state institutions at every administrative level. Under Deng Xiaoping's reforms (1978 onward), the party adopted market-oriented economic policies ("socialism with Chinese characteristics") while maintaining a monopoly on political power — a model that produced decades of rapid growth while attracting extensive scrutiny for governance of civil liberties.

## Founding and Revolutionary Period (1921–1949)

The CPC's founding congress met secretly in Shanghai in July 1921, initially with 13 delegates representing 57 members in six local groups. The party was established under Comintern guidance, reflecting Marxist-Leninist organizational principles imported from the Soviet Union. Chen Duxiu, a leading intellectual of the May Fourth Movement (1919), became the party's first General Secretary.

In 1923–1927, the CPC formed the First United Front with the Nationalist Party (Kuomintang, KMT) to resist warlordism and foreign imperialism. This alliance collapsed in April 1927 when KMT leader Chiang Kai-shek launched the "White Terror" — mass executions of CPC members in Shanghai and other cities that reduced party membership from approximately 57,000 to under 10,000. The party retreated to rural base areas in southern China.

Mao Zedong's strategy of peasant-based rural guerrilla warfare, developed during this period, diverged from orthodox Soviet-style urban insurrection and eventually prevailed within the party. From 1934–1935, Communist forces conducted the Long March — a 6,000-mile (9,600 km) strategic retreat from Jiangxi province to Yan'an, Shaanxi — losing roughly two-thirds of the 100,000 troops who began the march. The Long March became a founding myth of the CPC, establishing Mao's authority and cementing a core cadre who would lead China for decades.

During the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945), the CPC and KMT formed a Second United Front against Japan. The party used the wartime period to expand territory, recruit membership, and build administrative experience across the liberated zones of northern China. By 1945, CPC membership had grown to approximately 1.2 million and the Red Army to over 900,000 troops. Post-war negotiations with the KMT failed, and civil war resumed in 1946. The People's Liberation Army (PLA) defeated KMT forces decisively by 1949; Chiang Kai-shek and the ROC government retreated to Taiwan. On October 1, 1949, Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China from Tiananmen Gate.

## The Mao Era (1949–1976)

### Early Consolidation (1949–1957)

The party's first years in power involved land reform (redistributing land from landlords to approximately 300 million peasants), nationalization of industry, institution of the Soviet-style planned economy, and mass political campaigns against perceived class enemies. The PRC participated in the Korean War (1950–1953), dispatching approximately 1 million Chinese People's Volunteer Army troops in support of North Korea. The campaign to "Resist America and Aid Korea" also served as a domestic mass mobilization for the new state.

Land reform was accompanied by violent class struggle campaigns that killed an estimated 1–2 million landlords and "class enemies." The Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1957–1958, following a brief liberalization period (the Hundred Flowers Campaign, 1956–1957), purged some 550,000 intellectuals, professionals, and party members who had offered criticism.

### The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962)

Mao's Great Leap Forward was a mass campaign to rapidly industrialize China through rural collectivization and "backyard steel" production. Approximately 500 million peasants were organized into People's Communes; private plots were eliminated; grain production targets were drastically overstated by local officials afraid to report failures. The campaign produced the Great Chinese Famine of 1959–1961, with scholarly estimates of excess deaths ranging from 15 to 55 million, making it the deadliest famine in human history. Industrial production also fell sharply. The disaster temporarily diminished Mao's authority within the party; pragmatic leaders Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping managed economic recovery.

### The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

Seeking to reassert power and attack rivals he accused of "capitalist roading," Mao launched the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution in May 1966. Red Guard factions of students and youth mobilized across China, attacking "Four Olds" (old customs, culture, habits, and ideas), destroying cultural artifacts, temples, shrines, and books, and persecuting educators, intellectuals, and party officials including Deng Xiaoping (purged twice). The [Confucianism](../culture-society/confucianism.md) that had underpinned Chinese civilization for two millennia was explicitly targeted; Confucian temples were desecrated and the "Criticize Lin, Criticize Confucius" campaign (1973–74) portrayed Confucius as a feudal enemy. The Cultural Revolution caused an estimated 500,000 to 2 million deaths through violence and an unknown further number through persecution, imprisonment, and suicide. Universities were closed from 1966 to 1970, and the entire cohort of intellectuals educated during this period lost years of education. The movement's factional violence eventually forced Mao to deploy the PLA to restore order. Mao died in September 1976; the Gang of Four, including Mao's wife Jiang Qing, were arrested in October 1976, ending the Cultural Revolution phase.

## The Reform Era: Deng Xiaoping (1978–1997)

After a brief succession struggle, Deng Xiaoping consolidated power at the Third Plenum of the 11th Central Committee in December 1978, launching the "Reform and Opening Up" (*Gaige Kaifang*) policy. Deng's reforms proceeded along several axes:

- **Agricultural decollectivization**: The Household Responsibility System (1978–1984) allowed farming families to sell surplus production on the market after meeting state quotas, effectively ending commune agriculture and raising rural incomes dramatically.
- **Special Economic Zones**: Four coastal cities — Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen — were designated Special Economic Zones in 1980, offering tax incentives and relaxed regulations to attract foreign investment.
- **State enterprise reform**: Gradual introduction of market mechanisms in industry, though large state-owned enterprises retained privileged positions.
- **Ideological reconfiguration**: Deng's formula "socialism with Chinese characteristics" — also expressed as "It doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice" — deprioritized ideological purity in favor of economic performance, while the party maintained absolute political control.

Deng rejected political liberalization. When student demonstrations in Beijing's Tiananmen Square demanded press freedom and political reform in spring 1989, the party declared martial law, and PLA forces suppressed the movement on June 3–4, 1989. Casualty estimates range from several hundred to over 1,000 deaths; the exact number remains unknown as state records are classified. Deng subsequently accelerated economic reform while tightening political control — the model that has defined the CPC since.

## The Technocratic Consensus: Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao (1989–2012)

**Jiang Zemin** (General Secretary 1989–2002, President 1993–2003) oversaw China's accelerating integration into the global economy, culminating in WTO accession in December 2001. He also developed the ideological concept of the "Three Represents" (2000), which expanded the party's social base by admitting entrepreneurs and professionals — a significant departure from the party's working-class identity. The Jiang era saw GDP grow from roughly $400 billion (1992) to $1.5 trillion (2002), surging foreign direct investment, and Hong Kong's return from British rule (1997).

**Hu Jintao** (General Secretary 2002–2012, President 2003–2013) promoted the "Scientific Outlook on Development" (*Kexue Fazhan Guan*) and the "harmonious society" (*hexie shehui*) concept — emphasizing balanced, sustainable development and social stability over pure growth targets. China's GDP grew from approximately $1.5 trillion (2003) to $8.2 trillion (2012), making China the world's second-largest economy by 2010. The Beijing Olympics (2008) and Shanghai Expo (2010) were high-profile showcases of China's arrival as a global power.

## The Xi Jinping Era (2012–Present)

Xi Jinping became General Secretary of the CPC in November 2012 and President of the PRC in March 2013. His tenure has been marked by a significant centralization of power unprecedented since Mao.

**Anti-Corruption Campaign**: Beginning in 2012, Xi launched an anti-corruption campaign that by 2023 had investigated over 4 million officials, including more than 400 at the level of minister or provincial party secretary. High-profile targets included former Politburo Standing Committee member Zhou Yongkang and former PLA generals Xu Caihou and Guo Boxiong. Critics note the campaign also served to eliminate political rivals.

**Xi Jinping Thought**: The 19th Party Congress (October 2017) enshrined "Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era" in the party constitution — the first time a living leader's thought had been so named since Mao Zedong. The 2018 constitutional amendment removed presidential term limits, opening the path for Xi's indefinite rule.

**Governance of Xinjiang and Tibet**: The party has conducted extensive security campaigns in Xinjiang targeting the Uyghur Muslim minority population, including mass detention in re-education facilities estimated to have held 1–1.8 million people. UN human rights bodies have characterized these measures as potentially constituting crimes against humanity. The party frames them as counter-terrorism and vocational training.

**COVID-19 Response**: China's initial response to the COVID-19 outbreak (first reported in Wuhan in December 2019) included suppression of early warnings; subsequently, a "Zero-COVID" policy involving mass lockdowns and testing was maintained until December 2022, when it was abruptly abandoned amid nationwide protests.

**Taiwan Policy**: Xi has repeatedly asserted that "reunification" with Taiwan is a historical inevitability and has refused to renounce the use of force. Military pressure on Taiwan has intensified, with record numbers of PLA aircraft entering Taiwan's air defense identification zone.

## Party Structure and Organization

The CPC's organizational architecture, derived from Leninist principles, creates interlocking control at every administrative level:

| Body | Composition | Function |
|---|---|---|
| National Congress | ~2,300 delegates, convenes every 5 years | Formal highest authority; elects Central Committee |
| Central Committee | ~205 full members + 171 alternates | Governs between Congresses; elects Politburo |
| Politburo | 24 members | Key policy decisions |
| Politburo Standing Committee | 7 members | Apex of power; day-to-day decisions |
| General Secretary | 1 (Xi Jinping) | Chairs the PSC; leads the party |
| Central Military Commission | ~10 members | Commands the PLA |

Party committees are embedded in every state institution, university, major enterprise, and military unit. The party controls appointments across government, the judiciary, state media, and major state-owned enterprises through the Organization Department and the *nomenklatura* system — a list of approximately 500,000 positions subject to party approval.

## Ideological Lineage

| Ideology | Source | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Mao Zedong Thought | Mao Zedong | 1945–present |
| Deng Xiaoping Theory | Deng Xiaoping | 1997–present |
| Three Represents | Jiang Zemin | 2002–present |
| Scientific Outlook on Development | Hu Jintao | 2007–present |
| Xi Jinping Thought | Xi Jinping | 2017–present |

Each formulation was incorporated into the party constitution at successive Congresses, creating a layered official ideology (*guiding thought*) that successive leaders did not abolish but supplemented.

## Historical Rehabilitation of Confucianism

The party's relationship with [Confucianism](../culture-society/confucianism.md) traces a dramatic arc. The CPC initially allied with the May Fourth tradition that rejected Confucianism as a backward feudal system; the Cultural Revolution reached the apex of anti-Confucian rhetoric. From the 1980s, as Deng's economic reforms proceeded and the party sought cultural legitimacy beyond Marxist ideology, the official stance shifted. By the 2000s, Hu Jintao's "harmonious society" discourse drew explicitly on Confucian vocabulary, and the government established a global network of Confucius Institutes beginning in 2004. Xi Jinping has cited classical Confucian texts in speeches and promoted "excellent traditional Chinese culture" as a component of ideological education — integrating Confucian heritage into the party's legitimizing narrative.

## The Great Wall as National Symbol

The [Great Wall](../geography/the-great-wall.md) occupies a central role in CPC political symbolism. The national anthem of the PRC — the "March of the Volunteers" (*Yìyǒngjūn Jìnxíngqǔ*), originally written for a 1935 anti-Japanese film and adopted as the provisional national anthem in 1949 (formally enshrined in the constitution in 2004) — urges the people to "build our new Great Wall with our flesh and blood." The party has invested heavily in wall preservation and tourism infrastructure, particularly at Badaling and other sites near Beijing, positioning the Wall as evidence of Chinese civilization's historical greatness — and by extension, of the CPC as its modern custodian.

## Legacy and Contemporary Significance

The CPC has presided over both the worst man-made famines in history and one of the most rapid economic transformations in history. China's GDP grew from approximately $60 billion (1952) to over $17 trillion (2023), lifting an estimated 800 million people out of poverty by World Bank metrics. Life expectancy rose from approximately 36 years (1950) to 78 years (2023). These achievements occurred alongside documented mass political violence, suppression of civil liberties, and governance of religious and ethnic minorities under extensive coercion. The party's longevity — now spanning over 75 years in power — and its success in combining market economics with political authoritarianism constitute a distinctive political model studied and debated by scholars of comparative politics worldwide.
